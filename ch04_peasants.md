# Chapter 4: Peasants

The bamboo stops the way a sentence stops. One moment there are hundred-meter stalks on every side, and the next there is nothing but a wide ring of bare, packed earth, and beyond it, the city.

It is not large. That is the first thing I notice, and the first thing that bothers me. It sits in the middle of the endless farm like a pin in a map, maybe three kilometers across. But it goes *up*. Towers crowd against one another so tightly they seem to be holding each other upright, stone at the bottom, then brick, then wood, then something pale and glassy near the tops. Bridges run between them at a dozen different heights, some of them thick enough to carry carts, some of them barely ropes. Near the summits, lights burn in the late afternoon, steady and cold and pale. The same pale as the crystal.

Lower down, there are no lights at all.

I park Billy just inside the tree line, where the last stalks throw a shadow deep enough to hide an armored personnel carrier. It is not easy to hide an armored personnel carrier. It is a skill.

"It's so compact," Bethany says, leaning over my shoulder to look at the monitors. Her hand is wrapped in gauze from the med kit. She holds it slightly away from her body, like evidence. "All that land and they build straight up."

"Because all that land is farm." I tap the screen, where the edge of the bamboo runs around the city in a near-perfect circle. "Every square meter they build on is a square meter they aren't harvesting. This city doesn't sit on the farm. The city *serves* the farm."

"That's grim."

"That's economics. The two are frequently confused."

Three of my drones have been circling the city at altitude for six hours, too high to see, low enough to listen. Billy has been soaking up every scrap of sound they catch: market shouting, arguments, children, a man who has been singing the same four notes on the same bridge since noon. The script on the walls and signs gets photographed and fed in alongside it.

He has, in that time, learned the language.

"Why would they even speak a language we could learn?" Bethany asks. "They could be anything. We haven't seen a single person yet."

"They have mouths, they make sounds, and the sounds repeat in patterns. That's all a language model needs." I hand her an earpiece. "I wrote the translator on a train. Billy trains it on anything he hears. Six hours of an entire city talking is more than enough."

"You built a universal translator on a *train.*"

"I built a very good statistician on a train. A universal translator is just what that looks like from the outside."

She fits the earpiece in. On the monitor, a drone is watching a woman at the city's edge argue with a man pushing a handcart. Billy's voice, flat and faintly synthetic, murmurs the translation over the top of them.

*…not paying twelve motes for that. It's half rot.*

*It's Hessen rot. Hessen rot is good rot.*

"Oh," Bethany says, sounding like she has just watched me do the invisible trick again. "Oh, that's actually incredible."

"I know."

"You could have been rich. You could have just been rich and normal."

"I *am* rich. Normal is a waste of rich."

The signs, now that Billy can read them, turn out to say things like FRESH WATER, CONDENSED DAILY and NO LOITERING ON THE THIRD BRIDGE and, above one door, simply HONEST. Billy overlays the translations on the video in neat white text. The city becomes, in a few seconds, legible. It is the most satisfying thing that has happened to me since Alphonseo stopped moving.

I lean back in my seat.

"Do you think they'd notice if we drove Billy in?"

Bethany turns her head very slowly and looks at me. She does not say anything. She doesn't need to. The look does the work of several paragraphs.

I knew the answer. I just like asking.

---

We leave Billy in the shadows with four drones on overwatch and instructions to shoot anything that tries to climb him. I pack for a stroll into an unknown civilization: my pistol, the translator earpieces, a flashlight, a multitool, a notebook, a lighter, and three blocks of C4 distributed across my coat's many pockets.

"You're bringing *explosives* into a city," Bethany says.

"I'm bringing explosives *everywhere.* The city is not special."

"Can I at least have the second flamethrower?"

I consider this. I do not like to encourage her. On the other hand, she is the one who walks in front.

"No," I say. "You'll set yourself on fire. You have a history."

"*You* set me on fire."

"Which is how I know it's possible."

The ring of bare earth takes ten minutes to cross. The road leading into the city is packed dirt, rutted by cartwheels, and it ends at a gate in a low wall. The wall is mostly for show. You could climb it with a ladder and a mild sense of ambition. But there is a guard.

He is the first person of this world we have met up close, and I study him with the thoroughness he deserves. Human, or near enough that I can't tell the difference without a scalpel. Tall, weathered, bored. A long spear leans against the wall beside him, and in its tip, where a metal point would go, is set a small glowing crystal, the size of a thumbnail. Around his neck hangs a pouch that glows faintly through the cloth.

He looks at my coat, then at Bethany's lab coat, then at her bandaged hand, and his expression says that he has seen stranger, but not by much.

He says something. Billy murmurs in my ear.

*Entry's two motes. Each.*

"Motes," I repeat, trying the word in his language. My accent must be terrible, because he winces. Billy supplies the rest: a mote is money. From the context of six hours of haggling, it is *the* money.

I reach into my coat and bring out the crystal from yesterday. It is now a cloudy lump, dead as a pebble.

The guard looks at it. Then he laughs, a short honest bark.

*A husk? You're trying to pay me with a husk?* He nudges his friend on the other side of the gate, who hadn't been paying attention and now is. *She wants to pay with a husk.*

I put the crystal away with dignity. There is no other way to do anything.

"We don't have money," Bethany whispers. "We are going to be turned away from the magic city because we're *broke.*"

"Nobody is broke," I say, "who has something another person wants."

I take out the flashlight.

It is a very ordinary flashlight. Aluminum body, lithium cell, a white LED that I bought from a hardware store and did not even bother to modify. I click it on and point it at the guard's feet.

The laughter stops.

He stares at the circle of white light on the dirt. Then he stares at the flashlight. Then he looks, very carefully, for the crystal inside it, and does not find one, and I watch a small, private crisis play out across his face.

*Where's the mote?* he says. *Where's it drinking from?*

"It's a secret," I say, and Billy translates it back into his language in my voice.

I click it off. I click it on. His eyes follow the light like a cat's.

He waves us through without looking up from it.

*Welcome to Pinnacle,* he says, to the flashlight.

Pinnacle. A city three kilometers wide, sitting at the bottom of a farm, named after its top. I approve. You should always name a thing after its best part.

Five minutes later, we walk through the gate, and the guard is standing behind us, turning the flashlight over and over in his hands, clicking it on and off, holding it up against the sky to see if it runs out. It won't. Not for a few days, anyway. By then we'll be long gone, or in charge.

"You gave away a flashlight," Bethany says.

"I *sold* a flashlight," I say. "And learned something worth considerably more than two motes."

"Which is?"

"Everything here runs on crystals, and apparently nothing runs without them." I look up at the towers, at the cold lights burning at the top and the dark at the bottom. "And I have a vehicle full of things that don't need them."

---

Inside the wall, the city swallows the sky.

The streets at ground level are narrow enough that I could touch both sides if I held my arms out, which I do, once, to confirm it. The towers lean in overhead, and the bridges crisscross between them, and the result is a sort of stone canyon lit mainly by what daylight manages to fall down the gaps. It smells like cooking oil and people and damp. Laundry hangs from lines strung between windows. Everywhere, everyone is carrying something.

Nobody here has a lamp.

I notice that first and keep noticing it. Doorways are dark. A cobbler works by the gray light at the mouth of an alley, squinting. Three stories up, a woman leans out a window to read a letter, because the window is the only bright spot in her home. Meanwhile, high above, where the towers turn pale and glassy, the cold crystal light is already burning in daylight, spilling down from the upper bridges like something expensive being left running.

"Light costs money," I say.

Bethany follows my gaze upward. "So the rich live at the top."

"The rich live *in the light.* The top is a coincidence of engineering."

I take out my notebook. I have come here to learn, and magic is a thing people do, and people are all around me, doing things. I do not need a teacher. I need a good seat.

We find one on the steps of a shuttered shop, and I watch.

---

A water-seller has a stall on the corner under the sign that says FRESH WATER, CONDENSED DAILY. There is no pipe, no well, no barrel. There is a clay jar, and a very old man with a mote pressed between his thumb and the jar's rim. He doesn't close his eyes. He doesn't concentrate the way I did in the forest, straining like I was lifting a car. He just holds it there and hums, low and bored, and after half a minute the jar beads with water on the inside and begins, slowly, to fill.

The mote in his fingers dims. Very slightly.

"He's touching it," I murmur.

"What?"

"The jar. He's touching the jar, and he's touching the mote to the jar. He isn't projecting anything. He's making the smallest possible effect at the shortest possible distance." I write it down. *Contact is cheap. Distance is expensive.* "I made a fireball in your *hand* from two meters away. I've been casting like an idiot."

"You've been casting *on* an idiot," Bethany says, and holds up her gauze. "Specifically me."

I am too pleased to argue. Things I got wrong are things I can now get right. That's the whole point of wrong.

Farther down the street, two porters are moving a crate up the outside of a tower. There is no winch. One of them rides it, standing on the lid with a hand flat against the wood, and the crate rises in short, bobbing lurches, a meter at a time, while the second one shouts directions from below. At every landing the rider stops, swaps a spent mote for a fresh one from a belt pouch, and flicks the old one down to his partner. The partner catches it and drops it in a bucket already half full of cloudy husks.

Force, applied through touch, in small increments, with rests. An elevator made of a man.

I write that down too.

A child runs past us chasing a ball of light the size of a marble, which bobs a few centimeters ahead of her fingers and never quite lets her catch it. Her mother, carrying a basket of bamboo shoots, snaps something and the light goes out. Billy translates it as *that's not a toy, that's dinner.*

"Dinner?" Bethany says.

"Money," I say. "She means money."

And there it is. I sit back on the step. Every mote is a coin. Every coin is a battery. Every spell is a purchase. The water-seller isn't making water; he's spending a little money to turn it into slightly more money. The porter's bucket of husks is a bucket of receipts. The light at the top of the towers isn't just light. It's wealth, burned in public so everyone can see how much of it there is to burn.

This entire civilization uses cash as fuel.

"My first question," I say slowly, "was how to make money with magic. The answer is that magic *is* money. They've gone and made the question stupid." I grin at the street. "Which means the answer is simple."

"Oh no."

"Anything that makes light, heat, water or motion without a crystal is money that never runs out. And I have a one-megawatt reactor in a car outside."

"You want to open a *shop.*"

"I want to open an *economy.*"

---

We move deeper. I keep the notebook out.

The second thing I learn about this world is that there are people who collect the money. We see one on the next street: a man in pale gray with a bamboo-leaf crest stitched on his shoulder, walking stall to stall with a crystal the size of a grapefruit hanging from a chain around his neck. At each stall, the owner presses a few motes against the big crystal, and they dull, and the big crystal brightens a tiny bit. Nobody argues. Nobody even looks at him directly.

Billy catches fragments of what people mutter after he passes. *House tithe. Third this month. Up on the Leaf bridges they don't even pay for the lamps.*

"Tax collector," Bethany says.

"Harvest collector," I correct her. "Somebody owns that farm. Somebody owns the bamboo, which means somebody owns the crystals, which means somebody owns the money itself." I watch the man in gray go up a spiral stair toward the brighter levels, his crystal swinging. "These Houses don't tax the economy. They *print* it."

The third thing I learn, I learn from arithmetic.

I have no exchange rate. That offends me more than anything else in this city. I know what a mote *does* but not what it's *worth*, and a number without a unit is just a rumor. So while Bethany sits on a crate and nurses her hand, I price a day.

A bowl of bamboo-shoot porridge from a cart: one mote. A flat loaf the size of a dinner plate: one mote. A skewer of something fried, which Billy identifies as bird and which I choose to believe him about: one mote. A laborer eats three times a day, and nobody on this street eats better than a laborer.

Three motes per day.

I know exactly what it costs to feed a person on Earth for a day. I once had to defend a per diem to a military auditor, and I remember every number anyone has ever made me defend. Euros per day, divided by motes per day. The days cancel. What's left is euros per mote.

Dimensional analysis. The only magic I have ever really needed.

With a rate, the whole city prices itself. The gate toll, two motes each, is two-thirds of a day's food; they charge you a lunch and a half to walk in. The half-rotten cart from this morning, twelve motes, is four days of eating. The collector's grapefruit-sized crystal is a number I don't have yet, but I intend to.

Then I price the butcher, and the numbers stop working.

His stall sits where two alleys meet: hooks, a heavy wooden block scored by years of cuts, and a crate of live hens behind it. A farmer's cart sits at the corner. I watch the butcher buy three birds from it. Four motes each.

Twenty minutes later I watch him sell one of those same hens, plucked, cleaned and wrapped in a leaf, for four motes.

Four in, four out. Plus his labor, his knife, his rent, his stall. On paper he is running a business at exactly zero margin, and yet the crystal hanging over his chopping block is the brightest thing on the street. Brighter than the water-seller's. Brighter than anything in anyone's pouch. It hangs over the block like a small, well-fed moon.

Books balance. Energy balances. When they don't, you haven't found a miracle. You've missed a term.

"Something is wrong with the butcher," I say.

Bethany squints at him. "He looks fine."

"His margin is zero and he has the most money on the street. Either he's laundering it, or the Houses subsidize meat, or he has an income stream I can't see."

The butcher lifts another hen from the crate and puts it on the block. The cleaver comes down. Above it, the crystal seems to flicker, but it might just be the blade catching the light.

I write it down and underline it twice. *Butcher: missing term.* Then I close the notebook, because there is a great deal left to see, and an equation will always wait for you.

---

By the time the upper lights have finished warming the evening, my feet hurt, and I have eleven pages of notes, and Bethany has stopped looking up.

We stand at the bottom of a wide shaft between four towers, where the bridges stack overhead in layers, and far above us, on the brightest of them, a group of people is crossing. Even from here I can see that their clothes are pale and clean. One of them is being carried in a chair. Another tosses something over the rail. It falls glittering through the dark and lands a few meters from us: a husk, empty and cloudy. Down here, three children race for it anyway.

I look at the children. I look at the bridge.

"Peasants," I say.

I don't say it cruelly. I say it the way I would say *granite* or *sedimentary*. A classification.

Bethany turns to me with an expression I am coming to recognize: a tired person working very hard not to be surprised.

"Angline," she says. "We are standing at the very bottom. We have no money. You paid our entrance fee with a *flashlight.* We are the peasants."

"Temporarily."

"They're up there, and we're down here."

"Right now." I pocket the notebook. "The best should rule, Bethany. I said so in my slides. I simply haven't climbed the stairs yet."

She opens her mouth to argue. She doesn't get the chance.

"You."

The voice comes from behind us, and Billy translates it a half second late, so I hear the tone before the meaning. It is the tone of someone accustomed to people turning around.

I turn around.

It's a man in pale gray, the bamboo leaf on his shoulder, a big crystal glowing on his chest. Two guards flank him with crystal-tipped spears. In his gloved hand, held carefully, the way a person holds something they don't understand and are afraid to drop, is my flashlight.

He clicks it on. The white beam lands on my coat.

*Which one of you,* he says, *made a light that doesn't drink?*

Bethany looks at me. I can see her doing the arithmetic again. I can see it still not working out in her favor.

I smile the way I smile at a projector that has finished warming up.

"Allow me," I say, "to monologue."

# Chapter 12: The Optimal Path

The problem with opening a door from both sides is that you can't be on both sides.

For the Sable Gate, Bethany fired the flywheels from Earth while I pulled from the Green, and we talked by radio through a hole the size of a dinner plate. For the salt mine, there's no one to leave behind. Bethany is coming with me. Brask insists on coming, and I don't have the heart to say no, which is a phrase I've never used before. Everyone else I trust is on the wrong side of the universe or doesn't exist.

So I do what engineers have done since the first railway timetable. I synchronize the clocks.

In the salt mine, two hundred meters under the hills, the second flywheel bank sits in a gallery carved out of rock salt, humming, charged, wired to a resonator and a timer. The timer has one instruction. On the fifth day, at noon exactly, fire the full sequence.

I set my watch against it, to the hundredth of a second. Then I drive back to Fernholt, through the Sable Gate, and point Billy east.

---

Four hundred kilometers is four days, at the speed a convoy can manage on harvest roads. Billy leads, pulling a low trailer with the feeding ring folded on it like a steel flower. Behind him come two trucks from Earth, carrying a camp, a radio mast, crates of lamps and filters and tools, and three cores packed in foam and steel cradles, thirty thousand motes. Brask's best crew of eight rides in the second truck, looking out at the fields with the frank curiosity of people who have never been more than a day's walk from Pinnacle.

Neither have I, I realize, in the sense that matters. I've flown over the Green with drones. I've read it on LiDAR maps. I've never *driven* through it.

It's not what I thought.

From the air, the fields look like bamboo and nothing else: endless green columns in perfect rows. From the road, the bamboo is only the roof. Beneath the canopy, in the green-gold shade, there's a whole second world. Low crops I don't recognize grow in terraced beds between the stalks, broad-leaved and dark, the kind of plants that thrive in shade. Grazers move slowly through the rows, heavy-bodied, placid creatures somewhere between a deer and a goat, with long mobile lips for stripping leaves. Children with switches walk behind them. In the hollows where stalks have been cut and left, fungus grows in pale shelves as wide as a table.

Everything is eating everything. The grazers eat the understory. The fungus eats the fallen stalks. The bamboo eats the light. At the center of each field section, on a low stone plinth, sits a mother crystal, glowing faintly, surrounded by a ring of harvest stones worn smooth by centuries.

"It's not a farm," Bethany says, watching it pass from the passenger seat. "It's an ecosystem."

"It's a *machine,*" I say. "Tuned over five hundred years by people who couldn't write equations. Look at the layers. Canopy for the fastest growth. Shade crops underneath to use what light gets through. Grazers to turn plants into flesh. Fungus to turn waste into something harvestable. Every death, timed and caught." I watch a harvest crew lead a grazer toward a mother crystal, calmly, the way a farmer on Earth might lead a cow to milking. "They've built my vertical farm. They just built it sideways, and slowly, and in the rain."

"Does that bother you?"

"It *impresses* me. There's a difference." I think about it. "A small difference."

---

On the third day, the fields end.

They don't fade. They stop, at a line of ward-posts, each a pillar of pale stone carved deep with a rune and capped with a crystal the size of a fist. Beyond the posts, the bamboo grows wild: tangled, broken, hung with vines, never cut, never tended, the undergrowth thick and dark. The harvest road narrows to a track.

Brask's crew goes quiet.

"What's out there?" Bethany asks.

Brask, riding with us, looks at the wild bamboo for a long moment. *Things that eat,* he says. *Things that learned to use motes before we did. The wards keep them out of the fields. Out here—* He shrugs. *Out here, they're home.*

That night, we camp in a clearing with Billy's floodlights on and the drones overhead, and at two in the morning, Billy wakes me with a soft tone.

The thermal camera shows nothing. The night-vision camera shows nothing. The drones, hovering above the clearing, see only bamboo and the dark.

But Billy's radar sees something. A shape about the size of a horse, low to the ground, sixty meters from the trucks, moving slowly around the edge of the light. Circling.

It's invisible to light. It's cold enough to be invisible to heat. It's bending everything I can see with, except the one thing nobody in this world has ever had reason to hide from: a pulse of radio.

I watch it on the radar screen for twenty minutes. It circles twice. Then it moves away into the wild, and doesn't come back.

In the morning, Brask's crew finds prints at the edge of the clearing, deep and clawed, in a perfect ring around our camp. Brask looks at them for a long time.

*You saw it,* he says. *How?*

"Radio," I say. "It's hiding from light. It doesn't know there's anything else to hide from."

He gives me a long, strange look, and I realize I've just told a man from a world full of invisible warriors that I can see them. I file the look away, carefully, next to the Lord of Sable's.

---

On the fifth morning, we reach the cliffs.

The wild bamboo thins and ends, and the land simply drops away, a hundred meters straight down to a shingle beach, and beyond it, the Brine. Gray-green and restless, running out to a horizon with nothing on it. The wind comes off it smelling of salt and kelp and cold, and it hits my face like a slap after a year of warm green stillness.

Ruler's Reach lies below us to the south: the deep bay, the narrow mouth, the cliffs on either side like the arms of a harbor that someone started building and never finished. I've looked at it on drone footage for months.

It's much better in person.

"Oh," says Bethany, beside me.

I don't say anything. I don't need to. I just look at it, and let myself enjoy it, for almost a full minute, which is a long time for me.

Then I check my watch. It's 10:14.

We set up the ring on the cliff top, over the exact coordinates where the salt mine sits two hundred meters under the hills of Earth. Twelve ferrofluid rigs, their feeding runes angled inward. Three cores, unpacked from their cradles, glowing deep blue in the gray light. Thirty thousand motes: three times what opened the Sable Gate.

At 11:59:50, I raise my hand. Brask's crew stands back from the ring. Bethany counts the seconds from her watch.

At noon exactly, four hundred kilometers west and one universe over, twelve flywheels scream in the dark. On the cliff above the sea, a point of light blooms out of the air, and the feeding runes rise, and the cores begin to dim, and the door opens.

It grows faster than the Sable Gate. It grows bigger. Four meters, five, six. It settles at six point one, humming steadily, with the wind blowing through it from the sea into a salt-white tunnel on the other side, lit by floodlights, with rails in the floor I had laid last month.

"Six meters," Bethany says.

"Three times the motes, one and a half times the diameter," I say. "The curve holds." I'm already writing it down. "A locomotive will fit. A container on a flatcar will fit. A great many things will fit."

Behind me, one of Brask's crew makes a small sound and points down the cliff.

On the shingle beach below, at the edge of the bay, beside a row of boats pulled up above the tide line, there's a village. Thirty or forty low huts of driftwood and stone, smoke seeping from their roofs, nets drying on poles.

And on the beach in front of it, looking up at the cliff, at the humming ring of light where a moment ago there was only air, stand what looks like every single person who lives there.

---

There's a path down the cliff, narrow and switchbacked, worn into the rock by generations of feet. I take it with Bethany and two of Brask's crew, and leave the rest at the top with Billy and the door. I don't bring the glove. I do bring the pistol, because I'm not an idiot.

The villagers watch us come down the whole way. Nobody runs. Nobody comes to meet us either. By the time we reach the shingle, they've arranged themselves into a loose, wary crescent in front of the huts: forty or fifty adults, a lot of children held back by the shoulders, a few old people sitting on upturned boats. Some of them hold gaff hooks. None of them hold motes. I notice that immediately.

A woman steps out of the crescent. She's perhaps sixty, short and broad, with a face like weathered teak and two fingers missing from her left hand. She looks at my coat, at Bethany's lab coat, at the glowing ring on the cliff, and back at me, and I see exactly one question in her face.

*Which House?*

Billy's translator struggles with it for a second. Her accent is thick, the vowels flattened and salted, a coast dialect six hundred years apart from Pinnacle's. Then it catches up.

"None," I say. "I'm not from a House."

*Everyone's from a House.*

"Not me."

She looks up at the door again. The wind is blowing through it into a salt mine on another world, and at the top of the cliff, Billy sits beside it like a grey-green boulder.

*Then what are you?*

It's a better question than she knows. I give her the only answer I'm sure of.

"My name is Angline Ruler. I'm going to build something in that bay. I'd like to know who I'm going to be building it next to."

She considers that for a while. The crescent waits behind her.

*Marit,* she says finally, and jerks her head at the huts. *This is Shingle. You'd better come and sit down before the children lose their minds.*

---

I spend the rest of the day in Shingle, and I do what I always do in a new system. I watch, I count, and I write things down.

There are three hundred and twelve people. Forty-one huts. Eleven boats, pulled up above the tide line, beautifully made, planked with something dense and dark that I later learn is driftwood from a tree that grows only on the far coast. Every family fishes. The women mend nets and gut the catch; the men and the older boys take the boats out past the bay mouth at dawn. Everyone salts. The whole beach smells of it.

The huts are driftwood and stone, low and snug against the wind, and every one of them has a fire in the middle of the floor and a hole in the roof above it. The hole doesn't work. It's too small, and the wind off the sea pushes the smoke back down, and the inside of every hut I'm invited into is a gray haze that stings my eyes in seconds. The walls are black with soot. The children cough. Not dramatically, not like a sick child in a film. Constantly, and quietly, the way you'd breathe.

The water comes from a stream at the north end of the beach, the same stream where the goats drink and just downstream of where the village empties its buckets. Marit tells me, when I ask, that people get the sickness in the belly in summer, and that some of them, usually the old and the very young, die of it.

*It's the summer,* she says, shrugging, as though describing weather.

It isn't the summer. It's cholera, or something that behaves exactly like it. It's a water problem, and I know precisely what it costs to fix, because I've read the reports.

I write it down.

---

Motes, in Shingle, come from a rock.

Marit shows me at low tide. Out on the point, at the end of a spit of black stone, there's a sea stack the size of a house, crusted with barnacles and weed, and embedded in its face, just above the high-water mark, is a crystal. A wild one. Big as a barrel, cloudy and irregular, nothing like the neat mother crystals of the Green. The sea has been killing things against that rock for longer than anyone remembers: fish, crabs, seals, birds. The crystal drank every death.

The village guts its catch on the flat rocks beneath it. Every fish that dies there feeds it a little. Once a month, Marit climbs up with a chisel and knocks off a few chips.

*Forty motes, a good month,* she says. *Thirty, bad.*

"For three hundred people?"

*For three hundred people.*

Forty motes a month. In Pinnacle, a family of four spends eighty-four a week on food. Shingle doesn't buy food with motes; it catches it. The motes go on the few things the sea can't provide: grain from inland, cloth, iron fishhooks, and, once a year, a single lamp-mote for the midwinter feast, lit for twenty minutes, while the whole village sits in its light.

"No House has ever tried to take this?" Bethany asks.

Marit laughs, a short hard bark. *My grandmother's time, Sable sent a collector. He wanted a tithe of the rock. He took a boat out to look at the bay mouth.* She shrugs. *The Glass took him.*

"The Glass?"

Marit's face changes. She looks out past the point, at the gray water beyond the bay.

*You can't see it,* she says. *That's why it's called the Glass. It comes up under the boats. It's the size of three boats. You don't see it until the water bends.* She holds up her left hand with its two missing fingers. *We lost three boats this year. Eleven people.*

I look out at the water. At the bay mouth, a long ripple crosses the surface against the direction of the wind, bending the light as it goes, and then flattens and is gone.

The Houses left this coast alone for six hundred years. Not because it wasn't worth taking. Because the sea protected it.

---

It's dusk when I'm ready to leave. Bethany has spent the whole afternoon sitting in doorways, talking to mothers about coughs and bellies, and she's gone quiet in the way she gets quiet when she's angry about something she can't fix.

A child follows us to the foot of the cliff path. A girl, about eight, with a gap where her front teeth should be and a cough she keeps swallowing. She's been following me since noon, staring at my coat and all its pockets, with the frank acquisitive stare of someone trying to work out what's in them.

She stops at the bottom of the path and says something. Billy translates it a second late.

*Are you going to fix things?*

I look at her. I think about the huts, and the stream, and the Glass, and my notebook, which is now eleven pages longer than it was this morning.

"I don't know yet," I tell her, honestly. "I need to do the math."

---

That night, on the cliff top, with the door humming beside the camp and the wind coming off the sea, I do the math.

I do it properly. A needs assessment, the kind aid agencies pay consultants a fortune for and then ignore. Bethany sits across the camp table from me with her tea, watching me write, and doesn't say anything, which is how I know she's waiting for the answer to matter.

| Problem | Fix | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dirty water, summer deaths | Intake upstream of the goats, a buried pipe, a slow sand filter, a chlorine doser | Two days of Brask's crew, Earth parts, ~300 motes |
| Smoke in every hut | Steel flues from the Red Ridge, a small push-rune draft fan in each | ~40 motes a week for all 41 huts |
| Dark | Lamps and a swap station | My own product. Marginal cost: nothing |
| Belly sickness now | Oral rehydration salts and antibiotics from Earth | A few hundred euros, through Fernholt |
| Slow, vulnerable boats | Rune motors with small cores | ~2,000 motes a year |
| The Glass | A ward at the bay mouth, and radar on every boat | ~1,500 motes to build, a little to run |

I add it up.

About four thousand motes to set up. Maybe three thousand a year to run.

The farms print a hundred and twenty-six thousand motes a day. Five thousand an hour. Everything Shingle needs, everything that would stop its children coughing and its old people dying in the summer and its boats disappearing into the Glass, costs less than one hour of printing. Less than a hundredth of a percent of what I'll make this year.

I look at the number for a while.

"So you'll do it," Bethany says.

"I haven't finished."

"Angline, it's *an hour.*"

"Cost is only half an analysis. You need the other column." I turn the page. "What do I get?"

She sighs, the sigh of a woman who has been through this before, and settles back to listen.

---

I write the other column.

*Knowledge.* Three hundred and twelve people who know this coast better than any drone ever will. Currents, tides, where the kelp grows thickest, where the fish run in which season. Where the Glass hunts and where it doesn't. Every kelp farm I build in this bay depends on things these people know and I don't. I could spend five years learning them. Or I could spend an hour.

*Labor.* The kelp farms, the harbor, the sea-floor base: all of it needs hands that know boats. The rune lifters don't swim. I need a workforce here, on a coast four hundred kilometers from the nearest city. And there's one already standing on the beach.

*The coast.* There are other villages. Marit said so; the drones have seen them. Thirty or so between the cliffs and the southern headlands. Nine or ten thousand people, all of them living exactly like Shingle, all of them belonging to no one. Word travels along a coast faster than anywhere else. If Shingle has clean water and light and boats that come home, every village from here to the headlands will know within a season. They'll *come*.

*Legitimacy.* This one I underline twice. The Houses' power doesn't come from motes alone. It comes from land, and land is only worth anything with loyal hands on it. I learned that from Brask's history lessons. A captured field without loyal farmers goes wild. An empty coast is a claim that any House can dispute. A coast full of people who would rather die than go back to how things were is not a claim.

It's a country.

I put the pencil down.

"Four thousand motes," I say, "for a workforce, a knowledge base, a recruitment network, and a population that makes my claim to this coast impossible to dispute. It's the best return on investment I've seen since I sold a flashlight for sixty motes." I close the notebook. "It's not charity, Bethany. It's the optimal path."

"You could just say you want to help them."

"I don't want to help them. I want what helping them *buys.* Dying people can't work. Children who cough their way through childhood grow up to be adults who get sick at sea. Frightened people hide from you. Grateful people defend your coastline for free." I shrug. "If it happens to be kind, that's a coincidence of the numbers."

"And if the numbers came out the other way?"

I look at her.

I think about it. I think about it honestly, because she asked honestly. A world where Shingle costs a great deal and returns nothing. A world where the optimal path runs past that beach and doesn't stop.

"They didn't," I say.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one that matters. The numbers came out the way they came out." I pick up my tea. "Hypotheticals are for philosophers. I'm an engineer."

She looks at me across the table for a long moment, with that expression I still can't quite classify.

"Great plan, Ms. Ruler," she says.

"Don't make it weird."

---

There is one more thing, which I don't put in either column.

In the morning, before the crew wakes, I climb down the cliff path alone. The girl with the missing teeth is sitting on an upturned boat at the bottom, as if she's been waiting all night, which she may have.

I take the flashlight out of my coat. Aluminum body, LED, rechargeable. The same model I sold to a gate guard on my first day in Pinnacle for the price of an entry toll.

I click it on. Her eyes go wide, the same way his did.

"This is a sample," I tell her, and hand it over. "Product testing. You're my focus group. Report any faults."

She doesn't understand a word Billy translates. She doesn't need to. She clicks it on and off, on and off, holding it up to the gray sky to see if it runs out.

It won't. Not for a while.

I climb back up the cliff. Bethany is awake when I get to the top, sitting outside the tent with her tea, and she's watched the whole thing through the binoculars.

"Product testing," she says.

"Market research."

"She's *eight.*"

"Early adopters," I say, and go to look at the door, where she can't see my face.

---

I don't give Shingle anything. That's important.

Charity is a gift, and gifts depend on the whims of the giver, and everyone who's ever received one knows it. A gift makes you grateful, and then anxious, and then resentful. I don't want Shingle grateful. I want it *invested.*

So I make Marit an offer, sitting on an upturned boat on the shingle with the whole village listening. I'll build the water, the flues, the lamps, the boat motors, and the ward. In exchange, Shingle works for me. Kelp lines in the bay, starting this month. Pilots for my boats. Guides along the coast. Paid in motes, every week, at Pinnacle rates.

Marit does the arithmetic out loud, which I appreciate. Pinnacle rates are more money in a week than the rock gives the village in a year.

*And if we say no?*

"Then I build in the bay anyway, and I pay people from somewhere else to work there, and you watch them walk past with their wages." I shrug. "But the water gets fixed either way. I'm not having my workers drink from a goat stream."

She looks at me for a long time with those sea-colored eyes.

*That's not how lords bargain,* she says.

"I'm not a lord."

*Then what are you?*

I think about it. "Management," I say.

---

The water goes in first, because it's the one that kills people.

Brask's crew takes three days: an intake box at a spring well above the goat pasture, a buried pipe of Red Ridge steel running down to a slow sand filter built from beach gravel, and a little rune-driven doser that adds exactly the right amount of chlorine to a storage tank. The first time clean water runs from the tap at the head of the beach, most of the village gathers to watch it. Nobody says anything. They just watch water, which they've had all their lives, as if it were something new.

Which it is. It just doesn't look different. That's the problem with clean water; it's invisible. Nobody will notice it's working until the end of the summer, when nobody dies.

The flues go in next: steel pipes up through every roof, tall enough to catch the wind, each one with a small push-rune fan at its base drawing the smoke up and out. The first night, I walk through Shingle and put my head into hut after hut, and the air inside is clear. The walls are still black. The children still cough, but less. In a month, the soot will start to fade, and in a year, a doctor would be able to hear the difference in their lungs.

The lamps go in last, and they're the ones everyone remembers.

I install a swap station at the top of the cliff path, powered by a line run from the door, and on the first evening I hand out forty-one lamps, one for every hut. At dusk, one by one, the windows of Shingle light up, warm and white, and stay lit.

Marit comes and stands beside me on the beach, looking at her village.

*Midwinter,* she says quietly. *We light one lamp-mote at midwinter. For twenty minutes. Everyone comes and sits in it.* She's silent for a while. *It's midwinter every night now.*

"It's a subscription service," I say. "One mote a week. Deducted from wages."

She laughs, properly, for the first time since I've known her.

---

The Glass comes on the ninth day.

By then, four of Shingle's boats have rune motors, and all of them carry a small radar set wired to a screen in the stern, which the fishermen regard with deep suspicion and consult constantly. The ward at the bay mouth is half built: a ferrofluid grid the size of a barn door, sealed in a steel housing on a concrete footing on the rocks at the narrows, with a core behind it and a controller in a waterproof box.

I'm on the cliff when Billy's radar sounds, and a second later, faintly, so does every boat's.

Out beyond the bay mouth, where Marit's grandmother's collector went looking for a tithe, there's something in the water. On radar, it's a long, dense shape, thirty meters from end to end, moving just below the surface toward the fishing boats. To the eye, there's nothing. The water is gray and restless and empty. Then, very slightly, it bends.

The boats turn. With oars, they'd never make it. With motors, they come around in a spray of white water and run for the bay, and the shape follows, fast, faster than any fish has a right to be.

They pass the narrows. The shape follows them in.

I key the controller. The ferrofluid in the half-built ward rises in a single long pattern, twelve push bars fanned across the channel, and the core behind it begins to drink.

The water at the bay mouth heaves.

It's like watching a wall of air hit the sea. A long ridge of water rears up across the narrows, and on the radar screen, the shape stops dead as if it's hit glass. For a moment, it's visible. The bending fails as it strains against the push, and I see it: a long pale body like a ribbon of wet marble, fins like sails, and a head that's mostly mouth, twisting in the water at the edge of the ward.

Then it turns, and dives, and goes back out to sea, and the ward sighs down into a flat gray puddle of oil.

On the beach below, the boats run up onto the shingle, and the fishermen jump out, and nobody says anything for quite a while.

---

That evening, Bethany finds me on the cliff top with the radar recording on a loop, measuring the Glass from every frame.

"You could have killed it," she says. "That ward could have crushed it."

"I could have."

"Something that size. Something that *alive.*" She looks at the screen. "How many motes would that be?"

I've already done it. "Thirty meters. Perhaps twenty tonnes of animal. Eight hundred-odd motes per tonne of living tissue." I shrug. "Fifteen, twenty thousand. A very good afternoon."

"So why didn't you?"

"Because a dead Glass is a good afternoon, and a live one is a navy." I point out past the narrows at the open sea, at the long coast running south, at the empty horizon. "For six hundred years, the thing that's kept every House off this coast has been that animal. It doesn't take bribes. It doesn't negotiate. It doesn't need to be paid. And now I'm the only person in the Green who can see it coming and the only person who can keep it out of a harbor." I switch off the recording. "Why would I kill the only guard in the world who works for free?"

Bethany stares at me.

"You're keeping a *sea monster,*" she says slowly, "as *coastal defense.*"

"I'm *preserving an ecosystem,*" I say. "It's very modern of me."

---

By the end of the month, the people of Shingle have started calling me something. I hear it on the beach, in the boats, in the doorways of the huts.

It takes Billy a few days to work out that it isn't a translation error. It's my name, used as a title, the way you'd say *the Lady* or *the Lord.*

*The Ruler.*

Bethany thinks it's hilarious. I think it's accurate. We're both right.

---

The kelp lines go into the bay in the second month: long ropes anchored to the bottom and buoyed at the surface, seeded with spores the Shingle women scrape from the wild kelp beds past the point. In six weeks, the first lines are heavy with fronds three meters long. Kelp grows half a meter a day in good water. The bay is very good water.

It isn't only a crop. Kelp is full of sugars, and yeast eats sugar, and the yeast floors in every farm I own have been starving for feed since the spreadsheet turned red. The bay can grow the yeast's dinner faster than any field in the Green.

Which raises the question that has been waiting for me since the first night on the cliff.

The farms are four hundred kilometers west.

---

I lay it out in the camp, on the back of the cartographer's map, in the only language that makes logistics honest.

Tonnes. Kilometers. Energy per tonne-kilometer.

Kelp for the farms: thousands of tonnes a week, once the bay is fully planted. Steel from the Red Ridge, heading the other way, for the harbor and the sea-floor base. Cores and motes, in both directions. Copper, lithium, lamps, people. Eventually, everything, all the time.

There are three ways to move that much across four hundred kilometers.

Trucks are the worst. Every tonne on a road costs about ten times the energy of the same tonne on water, and I would need thousands of them, and a road that doesn't turn to mud, and drivers, and I would spend the rest of my life fixing tires.

Barges are the best. Nothing moves heavy things more cheaply than a flat boat on still water. One barge carries what forty trucks carry, for a fraction of the fuel. If there were a river from the Reach to Pinnacle, I'd already be building a harbor on it.

There isn't. So I'd have to dig one.

I do the math on a canal anyway, because I can't stop myself. Four hundred kilometers. Thirty meters wide, four deep. Forty-eight million cubic meters of earth, roughly ninety million tonnes. Plus locks, because the land isn't flat, and reservoirs to feed the locks, and a way through the root mats of five hundred years of House fields, which, it turns out, are nearly as hard as concrete.

With every rune lifter I own, working day and night, it would take about eleven years.

"Eleven years," Bethany says, reading over my shoulder.

"Eleven years for now. The lifters will get better. The swarms will get bigger. Force runes that can move earth in bulk are on the research list." I draw a long dashed line across the map, from the bay to Pinnacle, and write CANAL beside it, very small, in pencil. "One day. Not this year."

"So what this year?"

I draw a solid line, right beside the dashed one.

"Rail."

---

A train isn't as cheap as a barge, but it's much closer to it than a truck. Steel wheels on steel rails roll with almost no friction; a single locomotive can pull three thousand tonnes on the flat. It doesn't need water. It doesn't need locks. It can go anywhere I can lay two strips of steel. And I happen to own a steel mill.

I size it. A single track, with passing loops every twenty kilometers, double later. Rails at sixty kilograms a meter, two of them, four hundred kilometers: forty-eight thousand tonnes of steel. That's about fifty days of the Red Ridge's output, or thirteen farms' worth of frames.

I think about that trade for a long time. Thirteen farms now, or a railway that makes every future farm cheaper to feed, supply, and build.

I choose the railway. Farms make motes. Railways make *empires.*

Power comes next. I could run the trains on motes, with rune motors and a core in every locomotive, but I run the numbers and wince. A heavy freight train across four hundred kilometers would burn two whole days of the farms' output. Motes are money; burning money to haul kelp is the kind of thing Houses do. Fusion is much cheaper. So the line will be electrified: wires overhead, fed from *Eleven Months* and her sisters, and the same poles will carry power to the coast. Rail and grid, sharing one corridor. Build one, get the other nearly free.

Until the wires are up, the first locomotive comes through the Reach door from Earth: a secondhand diesel-electric freight engine, bought by a boring company from a railway in the far north that was glad to see it go. It squeezes through the six-meter door with half a meter to spare.

Bethany watches it roll out onto the cliff top, huge and orange and filthy, belching smoke into the clean sea air.

"I thought you hated combustion," she says.

"The diesel spins a generator. The generator powers electric motors." I pat its flank with real affection. "It's spinning magnets, Bethany. It's just spinning magnets with extra steps."

---

The hardest part of a railway is never the steel. It's the ground it crosses.

Four hundred kilometers from the Reach to Pinnacle, and most of them run through House fields. Every kilometer will need a charter, a right of way, a tithe, and a set of stairs. I'll have to climb a great many of them, and I'll do it, and I'll enjoy most of it.

But not the first sixty kilometers.

The first sixty kilometers inland from the cliffs are the wild bamboo, beyond the last ward-posts, where the things that eat live, and where no House has held a stake in six hundred years. There's no one there to ask.

So that's where I start.

The track-laying machine is a train that builds its own road. It rolls forward on the last rail it laid, carrying rails and sleepers from behind, and at its front end, a swarm of rune lifters sets each sleeper, drops each rail, and holds it to the millimeter while crawling welders fuse the joints. Radar watches the bamboo on both sides. Portable ward-runes, small ones I can carry, keep the wild things back. It lays three kilometers a day, and it doesn't need me.

But I'm there for the first one anyway.

There's a tradition on Earth, when a railway is finished, of driving a golden spike. Mine is at the start, not the finish, because I'm not waiting four hundred kilometers to celebrate. And it's not gold. It's silver, from Pinnacle, cast by Ottoline, stamped with her mark and with a small circle beneath it that means *made by Ruler.*

The people of Shingle climb the cliff path to watch. All three hundred and twelve of them. Marit stands at the front with her arms folded. The girl with the missing teeth stands beside her, shining a flashlight at the rails even though it's the middle of the day.

I raise the hammer. I drive the spike. The sound rings out over the cliff, clear and bright, and goes out over the sea.

Nobody cheers. It isn't their custom. But when I turn around, every one of them is looking not at the spike, but at the rail. Running away from the cliff, shining and new, into the wild green toward everywhere else.

---

That night, I sit at the top of the cliff with Bethany, looking down at Shingle.

Forty-one lit windows. The water tap gleaming at the head of the beach. The flues drawing clean smoke up into the dark. The boats pulled up in a neat row with their motors covered, safe inside a bay that nothing can enter unless I let it. Out past the narrows, on the radar screen, a long pale shape drifts slowly southward, patrolling a coast that belongs to it, and so, now, to me.

"You know what you're building," Bethany says.

"A kelp farm. A harbor. A railway."

"A country."

I don't answer right away. I'm thinking about the first presentation I ever gave her, in the lab, over Alphonseo's body. The slide about the Spanish and the British and the Americans, and how I intended to follow in their footsteps.

With at least fifty percent more human rights.

I look down at the lit windows of Shingle. At the clean water, and the children who will grow up breathing clean air, and the boats that will come home.

It was a joke at the time. Mostly. I've always been very good at math.

"The numbers came out that way," I say.

"Uh-huh," says Bethany, and doesn't make it weird.

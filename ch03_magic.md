# Chapter 3: Magic

"Darn."

I say it quietly. A leader should not show disappointment in front of her subordinates, even when that subordinate has failed to be obliterated by a thermonuclear device.

Bethany stands in the gap between two stalks, crystal cradled against her chest, squinting up at the sky as though she too has been expecting something to fall out of it. Nothing does. The bamboo creaks. Somewhere far above us, one of my drones adjusts its hover with a small, apologetic whine.

No nuke. No orbital lance. No sirens, no strike team, no furious local god. Just a tired woman holding a rock.

Still. A rock that glowed.

I have spent most of my adult life knowing everything, and I will tell you a secret about knowing everything: it is boring. Every problem is just a slower version of an answer you already have. But that crystal is humming with a pale, steady light that does not come from any chemistry I recognize, and I feel something I have not felt since my first chemistry set. Curiosity. Real, unsupervised curiosity. Maybe even the chance of new explosives.

"Bring it here," I say.

Bethany takes one step toward Billy. Then she stops.

Then she vanishes.

Not dramatically. There is no flash. One moment she is there, a slightly hunched scientist in a stained lab coat, and the next there is a lab-coat-shaped smear in the air, like heat shimmer on a summer road. The smear says, "Oh."

"Bethany."

"Oh," the smear says again, with considerably more feeling.

She flickers back into view, looking at her own hands. Then, experimentally, she scrunches her face in the way people do when trying to move their ears. Her feet leave the ground. Not far. Perhaps ten centimeters. She hangs there for about two seconds, wobbling like a bad hover drone, and then drops back into the mulch.

We look at each other across the clearing. I watch the arithmetic happen on her face. It is the same arithmetic she did in the loading bay, but for the first time the numbers are coming out in her favor.

"Why am I listening to you again?" she says slowly. "I have magic. Some kind of magic. I can turn invisible." She lifts the crystal a little, as if to show me. "I'm leaving."

She seems to be under the impression that she is allowed to make choices. It is a charming delusion. I decide to disabuse her of it.

I have found that messages are most clearly received when delivered in the form of bullets. Words can be misheard, but nobody misunderstands a bullet.

I have modified Billy's autocannon to fire considerably faster than its manufacturer considers wise. Ten thousand rounds a minute. For about five seconds, the forest is nothing but noise.

When it stops, there is a sad face carved into the soil about a meter in front of Bethany's feet. Two eyes, a downturned mouth. Smoking gently. It is, if I may say, quite well proportioned. The heat coming off it must be enough to feel on her shins.

Behind it, a bamboo stalk the width of an airplane begins to lean, thinks better of it, and settles.

"Does that clarify things?" I ask.

"M-message received."

She walks the crystal back to Billy with the careful steps of someone crossing a frozen lake, and hands it up to me. She does not turn invisible on the way. Good. She is learning.

---

Every good scientist, upon encountering a new object, begins with the same experiment. The poke test.

I poke it.

People dismiss the poke test, but that is because they do not appreciate the instrument. The human fingertip is a staggeringly sophisticated piece of biological hardware, dense with mechanoreceptors and thermoreceptors refined over a few hundred million years of evolution. One firm poke tells me the surface is hard, smooth, slightly warmer than the air, and faintly tingly in a way that is not temperature and not electricity. Average kinetic energy, modulus of elasticity, surface structure. All from one finger. Nobody gives the finger enough credit.

The tingle gets stronger the longer I hold it. It feels like the crystal is leaning toward me.

When a person discovers that magic is real, it tells you everything about who they are. A child thinks of flying. A romantic thinks of love potions. A priest has a very bad afternoon.

I have two thoughts, in this order.

The first is: *How do we make money with this?*

The second is: *Fireball.*

"You found magic," Bethany says, reading my face with an accuracy I find irritating, "and the first thing you thought about was money."

"Second thing was fireball."

"That's not better. There is something deeply wrong with you. On a fundamental level."

"What can I say? Social conditioning works. Every piece of media I've ever consumed was built around making money. What did you expect, wonder?"

"Says the cannibalism advocate."

"I made some very good points. You have to admit."

"Can you please never mention cannibalism to me again. Ever. I really don't want to hear it."

I look at her for a long moment.

"Sheep."

The fury that one word produces on her face is worth more than the crystal. But like a good sheep, she chooses to let it go.

"Fine," I say. "We test everything a magic system traditionally does, one at a time, and write it down. Science." I pull a notepad from one of my coat's many pockets. "But fireball first. For morale."

I close my hand around the crystal and pull. There is no better word for it. Something warm and thin slides out of the stone and up my arm, settling behind my sternum like a held breath. I picture a ball of fire the size of an orange. I picture it very clearly. I picture it sitting neatly in the palm of the nearest available hand.

The nearest available hand belongs to Bethany.

I know it has worked because she starts screaming immediately.

I will it away quickly, throwing it rather than extinguishing it, which in hindsight is an error. It sails into the undergrowth and sets a small patch of something on fire. Bethany is clutching her wrist and making a noise I would describe as operatic.

"Oh. Sorry. That's my bad. I assumed you'd get some sort of basic fire resistance, since it was your hand." I lean over to examine the damage. "It's really not that bad. Second-degree at most. A flesh wound."

"One day," Bethany says through her teeth, "I will kill you."

She says it as though she genuinely believes it is possible. I let her have that. It is important for the people around you to have hobbies. And since I am, in point of fact, immortal, it is a harmless one.

The burn does present an opportunity, though. A good scientist wastes nothing, least of all an injured colleague.

"Hold still. We're testing healing."

"You are not doing anything else to my hand."

"I'm making it better. That's the opposite of what I did before. You should be thrilled."

I pull another thread of warmth out of the crystal and picture her skin knitting closed, pink and new. Nothing happens. I picture it harder. I picture the cells dividing. I picture the specific proteins. I picture a nurse. After several minutes the crystal is noticeably dimmer, Bethany's hand is exactly as blistered as before, and she is glaring at me with an intensity that makes concentration difficult.

It appears that, unfortunately for Bethany, this magic cannot heal. Not broadly, anyway. I make a note.

She is making it very hard to focus with all the threats against my life. Which gives me an idea.

I look her in the eye, pull on the crystal, and think, very clearly: *You are calm. You are loyal. You love the plan.*

"Why are you staring at me like that," Bethany says.

*You would like to carry the heavy things.*

"Stop it."

Nothing. Mind control does not work. I will admit this is the result that disappoints me most. What is magic even for, if not to make everyone do what you say? It would have saved so much ammunition. Lame.

---

The rest goes faster once I stop trying to fix Bethany.

I can push. Not with my hands, but with that warm pressure behind my sternum, shoving outward. I nudge a fallen leaf, then a stone, then myself. I rise about half a meter before the effort makes my eyes water, and I hang there, hovering over the hood of an armored personnel carrier, feeling tremendously pleased. I am graceful about it. Bethany would tell you I am not. Bethany is biased.

I can bend light around myself, the same trick Bethany stumbled into. From the inside it looks like the world has been painted on a soap bubble.

I can stir the air into a short, sharp gust. I can pull moisture out of the humid forest into a wobbling ball of water. I can, of course, make fire. Elements, it seems, are negotiable.

And I can ask questions. That is the one that makes my breath catch. I hold the crystal, think *how hard does this world pull*, and simply *know*. Not a number exactly, but a weight, a sense of it, like knowing a bag is heavier than it looks before you lift it. I run the comparison three times against my own sense of Earth. Near enough 9.81 meters per second squared. Slightly less.

In the bamboo, surrounded by an unknowable world, I have learned the gravity. I am very nearly moved.

"You look happy," Bethany says suspiciously.

"I know something," I say. "It's the best feeling in the world."

---

The crystal is dimmer now, a lot dimmer, and the question of where the energy comes from has started to itch.

I pull up the feed from the drone I left watching the other crystals. They sit at neat intervals along the rows, but only along the rows where the bamboo has been cut. Where the stalks stand whole, there are no crystals at all. The felled stalks are not rotting in the usual way either, now that I look. They are grey. Drained. As if something has wrung them out.

"The plants," I say. "They cut the plants, and the crystals soak up whatever comes out. That's the harvest. Not the wood. The energy."

"So it's a battery farm," Bethany says. "A really, really big battery farm."

"This whole forest is a power plant." I look up at the ranks of bamboo marching to the horizon and feel a deep professional respect. "And not a single magnet. Barbarians."

Whether it works for anything besides giant bamboo, I have no way to know. I file it under *later*.

---

That leaves the important experiment.

"No," Bethany says, before I have even opened my mouth.

"You don't know what I'm going to do."

"You're going to try to make gold."

I am going to try to make gold.

I have a brass casing from the warning burst. Brass is copper and zinc. Gold is gold. It is only protons, really. A small rearrangement. I cup the casing in one hand and the crystal in the other, and I picture it turning yellow and heavy and valuable. I picture the market price per ounce. I picture a vault.

The crystal empties.

It does not dim. It *empties*, all at once, like a drain being pulled, and the warmth shoots out of my chest so fast I sit down on Billy's hood without meaning to. When I open my hand, the casing is exactly as brass as before. Slightly warm. That is all.

The crystal is now a lump of cloudy glass.

"Darn," I say, for the second time today.

Bethany looks at the dead crystal, then at me, and smiles for the first time since we left Earth. It is not a nice smile.

---

I write up the results. Science is not science until it's written down.

| Test | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Fireball | ✓ (sorry, Bethany) |
| Mind control | ✗ |
| Healing | ✗ |
| Elemental manipulation | ✓ |
| Force application | ✓ (including flight, briefly, gracefully) |
| Simple information about the world | ✓ (gravity ≈ 9.8 m/s²) |
| Direct removal of matter | ✗ |
| Transmutation | ✗ (can't change the properties of things that exist; very expensive to find out) |
| Actual necromancy | Untested |

The last line bothers me. Untested is not a result. It is an admission.

The problem is that I have no corpse. I do, technically, have a *future* corpse. She is sitting on a root nursing her hand and muttering. I suspect she would object to having her timeline moved up. And even if she didn't, I have no energy left to try it with, which would make it less of an experiment and more of a murder. I have already done one of those this week and do not want to be repetitive.

I will not kill Bethany just to test some magic, I decide. Probably. Not today.

I am still weighing the "probably" when Billy chimes.

One of the long-range drones, far out to the north, has stopped sending pictures of more bamboo. It is sending something else. Stone. Height. Smoke from a hundred chimneys, and lights, and towers packed so tightly together they look like a single structure.

A city.

I smile.

"Bethany," I say. "Get in. We're going to meet the peasants."

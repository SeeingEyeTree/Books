# Chapter 14: The Interview

### Angline

She tells me all of it.

The phone, ordered through Fernholt in a box of lab supplies. The lift up the shaft in the dark. The rain. The four rings. Two minutes and fifty-one seconds. The kitchen clock that runs four minutes fast, and the certificate on the fridge, and the crusher.

She tells it the way she'd present a failed experiment: in order, with times, leaving nothing out. She doesn't cry. She doesn't apologize, either, not in words. She just finishes, and puts her hands flat on the table between us, and waits.

The feed from the salt mine gate is still playing on my phone, on a loop. The gray car. The umbrella. The camera, lifting and lowering.

I watch it through twice more before I say anything.

"The phone touched a tower," I say. "The nearest one to the mine is on a hill four kilometers west. Her office asked your mother's carrier for every call to that number, got one from a phone that's never been seen before or since, looked at which tower carried it, and drew a circle. There are nine buildings in that circle. Eight of them are farms."

"I know," Bethany says quietly. "I worked it out on the way down."

"And you didn't tell me."

"You were in Pinnacle. I thought—" She stops. "I thought three minutes was nothing."

"Three minutes is enough to find anyone on Earth. You know that. You're a scientist."

"I know that *now.*" For the first time, her voice cracks a little. "I knew it then, too. I just wanted to hear her."

I look at her for a long moment.

There's a version of me that would be very angry right now. I can feel her in there, doing arithmetic: the salt mine, the door, the coast, everything I moved east because it was the one place nobody could touch. All of it, now, at the end of a phone call to a kitchen with a clock that runs fast.

But that version of me has never been very good at arithmetic when she's angry. And the numbers here, when I actually run them, don't come out the way she'd like.

"Three things," I say.

Bethany braces.

"First. You ordered a phone through my procurement system and I didn't catch it. That's not your failure. That's mine. A system that lets someone order an untraceable phone in a box of pipettes without anyone noticing is a system with a hole in it. I'll fix it."

She blinks.

"Second. You called your mother. I haven't called mine in nine years, and I have no intention of starting, but I'm told that other people's mothers are different. You'd been away from yours for eighteen months, and you'd just kept thirty-three people alive, and you wanted to tell someone. That's not irrational. It's just expensive."

"And third?"

I turn the phone around and show her the loop. The woman in the rain, lowering her camera, standing very still in front of the gate.

"Third. Detective Inspector Nkemelu found Alphonseo's email came from inside the building. She found his badge never left. She found the car. She's the only person on Earth who has ever checked Helsinki. And now, a year and a half later, with no case, no body, and no help, she's found a salt mine owned by three companies I built specifically so no one could find it. From one phone call." I pause. "Bethany. Do you know how rare that is?"

She stares at me.

"You didn't compromise the salt mine," I say. "You sent me a job applicant. The best one I've ever had."

"You're going to *hire* her?"

"I'm going to *interview* her," I say. "She's already passed the practical."

---

She comes back the next night. I thought she would. People like her always come back to the thing they haven't solved.

I'm waiting inside the gate, under the shelter of the shaft head, with two coffees in paper cups and an umbrella. I leave the glove in the Green. I bring the pistol, because I'm not a fool, but I keep it in a pocket I'd need both hands to reach. And a block of C4 in the lining, for good measure, because I bring explosives everywhere and a salt mine is not special. This is an interview. You don't bring a weapon to the table, even if you'd like the other side to know you own one.

The gray car pulls up at 11:20 and parks where it parked before, lights off. She sits in it for a while. Then she gets out, with her umbrella and her camera, and walks to the gate, and sees me.

She doesn't jump. I give her full marks for that.

"Inspector Nkemelu," I say, through the chain-link. "You look wet. I brought coffee."

She looks at me for a long moment. At the coffee. At the coat.

"Dr. Ruler," she says. "You're under arrest."

"Am I?" I open the gate and hold out a cup. "You're alone. You came in your own car, at night, with a personal camera. There's no one else in that car, and no one parked down the road; I've had a drone on it for an hour. Nobody arrests the most wanted woman in Europe alone, without a warrant, at midnight, unless they've been told to stop." I tilt my head. "Who closed your case, Inspector? Was it a colonel?"

Her face doesn't change. That's how I know I'm right. People whose faces don't change are working very hard.

"Eleven months ago," she says at last. "National security. They took my files. I was told Dr. Alphonseo's disappearance is a matter for other agencies, and I should find something else to do."

"And you didn't."

"I did. I have forty-one open cases." She takes the coffee, finally, and doesn't drink it. "I do this one on my own time."

"Why?"

"Because nobody else would. Because a man walked into a building and never came out, and everyone who could tell me why decided it didn't matter." She looks past me at the shaft head, the lift housing, the crusher. "Because it's the most interesting thing that's ever happened to me, and I can't stand not knowing."

I smile. It's an honest smile. I don't give many of those.

"Then let's start with that," I say. "You want to know what happened to Alphonseo. I'll tell you. All of it."

---

I tell her.

I tell her standing in the rain by the shaft head, while the lift hums behind us, the way I'd give a technical briefing. The lab, the morning of the Friday. The pistol. What he was going to stop me from doing. The shot. The iron in his blood and the iron in the bullet, and that I prefer to call it adjacent to natural causes, though I appreciate that she may not. The projector warming up while he died. The email to the department, from the lab, which I now accept was sloppy. And then, on the Saturday morning, at four o'clock, the thermal reservoir, and the gloves, and one of his feet each.

She listens without interrupting once. When I finish, she's very quiet for a long time.

"The pool," she says finally. "I stood in front of it. I asked you what it was for."

"And I said heat."

"It was a confession."

"It was an honest answer. I don't lie when I don't need to. It's inefficient." I sip my coffee. "You'd have found out eventually. You were closer than anyone. I'd rather you heard it properly, from someone who can explain the parts you'd have gotten wrong."

"Why are you telling me at all? You understand I'm a police officer."

"I understand that you're a police officer who's been told to stop, and didn't, and found me anyway, with nothing but a phone record and your own time." I set down my cup on the ledge of the lift housing. "I'm telling you because the truth about Alphonseo isn't the interesting part, Inspector. It's the smallest thing I could show you tonight. It's the answer to a question you've been asking for eighteen months. And it's nothing, *nothing*, compared to the question you haven't asked yet."

"Which is?"

"Where the salt mine goes."

She looks at the shaft head. At the lift. Two hundred meters of dark down there.

I can see her weighing it. She's a careful woman, and I'm the person who shot a man and put him in lava, and there's a very reasonable voice in her head telling her to go back to her car, call someone, anyone, and never come here again.

Then I watch the other voice win. The one that drove out here alone, at midnight, on her own time.

"Show me," she says.

---

We ride the lift down together. Four minutes, in the dark. She keeps one hand in her coat pocket the whole way, where I assume she has something she'd like me to know she has. I don't mind. It's only polite.

At the bottom, the salt gallery glitters in the floodlights, and the rails run down the middle of it, and at the far end, a circle of shimmering air hums six meters across, and through it, faintly, blows a warm wind that smells of the sea.

Nkemelu stops walking.

She stands on the rails and looks at it for a long time. I don't say anything. I've learned, since the gate guard and the flashlight, that some things are better seen in silence.

"What is that," she says finally, very quietly. It isn't a question.

"The answer," I say, "to the question you haven't asked yet."

She walks to it. She puts her hand out, the way people do at the edge of a waterfall, and feels the warm wind on her fingers. Then she looks back at me once, and steps through.

I follow her.

On the other side, it's night, and the air is warm and dry, and the cliff top stretches away under a sky full of stars no one on Earth has ever named. Below us, the town glows: fourteen hundred lit windows, the water tower, the clinic with its sign, the pier running out into the black bay. Beyond it, the Brine, silver under a moon that is very slightly the wrong color.

Detective Inspector Nkemelu stands on the cliff with her wet umbrella still in her hand and looks at all of it.

"Oh," she says.

---

I give her the tour. It takes the rest of the night.

She doesn't talk much. She looks. She's a professional looker, and she looks at everything the way I'd look at a machine I was going to be asked to fix: the water tower and the filters, the flues on the cabins, the latrines downhill and downwind. The kelp lines in the bay. The railway, running inland into the dark. She asks short, precise questions. How many people. Where from. Who pays for this. Who decides.

I answer all of them honestly. It's a novel experience. I find I rather like it.

At the top of the cliff, I take a mote out of my pocket and put it in her palm. She holds it up to the light. She feels the tingle climb her wrist, and I watch her decide not to flinch.

"What is it?"

"Money," I say. "And fuel. And, if you know how to ask it, a way to find things out." I close her fingers around it. "Keep that one. You'll want to understand it later."

---

The clinic lights are on. They're always on.

Bethany is inside, sitting at the courtyard table with her notebook, pretending to work. She's been pretending since I left for the salt mine. She stands up when we come in, and then she sees who's with me, and goes very still.

Nkemelu looks at her for a moment. She has seen this face before: on a television screen, beside the words *murder* and *terrorism*, and in a holiday photograph on a mother's fridge.

"Dr. Bethany," she says.

"Inspector."

There's a long silence. I'm fairly sure both of them are waiting to be arrested, in different ways.

Then Nkemelu says, in a quite different voice, the one I suspect she uses at doorsteps when the news is not the worst kind:

"I went back to see your mother last week. After I traced the call." A pause. "She's well. She wanted me to tell you, if I ever found you, that she's fixed the kitchen clock."

Bethany sits down very suddenly.

I decide it's a good moment to go and look at the water tower.

---

I find Nkemelu at dawn, on the cliff edge, watching the sun come up over the Brine. The sea turns from black to gray to a pale, shining green. Below us, the fishing boats are going out, their rune motors humming, their radar screens glowing in the sterns.

She doesn't look at me when I come up beside her.

"You killed a man," she says.

"One."

"That's not a defense."

"No. It's a count." I nod down at the town. "Fourteen hundred and sixty people down there have clean water. Thirty-three of them are alive because of a woman whose mother you visited. Before we came, the Glass took eleven people a year from this beach, and summer took more." I shrug. "I'm not asking you to approve. I'm telling you the numbers."

"You're doing arithmetic with a human life."

"I'm always doing arithmetic. So are you. You have forty-one open cases, and you drove out here at midnight for this one. You decided it mattered more." I look at her. "We both count, Inspector. We just count different things."

She's quiet for a long time.

"What do you want?" she says finally.

It's the only question that matters. For once, I'm glad to be the one answering it.

---

"I want to hire you," I say. "Not to arrest anyone. Not to work for me, exactly. To investigate. On Earth, where I can't go, with every tool I have."

"What tools?"

"Drones that can sit on a roof for a week. A shell company to be anyone you need to be. Money you won't have to account for to a superintendent. A translator that can learn a language in six hours. And that." I nod at her closed hand, where the mote is. "There's a way of holding one of those and asking the ground what's under it, and the ground answers. Not perfectly. But it answers."

She looks down at her hand.

"And what would I be investigating?"

I give her two cases. I've been carrying them around for months.

"The first. Sub-basement four. The room I blew up. There was a door there, the first one, and I closed it. But a door, once it's been torn open, leaves something behind. A scar. It's much easier to open again than it was to open the first time. And the building now belongs to a colonel who believes it's his."

She gets it immediately. I watch her get it.

"You think they're trying to reopen it."

"I think if they manage it, a company of soldiers comes out in a field fourteen kilometers from a city of two hundred thousand people who have kept a very delicate peace for five hundred years. And the first thing any of them will see is men with rifles." I look at her. "I'd like to know what they're doing down there. Who's working on it. How close they are. I can't ask, and you can."

She's quiet for a moment. "And the second?"

"The first door was already there when they found it. Under an orphanage. Nobody made it; it was a weak place, a thin spot, dormant, waiting. I've made two doors since, from nothing, and each one cost me a season and thirty thousand motes." I spread my hands. "If there was one natural thin place on Earth, there may be others. Buildings where compasses misbehave. Construction sites that stopped for no reason. Rooms that are always slightly too cold. Somewhere, there are reports nobody read. I want every one of them found."

Nkemelu looks out at the sea for a long time. The boats are small now, near the bay mouth, and past them, on the edge of the radar's range, something long and pale drifts slowly south.

"I'm a police officer," she says. "I'll still be one. I'm not giving up my warrant card."

"I'd be disappointed if you did."

"If I ever find out you've done something I can't live with, I'll arrest you. Or try."

"I'd expect nothing less. Put it in the contract."

"There isn't a contract."

"There's always a contract," I say. "Sometimes it's just very short."

She finally turns and looks at me. There's something in her face that I recognize, because I see it in the mirror every morning: the terrible, helpless pleasure of a mind that's just been handed a problem it can't put down.

"I'm not doing this for you," she says.

"I know. You're doing it because you can't stand not knowing." I hold out my hand. "Neither can I. Welcome to the team, Inspector. I'm told we have excellent water."

### Bethany

I think about my mother for most of the next week, at odd moments. Checking chlorine at the tap. Signing for a flatcar of cabins. Lying awake in the clinic at night.

I don't know what to do with it. So I work.

---

There's a lot of work.

Oren comes down on the train at the end of the week, with a letter for Angline and news for anyone who'll listen. He likes the train. He sits in the front car with his face almost against the glass, like a child, and he gets off at the cliff looking ten years younger. Then he remembers why he came, and looks his age again.

*The Houses are building,* he tells us, at the clinic table, over tea.

Verdane's harvest-masters have been living in Farm One since the handover, eating there, sleeping there, walking every floor with notebooks. Ochre has started a tower of her own in a field outside the east wall: stone at the base, bamboo above, eight floors already. Sable is hiring rune carvers. All of them. At any price.

*Five years, you said.* Oren looks at Angline. *The Lord of Sable says three.*

Angline doesn't look worried. She never does. But that night she doesn't sleep either, and neither do I, and by morning the whiteboard in the clinic courtyard is covered in her handwriting.

---

"It's a race," she says, standing in front of it with a marker, while half the coast's foremen sit on crates and try to follow. "They have more land than we do. More people. Five hundred years of knowing exactly how every living thing in a field dies. And, in three years or five, they'll have towers."

"So how do we win?" asks Marit, who has decided, without asking anyone, that she sits in on everything now.

"We have two hundred years of industry they've never seen," Angline says. "Steel. Motors. Controllers. And *power.*" She underlines it. "Their towers can grow yeast. They can grow larvae and fungus in the dark. But the moment they want to light a floor of plants, they need a mote for every twenty minutes, and that's a tower that bankrupts its House in a season. Their copies will be half-farms. Unless they buy power from us." She smiles. "Which they will."

Then she writes the unit.

*FARMS PER WEEK, PER WEEK.*

The foremen look at each other. Marit frowns.

"Farms per week is how the Houses will think," Angline says. "A number, and a date. That's rookie arithmetic. I don't care how many farms we build. I care how fast the *rate* grows." She taps the words. "Every farm prints motes. The motes buy another swarm of lifters, another furnace at the ridge, another reactor. So every farm makes the next farm come faster. Right now, we can finish one every twelve days. I want that rate to double every ten weeks. By the end of the year, ten farms a week. By the end of the next, I stop counting and start measuring."

She draws a curve on the whiteboard. It starts flat and ends almost vertical.

"The trick is the bottlenecks," she says. "There's always one. You find it, and you push it until it isn't the bottleneck anymore, and then there's a new one, and you push that. Steel. Then power. Then feed. Then whatever comes after feed. Reality has a lot of bottlenecks. Every one of them can be pushed."

I look at the curve, and then out the window, where the fourteen hundred people of the coast are going about their morning, and I feel something in my stomach tighten. Because I already know what comes after feed.

---

She lays it out. It's a good plan. It's always a good plan.

The steel comes by rail. The right-of-way is signed, and now three track-laying trains work at once, one from the coast, one from the Red Ridge, one out of the middle, each laying three kilometers a day. The line will be through in six weeks instead of four months. Until then, steel comes through the salt mine from Earth, bought by Kessel, which Angline hates, because every tonne is a tonne someone on Earth might wonder about.

The power comes from a reactor. The coast needs its own, and there's finally enough tritium for it: a second full-size plant, on the plateau behind the town. Angline names it *Bielefeld,* "because, as every German knows, Bielefeld doesn't exist, and neither, officially, does this."

The lifters get better. The optimizer has found a way to carve a second motif into a push rune, a keel, Angline calls it, that sends the rune's backward push into the ground under it instead of into the machine. It's less efficient. It also means the lifters don't need heavy platforms to push against anymore. They're half the weight, and they build a floor in a day and a half instead of two.

And the feed comes from the bay: kelp, thousands of tonnes of it, sugar for the yeast, grown for free in the cold green water.

"Steel's handled. Power's handled. Feed's handled," Angline says. "On that curve, we finish the year with about a hundred and fifty farms."

"And who works in them?" I ask.

She turns around.

"A hundred and fifty farms," I say. "Even with the lifters and the controllers, every one needs harvest crews, maintenance crews, someone to run the chambers, someone to fix a lifter when it breaks. Call it sixty people a farm. That's nine thousand people. That's everyone on this coast, Angline. Including the children. Including Marit. And none of them had seen a screen before this year."

The courtyard goes quiet. Marit, beside me, doesn't move, but I can feel her approving.

Angline looks at me for a long moment. I've learned to recognize this look. It's the one she gives a number she didn't expect, before she works out where it came from.

"Robots," she says. But she says it slowly, the way she says things she's already doing the math on.

I wait. She does the math out loud, because she can't help it.

"The lifters and welders were worth automating. One job, done ten thousand times, the same way every time. But a harvest floor isn't one job. It's a hundred small ones, all different, all changing when the optimizer changes the crop. I'd need machines for each, and models to run them, and I'd need data to train the models, and there isn't any. Nobody has ever run a farm like this. I'd spend a year building robots to save a month of teaching people." She grimaces. "It's spending five hours automating a job that takes twenty minutes by hand. Scaled up to an empire."

"So people," I say.

"So people." She looks at the curve. "Which makes people the bottleneck."

"Then push it," I say. "Like you said."

So I tell her how.

Housing first, for every farm, before the frame goes up. A training school, six weeks, paid. And the schools have to grow on the same curve as the farms, or the curve stops. So the best students from every cohort stay on to teach the next one. Trainers training trainers. Every cohort bigger than the last.

And hiring, everywhere we can reach. Every village on this coast, down to the southern headlands and past them. Every town along the railway, the moment the line opens. The lower cities of Pinnacle, where half the people I met on our first day were looking for work.

"The Houses won't love that," Marit says.

"The Houses can compete on wages," says Angline, with something close to glee.

"And they'll be *loyal,*" I add, because I know what language she thinks in. "People you trained, housed and paid. That's your legitimacy. You said it yourself."

Angline looks at the whiteboard for a long time. Then she picks up the marker, and under *FARMS PER WEEK, PER WEEK,* she writes a second line, the same shape.

*TEACHERS PER WEEK, PER WEEK.*

"Fine," she says. "Doctor Director. The people curve is yours."

I don't laugh. But I want to.

---

The first school opens eleven days later, in six containers on the plateau, with thirty-two students. The youngest is sixteen. The oldest is Marit, who says she's only there to keep an eye on things, and who is, by the end of the first week, the best in the class at reading a controller screen. The second cohort, six weeks later, is seventy-one students, and four of the first class are teaching it.

The first coastal farm switches on the same month: thirty floors, kelp-fed, printing forty thousand motes a day, staffed entirely by people from the coast.

I stand on its roof at dusk, with the bay below and the lit town behind, and the rail running away west into the dark where, somewhere, three Houses are building towers as fast as they can.

For the first time in my life, I'm in a race and I'm not afraid of losing it.

### Angline

Nkemelu's first report arrives five weeks later, at two in the morning, through the salt mine door. On paper.

I gave her a secure tablet, an encrypted phone, and a drone kit worth more than her car. She comes up the cliff path with a cardboard folder under her arm, held shut with an elastic band.

"Paper doesn't get hacked," she says, when I look at it. "And it burns."

I decide not to argue. I respect a woman with a threat model.

We sit in the clinic courtyard, under the lamp. Bethany comes out in her lab coat, with tea for all three of us, and sits down without being asked. Nkemelu opens the folder.

---

"The sub-basement first," she says.

She lays out photographs. The building, from across the street: scaffolding, a new security fence, guards at the loading dock I once drove Billy out of. A drone shot from the roof of a neighboring office, zoomed in on a ventilation stack: on the thermal image, it glows.

"The official story is structural remediation after a gas explosion," she says. "It's been eighteen months. The building is still closed. There's a defense contractor on the site with a classified contract, and the building's electrical supply was upgraded four months ago with a new substation feed." She taps a document. "Thirty megawatts."

I look at the number. A great deal more than a remediation needs. Somewhat less than my flywheel bank delivers in a pulse.

"They've advertised," she goes on, laying down a printout of a job listing, very carefully anonymized. "Three posts. *Field resonance engineer.* Requires experience in high-energy pulsed power and electromagnetic field shaping. Security clearance essential." She looks at me. "Is that a real job?"

"It is now," I say. "It's my job. Where did they get the design?"

"From you." She lays down one more photograph: a gray metal box, scorched and dented, on a lab bench under fluorescent light, surrounded by people in lab coats. I recognize it immediately. VENTILATION IMPROVEMENT, UNIT 4. What's left of it. "They dug it out of the rubble. The charge on it didn't destroy it completely. And they recovered your lab servers from the fifth floor." A pause. "You kept backups."

I close my eyes.

I kept backups. Of course I kept backups. I'm an engineer. The one time in my career when I should have been sloppy, I was diligent.

"How do you know all this?" Bethany asks.

Nkemelu hesitates. It's the first time I've seen her hesitate.

"I have a source inside the building," she says. "Facilities. He's been there eleven years. Nobody listens to him. He was very pleased that someone finally did."

There's a long silence.

"Gerald," I say.

"Gerald," says Nkemelu.

Bethany puts her face in her hands. I think she's laughing. I'm not. I'm thinking about the ledger, and the line I drew under Gerald's name, and the note beside it: *Gerald gets nothing.*

Apparently, Gerald has found something to do with his nothing.

---

"How close are they?" I ask.

"Gerald says they've booked the substation for a full-power test in eight weeks. There's a date on a whiteboard in the room above the sub-basement. He photographed it." She slides the photograph across. A whiteboard. A date. Beside it, in neat engineering handwriting, a sketch I recognize, because I drew the original: a resonator, and a circle, and an arrow pointing through it.

And underneath, the words: *RECOVERY OF ACCESS.*

I look at it for a long time.

Thirty megawatts isn't enough to tear a new door. It wouldn't be enough for the Sable Gate, or the Reach, or anywhere nobody's torn before. But sub-basement four has been torn. The scar is there, under the rubble, waiting. And a door that's been opened once only needs a push.

Thirty megawatts is a push.

"If they open it," Bethany says slowly, "where does it come out?"

I've known the answer since Nkemelu opened the folder. I've been trying to find a way for it not to be true.

"The old portal site," I say. "In the bamboo. Northeast of Pinnacle. A few hundred meters from Farm One."

The farm I gave to House Verdane. With Verdane's harvest-masters living on every floor. A morning's walk from the Lady's tower, and the Shades who live in it, and a great crystal four hundred years old.

A company of soldiers with rifles, coming out of the air, into a House field, next to a House farm, in a world that has kept a very delicate peace for five hundred years by never, ever surprising anyone.

---

Nkemelu closes the folder. She puts the elastic band back around it, very carefully.

"There's more," she says. "The thin places. I have eleven reports so far. Most of them are nothing. Three of them aren't." She taps the folder. "But that can wait."

"It can," I agree.

She looks at me across the table, under the lamp. She's tired. She's been doing this on her own time, in her own car, on top of forty-one open cases, for five weeks. And there's something in her face I haven't seen in it before, not at the gate, not at the pool.

She's enjoying herself.

"Eight weeks," she says. "What are you going to do?"

I look at the photograph of the whiteboard. At the date. At my own resonator, drawn in someone else's hand, and the words underneath.

I think about the ledger. About the colonel who wanted a door, and my promise that one day I'd open one for him.

It seems he's decided to open one himself.

"Inspector," I say, "how would you feel about a little breaking and entering?"

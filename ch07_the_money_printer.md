# Chapter 7: The Money Printer

The drone feed comes through the portal perfectly well. Signals don't care which universe they're in. But I don't trust anything I can't pull on, so on Thursday morning I run a fiber-optic cable from the loading bay, down the ramp, and straight through the shimmering circle into the bamboo, and I bolt a junction box to the nearest stalk.

It's the first piece of infrastructure in the Green that belongs to me. I stand back and admire it for longer than a junction box deserves.

"It's a box," Bethany says.

"It's a *beachhead.*"

The search party is still out there. The drones have tracked them all night: six men sweeping the rows in slow, careful lines, and Oren at the front, shaking his dying flashlight like a thermometer. By morning they've drifted within half a kilometer of the portal. They look tired. They look like men who have been told not to come back without me.

I could avoid them. The portal is well hidden, the drones see everything, and Billy could put a sad face in the dirt in front of each of them.

But hiding is for people who can't win negotiations. I walk out to meet them.

I bring Bethany, because it's polite to bring staff. I bring my pistol, because it's prudent. And I bring one small AA battery in my coat pocket, because it's the most important thing I own.

We find them in a gap between two stalks, sitting on a fallen trunk and sharing a flask. The guards scramble up and level their spears, crystal tips glowing. Oren doesn't get up. He just looks at me for a while, with the expression of a man who has walked all night and been proven right in the worst possible way.

*Angline Ruler,* he says. *From farther than our maps go.*

"Oren," I say. "From House Verdane. You look tired."

*The Lady wants to see you.*

"I know. That's why I came." I hold out my hand. "Give me the light."

He hesitates, then hands it over. The beam is a faint orange smear, barely enough to see by. I unscrew the end, tip out the dead cell, and drop in the fresh one from my pocket. I screw it closed and click.

White light floods the clearing. It's so bright compared to the dying glow that two of the guards flinch.

Oren stares at it. Then at me.

*The Lady said it drinks,* he says slowly. *She said you didn't tell me from what.*

"She's right." I hand it back to him. "It drinks from a little cylinder, and I'm the only one in this world who can fill it." I let that land. "Your Lady doesn't need to find out what it drinks, Oren. She needs to find out what it costs."

He turns the flashlight over in his hands, the way he did the first time. I watch him understand, very slowly, that he is not holding a light. He's holding a subscription.

*She'll want to own it,* he says.

"Everyone wants to own it. That's how you know it's good." I nod toward the city. "Take me to her."

---

At the base of House Verdane's tower, a porter with a belt of motes offers to lift us to the top. Guests of the Lady don't climb.

I decline and take the stairs. All one thousand and eight of them.

Oren climbs behind me without a word, but around step six hundred I catch him looking at me with something new in his face. It might be respect. It might be concern for my knees. Bethany, somewhere below us, has stopped speaking entirely and is saving her breath for hatred.

The Lamp Hall is exactly as Oren's report would have described it, if I'd read it. Cold, white, enormous. The great crystal at its center hums like a transformer. The Lady sits beneath it in a high chair of pale bamboo, and she is older than I expected, thin and upright, with the stillness of someone who has never once needed to raise her voice.

She doesn't offer us seats. I take one anyway, a bench by the wall. I pull it over and sit across from her. Bethany collapses beside me.

*You are the woman with the light that doesn't drink,* the Lady says.

"It drinks," I say. "You were right. I'm the only one who can pour."

She studies me for a long time. *I could have you held here until you tell me how.*

"You could." I nod politely. "But the part that pours isn't in this city. It isn't in this *world.* If I don't go home, in eight weeks there'll be no more little cylinders, and every light I sell will go dark, and so will the only person who knows how to make them." I spread my hands. "I monologue responsibly, my lady. You'll get the details when you join the fold."

The Lady's mouth does something that, on a warmer face, might be a smile.

*What do you want?*

And there it is. The only question that matters in any negotiation. People think the trick is knowing what you want. The trick is making them ask.

"Three things," I say. "First: a charter. The right to sell light in every quarter of Pinnacle that House Verdane holds, under your seal, so nobody's guards bother my shops."

*And in return?*

"A tithe. One mote in every ten I take."

*Half.*

"One in ten. You don't have to build anything, staff anything, or fill anything. You just have to keep your collectors from stealing my lamps. One in ten of a very large number is a very large number."

She lets that sit. *And the second thing?*

"Land. Outside the wall, at the edge of the ring, northeast. Four hectares of bare ground where I can build."

*And the third?*

"Your rot."

For the first time, she looks surprised.

"When your harvesters cut the stalks," I say, "they leave them lying there. Gray, drained, rotting. I want the right to take them. All of them, from every field House Verdane owns."

*The husk-wood is worthless.*

"Then it won't cost you anything to give it to me."

The Lady looks at me for a long time, and I can see her searching the offer for the trap. She won't find it. The trap isn't in the offer. It's in what she doesn't know about steam turbines.

*There are three Houses in Pinnacle,* she says at last. *Verdane, Sable, and Ochre. If I refuse, you'll go to Sable.*

"I'd prefer not to. You sent a man to find me instead of a man to kill me. I like that in a partner." I shrug. "But Sable is only four towers away, and I have very good knees."

A long silence. The great crystal hums.

*One in ten,* she says. *The land, and the rot. Under my seal.* She lifts one thin finger. *And you will not harvest. Not on our land, not in our city, not without license from this House. Understood?*

I don't know what she means. I *hate* not knowing what someone means. But I have learned that the right response to a condition you don't understand, in a negotiation you're winning, is to agree and look it up later.

"Understood," I say.

*Oren will be your liaison. You will answer to House Verdane.*

"Of course," I say. "Completely."

On the way down, Bethany leans close to me. "You just promised to answer to her."

"I did."

"Do you?"

"She believes I do," I say. "It's load-bearing."

At the bottom of the stairs, I turn to Oren, who looks like a man who has just watched two storms agree to share a sky.

"Oren. What did she mean by *harvest?*"

He gives me a strange look, the kind you give an adult who asks what bread is.

*You should talk to Brask,* he says.

---

The Lyceum is the only building in Pinnacle that leans. It's a crooked tower of old brick at the eastern edge of the city, full of scholars who have long since stopped noticing that the floors aren't level. Oren leaves us at the door with the air of a man handing a cat to a vet.

Brask is in the basement, and I hear him before I see him. A rhythmic creak, a whir, and the unmistakable faint crackle of a spark.

He's a small, ancient man with white hair standing straight up, cranking a wooden handle with both hands. The handle turns a wheel. The wheel carries a lump of black lodestone. The lodestone spins inside a cage of hand-wound copper wire. And a needle on a crude dial twitches every time it goes around.

A generator. A real one. Hand-built, inefficient, beautiful.

I stand in the doorway and feel something I can only describe as religious.

"You spin the magnet," I say.

He looks up, still cranking. *Someone has to.*

We get along immediately.

It takes about ten minutes of mutual showing-off to establish trust. He shows me his wheel, his Leyden jars, and a frog leg that twitches when he touches it with two wires. I show him an LED and a nine-volt battery, and when the LED lights up, he sits down on the floor and laughs until he cries. Bethany, still recovering from the stairs, lies down on a bench and closes her eyes.

Eventually, I ask him.

"The Lady said I can't harvest without a license. Oren looked at me like I'd asked what bread was. What is harvest?"

Brask stops laughing. He gets up slowly, goes to a cabinet, and comes back with two things: a cloudy, empty husk, and a shallow basin with a live fish swimming in it, about the length of my hand.

*Nothing shines for free,* he says. *You've heard that?*

"The Lady said it."

*Everyone says it. Most don't think about what it means.* He sets the husk on the table beside the basin. Then he lifts the fish out, and before I can ask what he's doing, he strikes it once, sharply, against the table's edge.

The fish goes still.

The husk *glows.*

Not much. A faint blue warmth, like a coal. But a moment ago it was dead glass, and now it isn't.

I stare at it. Behind me, Bethany has sat up on the bench.

*Everything that lives,* says Brask, *gives something up when it stops living. The crystal drinks it. That's all a mote is. The end of something, kept.* He turns the husk in his fingers. *The Houses grow the stalks because nothing grows faster, and when the harvesters cut them down, the crystals by the fields drink the endings. That's the harvest. That's the money.*

And there it is. The missing term.

The butcher. Four motes in, four motes out, and the brightest crystal on the street, hung right over the chopping block. He wasn't running a business at zero margin. He was running two businesses, and the one that sold meat was just the part he could show people.

"The butcher," I say aloud.

*Holds a license from Verdane,* says Brask. *Every killing near a crystal in this city is licensed or it's theft. Poaching, they call it. Hands off for the first offense. The Houses don't only own the money, Ruler. They own the right to make it.*

I take out my scale and weigh the fish, because I can't help myself. Half a kilogram. I estimate the husk's new glow against the calibration I made on Monday. Roughly 0.4 of a mote.

"About 0.8 motes per kilogram," I murmur, writing. "For fish. How much does a stalk give?"

*A full stalk? Three, four thousand motes. Depends on the season.*

I run the numbers in my head. A stalk weighs several hundred tonnes, living. Four thousand motes from, say, four hundred thousand kilograms. A hundredth of a mote per kilogram. The fish gave eighty times more, per kilogram, than the bamboo.

"So animals are worth far more than plants, by weight," I say. "But the Houses farm plants."

*The fish took a year to grow,* Brask says. *A stalk grows a hundred meters in a season. Fed by nothing but rain and light.*

Of course. It isn't motes per kilogram that matters. It's motes per square meter per day. The Houses have spent centuries optimizing for it without ever writing the equation down. The best crop isn't the one that's worth the most when it dies. It's the one that grows fastest while it's alive.

I keep going, because a number that exists wants to be finished.

"A person," I say, "would be about seventy kilograms. At fish rates, fifty-some motes. Call it nineteen days of food."

The basement goes very quiet.

"*Angline,*" Bethany says.

"And eighteen years to grow," I continue. "Plus food, plus housing. Humans are a *catastrophically* bad crop. The numbers don't work at all." I underline it. "Good. That's settled."

"You did the *math on people.*"

"I do the math on everything," I say. "This time the math says no. That's what math is *for.*"

Brask is watching me with an expression I can't read. After a moment, he speaks, very quietly.

*Why do you think only the Houses may harvest? Someone did your sum, Ruler. A long time ago. Before the Houses made it law.* He sets the glowing husk down between us. *They didn't get the same answer you did. Not at first.*

Nobody says anything for a while. The lodestone wheel creaks to a stop.

Then I close my notebook, and I hold out my hand.

"Brask," I say. "How would you like to spin magnets for a living?"

---

I don't sleep that night. I sit in Billy's cabin in the tree line with the notebook on my knees and the fiber-optic junction box blinking green through the windshield, and I draw the machine.

Not a lamp business. Not a power plant. The thing the Houses have and nobody else is allowed to build.

A money printer.

The Houses farm bamboo in the open because they have no choice. Their crops need light, and the only light they can make on demand costs a mote per twenty minutes. So they grow under the sun, on the ground, one layer thick, across a sea of green the size of a country. It's the only way to farm when light is the most expensive thing in the world.

For me, light is cheap.

I write the chain down the page. *Husk-wood → boiler → turbine → electricity → LEDs → plants → harvest → motes.* An LED turns about half of its electricity into light. A mote turns a tenth of a percent of itself into light. I'm five hundred times better at making light than anyone on this planet, and light is what plants eat.

Which means I don't have to farm on the ground. I can farm *up.*

Bethany wakes up in the passenger seat around three and finds me still drawing.

"Is that a building?"

"It's a vertical farm. Thirty floors. Every floor a growing room, every room under LEDs, every harvest next to a crystal." I tap the page. "The only number that matters is motes per day. Per square meter of *ground.*"

"Why of ground?"

"Because that's how the Lady licenses harvest. By the field." Oren told me on the walk back: a license covers a plot, marked out in stakes. Not a volume. Not a yield. A patch of land. "She'll license me a quarter hectare to harvest on, and she'll think I'm growing vegetables in a shed. She measures in hectares. I measure in floors."

Bethany rubs her eyes. "And what are you growing?"

"That," I say, "is the question. And I'm not going to answer it. A machine is."

---

Here's the thing about optimizing a living system: you can't reason your way to the answer. Biology has too many variables, most of them unmeasured and half of them lying to you. You don't design a crop. You *search* for one.

So I design a search.

A hundred sealed growth chambers, each the size of a small refrigerator, each with its own LED panel, its own sensors for temperature, humidity and carbon dioxide, a camera, a scale, and a husk mounted at the top to catch whatever dies inside. A harvest arm in each one, so the killing happens on schedule and in front of the crystal. A lux meter on each husk that reads its glow every minute, calibrated against my calorimetry. Every chamber reports to a rack of computers, and on the computers runs an optimizer.

The optimizer's job is simple. Every day, it looks at every chamber and asks: *which one printed the most motes per day, per square meter?* Then it keeps the winners, changes the losers, and proposes new experiments. Different species. Different light colors. Different temperatures. Harvest at three days, at five, at eight. Feed with this, not that. It never sleeps and it never gets attached to a hypothesis. It's the best research assistant I'll ever have, and it doesn't complain about stairs.

"This is drug discovery," Bethany says, reading over my shoulder. "High-throughput screening. We used to run exactly this in pharma, with cell cultures."

"You worked in pharma?"

"For three years before Alphonseo. It's the same loop. Screen, measure, keep what works, iterate." She looks at the drawing again, and I can see something happening behind her tired eyes, some part of her that's been asleep for a long time waking up. "Except the drug is money."

"The drug is *always* money," I say. "This time we're just being honest about it."

She takes the pencil out of my hand and starts adding to the chamber diagram. A nutrient drip. A pH sensor. Duplicates of every condition so the results can be trusted.

"You need controls," she says. "Every experiment needs controls."

I let her keep the pencil. I've found my biology lead.

---

We spend the rest of the night on what goes into the chambers first. The optimizer needs a starting population, and a bad start costs weeks.

*Bamboo.* Obviously. The Houses spent centuries breeding it for speed. But we cut it young, at the shoot stage, when it's growing fastest, not at a hundred meters.

*Duckweed and algae.* The fastest-growing plants on Earth. Duckweed can double its mass in two days. It floats in trays that stack perfectly.

*Fungus.* This is the one I'm most excited about. "Mushrooms don't photosynthesize," I tell Bethany. "They don't need light at all. They grow in the dark, on dead plant matter, and on Earth we already farm them on racks stacked floor to ceiling. They're built for vertical farms." I tap the stack of gray stalks in the drone image. "And we have hundreds of tonnes of dead plant matter that the Houses call rot."

"So the fungus eats the husk-wood."

"Some of it. The rest goes to the boilers. And then—"

*Insects.* Brask's fish gave eighty times more motes per kilogram than bamboo. Animals are worth more when they die. And there is one animal on Earth that grows faster than almost anything: the black soldier fly larva. It eats rotting organic waste, including spent mushroom substrate, and it can multiply its body weight thousands of times in two weeks.

I draw the loop, and it's the prettiest thing I've drawn since the turbine.

*Husk-wood feeds the fungus. The fungus is harvested. The spent substrate feeds the larvae. The larvae are harvested. Their droppings fertilize the duckweed and bamboo. The plants are harvested. Their waste feeds the fungus again. Whatever's left burns in the boiler, and the boiler lights the floors.*

Nothing wasted. Everything, eventually, ending in front of a crystal.

"It's an ecosystem," Bethany says quietly.

"It's a *mint,*" I say. "An ecosystem is just a mint that hasn't been optimized yet."

---

The first trailer comes through the portal eight weeks later, on a Tuesday, with four centimeters to spare on each side. I ride on top of it. I'm not sure I've ever been happier.

After that, it's a river. Trailer after trailer, pulled through by Billy, loaded in the sub-basement at night and unloaded in the bamboo by morning. Cartons of LEDs. Pallets of batteries. The Latvian turbine, in eleven pieces, each one labeled in Janis's careful handwriting. Boiler sections. Copper. Steel. Rourke's budget pays for a new portal monitoring suite in sub-basement four, as described in the paperwork, and the suite happens to include a very good forklift.

The site is four hectares of bare ground at the northeast edge of the ring, marked out with House Verdane's stakes. Brask arrives on the first morning with a bag of tools and forty years of pent-up ambition, and within a week he's teaching a crew of local laborers how to wind copper and pour concrete, and paying them in motes out of my pocket, and refusing to go home at night.

The drained stalks come in on carts, pulled by porters who laugh at me for paying them to haul rot. They stop laughing when they see the boiler.

---

I light it myself, on the thirty-ninth day.

Husk-wood burns hot and clean, better than I'd hoped. The water in the boiler climbs toward steam. The pressure gauge rises. And then, with a sound I will remember on my deathbed, which will not be for a very long time, the turbine begins to turn.

Slowly at first. Then faster. A low whine that climbs into a hum, a hum that climbs into a song.

The magnets spin.

Brask stands beside me with tears running openly into his beard. I don't cry. I'm perfect, and perfect people don't cry. But there's a great deal of steam in the air, and my eyes do get damp, and I choose not to investigate the cause.

"It's just a turbine," Bethany says, from behind us, very gently.

"Bethany," I say, "nothing is *just* a turbine."

---

The first swap station opens in the lower city eight days later, at the bottom of the same stone shaft where I once watched a noble toss a husk down to fighting children.

It's a narrow shopfront with a counter, a wall of charged batteries glowing faintly green on their chargers, and a sign above the door in the local script that Billy translated for me and that I painted myself. It says, simply: LIGHT. ONE MOTE A WEEK.

By the time we open the shutters in the morning, the line goes around the corner.

We sell out of lamps by noon. We sell out again the next day. By the end of the first week, two thousand one hundred households have subscriptions. By the end of the month, nine thousand, and three more stations are open in House Verdane's quarters, each with a line.

And on the fourth evening, walking home through the shaft at dusk, I look up.

The lower city is lit.

Not brightly. Not like the Leaf bridges. But window after window, all the way up the dark canyon of stone, glows warm and white where last month there was nothing. A woman is sorting laundry by it. A cobbler is still working. And three stories up, in a window I recognize, a boy of about twelve is sitting with a book on his knees, reading by a lamp he pays a mote a week for, because he wants to and now he can.

I look at him for a while.

"You're smiling," Bethany says.

"It's *market penetration,*" I say. "Look at that. Nine thousand households. That's a monopoly."

"Uh-huh."

"A monopoly on light, in a city where light is *status.*" I wave a hand at the glowing windows. "Every one of those people knows who lit their home. Not the House. Me. When the Houses want something from the lower city, they'll have to ask the person who keeps the lights on."

She keeps looking at me.

"And also," I say, "the boy can read now. Which is a pleasant side effect. Don't make it weird."

---

The politics start the following week.

An envoy from House Sable arrives at the plant with a gift of wine and a question about whether I'd consider selling in Sable's quarters. An envoy from House Ochre arrives the day after with a slightly bigger gift and the same question. Word has spread through the towers: the lower city of Verdane is lit, and the lower cities of Sable and Ochre are not, and they are noticing.

I tell both envoys I'm bound by my charter with Verdane, and I'm sure something could be arranged, and I'd so love to meet their Ladies. Then I send a note up to the Lamp Hall, through Oren, mentioning very casually that Sable and Ochre have been asking.

The Lady grants me the harvest license the next morning. A quarter hectare, staked out at the corner of my site. The fee is one mote in ten of the yield. She thinks it's a reward for loyalty.

It's a quarter hectare of ground. I'm going to put thirty floors on it.

---

The research loop starts on the day the license arrives. A hundred chambers, humming in a shed beside the turbine hall, each with a husk mounted at the top like a small glass eye. Bethany runs it. She's there before me every morning, which I've decided not to take personally.

Six weeks later, the first real results come in. We read them together in the shed at midnight, with the turbine singing next door.

The Houses' bamboo fields, we've calculated, make about a third of a mote per square meter of ground per day. That's the number to beat.

One floor of duckweed trays, under LEDs, does 0.4. Bamboo shoots, cut young: 0.6. Oyster mushrooms on husk-wood, in the dark, no light at all: 0.9. And the black soldier fly larvae, eating the spent mushroom substrate: 1.7, per floor.

Multiply by thirty floors.

"Fifty motes per square meter of ground per day," Bethany says softly. "From the full loop. Stacked."

"A hundred and fifty times what the Houses get from the same land," I say. "And that's the *first* six weeks. The optimizer hasn't even started getting clever yet."

The money printer is on.

But Bethany isn't smiling. She's scrolling through the chamber data with a small frown, the one I've learned means she's found something she didn't expect.

"Chamber sixty-one," she says. "The optimizer put yeast in it last week, just to test a nutrient mix. It's not supposed to be a crop."

"And?"

She turns the screen toward me.

Per kilogram harvested, chamber sixty-one is printing seven times what the larvae do. Seventy times what the bamboo shoots do. Its husk is the brightest one in the shed.

We look at it for a long time.

"That doesn't fit the mass model," I say.

"No." Bethany pulls up the other chambers beside it. "But look at what yeast *is.* No shell. No wood. No bones. It's nothing but living cells, all of them working as hard as they can." She taps the bamboo line. "A stalk is mostly wood. Dead cells holding up a thin layer of living ones. The fish was nearly all muscle and blood."

I see it before she finishes. Fish, eighty times the bamboo. Larvae, soft and fast-growing, better than the mushrooms. Young shoots better than old stalks. It's been in the data the whole time.

"It isn't how *much* dies," I say slowly. "It's how *alive* it was when it did."

Bethany nods.

I open the optimizer's configuration on the screen, find the objective function, and add a second term under the first. *Maximize living tissue at harvest.* Soft, fast, hot, hungry things. Everything growing as hard as it can, right up to the moment it stops.

The turbine keeps singing next door. It doesn't care. It just spins.

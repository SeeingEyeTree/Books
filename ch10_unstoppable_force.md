# Chapter 10: Unstoppable Force

The disguise is the worst thing I have ever done. I want that clear. I have shot a man, lowered him into lava, and blown up a government facility, and none of those required me to wear a cardigan.

It's beige. It has pilling at the elbows. Bethany bought it from a charity shop along with a mousy brown wig, a pair of thick-framed reading glasses, and a canvas tote bag that says I ♥ SOURDOUGH. When I put it all on and look in the bunker's cracked mirror, I see a woman who has opinions about parking at the garden center.

"Nobody will look at you twice," Bethany says, with what I believe is real pleasure.

"Nobody will look at me *once.* That's the problem. I've spent my entire life being looked at. It's load-bearing."

"It's three hours."

"It's three hours of *being beige.*"

We take a rented van with forty kilograms of silver in the back, wrapped in old towels. That's a test load. The dealer is in a port city two hours north: a narrow coin shop between a chandlery and a closed-down bakery, with a sign in the window that says WE BUY GOLD and, underneath, in much smaller letters, and other things.

Her name is Ottoline Maas. She's about seventy, small and sharp, with a jeweler's loupe on a chain around her neck and the calm of a woman who's been offered stolen goods by every kind of person there is. Bethany found her through a man who knew a man who used to fix Billy's predecessor. It's a long chain of men. It ends in Ottoline.

She unwraps the first bar in the back room, under a bright lamp, and turns it over in her hands. She weighs it. She scratches a corner and tests it with a drop of something from a brown bottle. She fits the loupe to her eye and studies the stamp, the cartographer's mark from Pinnacle: a little tower inside a circle.

Then she asks exactly one question.

"Where is this mark from?"

"Somewhere with very bad paperwork," I say.

Ottoline considers this for a moment. Then she nods, as if it's the most sensible answer anyone has ever given her.

"It'll need melting down. Recasting. New bars, my stamp, and it's as clean as anything in a bank." She sets the bar down. "Twelve percent for the melt and my silence. Cash, or transfer to any company you like, as long as the company has a boring name."

"Fernholt Data Storage."

"That's very boring," she says approvingly. "That'll do."

---

On the drive back, still in the cardigan, I do the math on the back of the van's rental agreement.

Forty motes a kilogram in Pinnacle. Ottoline's price per kilogram, minus her twelve percent. Motes per kilogram there, euros per kilogram here. The kilograms cancel. What's left is euros per mote, by way of silver, and it's a far better rate than any mote will ever get on Earth as heat.

A mote on Earth is a cup of tea. A mote spent on silver in Pinnacle is dinner for two at a restaurant with cloth napkins.

"So it works," Bethany says.

"It works. Six hundred kilos covers the flywheel deposits and the first turbine." I fold the paper into my tote bag. It's the only good thing about the tote bag. "And once the door's open, the farm prints motes, the motes buy silver, Ottoline turns the silver into boring money, and the boring money buys turbines. A loop."

"A laundering loop."

"A *supply chain,*" I say. "Laundering is just what supply chains are called by people who haven't got one."

I pull off the wig at the first red light out of town. I can feel my hair starting to recover.

---

The flywheels arrive over six weeks, in trucks marked FERNHOLT DATA STORAGE — CRITICAL INFRASTRUCTURE, at hours when nobody is awake to wonder why a data center needs twelve grid-storage flywheels and a room with blast doors. They go down the long tunnel on Billy's trailers and into the hangar, and we stand them in a row along the wall like a line of steel soldiers.

Each one is a heavy rotor in a vacuum drum, riding on magnetic bearings, able to spin at twenty thousand revolutions a minute. Each one stores about ninety megajoules. Twelve of them: a little over a billion joules, all spinning in the dark.

"It's beautiful," I say, the first time Billy's reactor spins them up. The hum starts low and climbs into a note so pure I can feel it in my fillings.

"It's loud," says Bethany.

"Beauty is often loud."

One megawatt from Billy fills the whole bank in about twenty minutes. That's the good news: I can try, recharge, and try again, all day. The bad news is that the whole bank is only a billion joules. Four hundred megawatts for two and a half seconds. Or a hundred megawatts for eleven. It's the bottom corner of my honest range, not the top.

So we'll find out quickly whether the bottom corner is enough.

Key number two sits at the far end of the hangar: a new resonator, built over a month from memory and improved in every way I could think of, mounted on a steel frame where the Cold War generals once kept their map tables. Thick cables run from it to the flywheels. A camera, a thermal imager and a radio receiver watch the empty air in front of it.

---

Attempt one: a hundred megawatts for five seconds.

The flywheels roar down. The lights in the hangar dip. The air in front of the resonator shivers like heat over a summer road and then goes still.

Nothing.

I recharge. Twenty minutes. Bethany makes coffee that tastes like 1983.

Attempt two: the whole bank, four hundred megawatts, all at once.

The flywheels scream, and then, in less than three seconds, fall silent. And in the air in front of the resonator, at head height, there's a point of light.

It's the same point I watched sub-basement four's portal collapse into, the night I closed it. A door, curled up as small as a door can be.

It hangs there for forty seconds. Then it winks out.

"That's something," Bethany says.

"That's a *door,*" I say. "Too small to use. Too short to hold. But it's a door." I'm already scribbling. "It opened. Something on the other side just couldn't keep it open."

"On the other side?"

"Doors have two sides, Bethany. The old one had a thin place holding it from the Green. This one has nothing. It's like pushing open a door with no hinges." I look at the flywheels, humming back up to speed. "I can push from here all I like. Someone needs to pull from there."

"With what?"

"Motes," I say. "Ten times stronger over there. And the vault is full."

---

Attempt three is not about opening a door. It's about opening it long enough for me to get through.

I rebuild the pulse so the flywheels discharge in stages instead of all at once: a big kick to open the point, then smaller kicks, one after another, holding it open. By the eighth try, the point opens into a ragged circle about sixty centimeters across and holds for four minutes before it sags.

Four minutes. Sixty centimeters.

Enough for a person, if the person is determined and not precious about her coat.

"No," says Bethany.

"You don't know what I'm going to do."

"You're going to crawl through a hole in the universe in the middle of House Sable's farm and walk fourteen kilometers in the dark to find Brask."

I am going to do exactly that.

"If it closes behind you—"

"Then you recharge the flywheels and open it again. Twenty minutes." I hand her the radio. "Signals pass through. When we're ready over there, I'll call. You'll fire the full sequence from this side, and we'll pull from that side at exactly the same moment."

She looks at the radio. Then at me. "And if it doesn't work?"

"It'll work." I shrug out of my coat, fold it, and hand it to her. "Look after this. It's irreplaceable."

At 11:52 that night, the flywheels roar, the ragged circle opens in front of the resonator, and I go through it on my hands and knees, face first, into warm, green, night-dark air that smells of cut stalks.

The Green.

I stand up in the middle of a harvesting row, bamboo towering a hundred meters on either side, stars overhead in constellations no one on Earth has ever named. Behind me, the little ragged door hangs in the air, glowing faintly, with Bethany's worried face on the other side of it. Then it shrinks to a point, and goes out.

I check my compass. North. Pinnacle's pale crown of lights is just visible over the stalks, fourteen kilometers south.

I start walking.

---

Brask is asleep beside the turbine when I get there, just before dawn, exactly as I pictured him: curled up on a pile of sacks in the turbine hall with a blanket over his shoulders and a spanner in his hand. The turbine is spinning. The farm's windows glow. Eight floors, all lit.

I nudge him with my foot.

He opens his eyes. He looks at me for a long moment, as if I might be a dream brought on by too much machine oil.

Then he gets up and hugs me.

I allow it. It's been a long walk.

*You said a different door,* he says into my shoulder. *I didn't believe you.*

"I always keep my promises," I say. "It's one of my many virtues. How's the farm?"

He steps back and tells me, quickly, the way you'd report to a general. The turbine never stopped. The farm printed every day. He paid the crews from the vault and sent the tithe up the thousand and eight stairs every week, exactly on time. The Lady asked where I was. He told her I'd gone to fetch more light.

*She didn't like that,* he says. *She sends Oren every week to ask again.*

"Good. Let her wonder." I'm already walking toward the Rune Shed. "Brask, I need every mote you can carry, a ring of ferrofluid rigs, and a wagon. We're going to Sable's fields."

---

It takes a day to build the ring and haul it north. Twelve ferrofluid rigs, set in a circle four meters across around the spot in the bamboo where I came through, each one showing a feeding rune: long push bars angled inward at the rim, a hollow at the center of each. Behind them, on the wagon, sit nine thousand motes in trays. Brask's crew guards the perimeter with lanterns and nervous faces. They're in House Sable's field without Sable's permission, and they know exactly what that means.

At midnight, I pick up the radio.

"Bethany."

Her voice comes back clear. Signals pass through. *I'm here. Flywheels at full.*

"On three. Full sequence."

*On three.*

"One. Two. *Three.*"

On the far side of the universe, twelve flywheels scream. In front of me, the point of light blooms out of nothing. And on this side, Brask throws the switch, and the magnets under the ferrofluid rise, and twelve feeding runes begin to drink from nine thousand motes at once.

The point becomes a circle. The circle grows. Sixty centimeters. A meter. Two. The runes glow at the rim, pulling the door wider the way you'd pull a curtain, and the trays of motes on the wagon go dark row by row. Three meters. Four.

Four point two meters, and it stops.

Not because it's sagging. Because it's *settled.* The edge stops shimmering and starts humming, the same steady, indifferent hum the old portal always had, like something that intends to be here for a long time.

Through it, I can see the hangar. The flywheels. The resonator. Billy. And Bethany, standing in front of all of it with my coat in her arms, staring at me through a door wider than the one I blew up.

I walk through it standing up.

"Four point two meters," I tell her. "Six hundred millimeters wider than the old one. Trucks will fit."

She hands me my coat without a word. I put it on. It feels like armor coming home.

---

The door is open. The motes are flowing. And the bottleneck on everything, again, is me.

The farm needs twenty-two more floors. The second farm needs thirty. Every floor needs two hundred beams lifted, set and welded, and I can lift perhaps sixty beams a day before my head starts to split and my welds start to wander. At that rate, the first farm is finished in about eleven weeks, the second a year after that, and the forty-one cities of the Green get their lights sometime after I'm dead, which will not be for a very long time but is still a deadline.

I've built a rune motor. I've built runes that switch on and off. What I need now is a rune that builds.

Not a person with a mote. A machine that lifts a two-tonne beam, carries it ten meters up, holds it in exactly the right place, and doesn't get a headache.

*So build a crane,* Brask says. *With a rune motor on the winch.*

"A crane swings," I say. "And a winch is slow. But a push rune doesn't need cables. It can hold a beam in the air from underneath, and put it anywhere. It's the perfect actuator." I pause. "It's just very stupid."

---

The first rune lifter is a steel platform the size of a dining table, with a push rune on each of its four corners, pointed down. Each rune is a ferrofluid tray over a magnet grid, fed by a small magazine of motes that clicks a fresh one into range whenever the last one dims. On top of the platform sits a two-tonne beam. Around the edges: laser rangefinders, a tilt sensor, strain gauges. A computer in a steel box.

The first time I switch it on, I tell the computer to lift the beam to four meters and hold it there.

The runes fire. The platform leaps upward, far too fast. It shoots past four meters to six. The computer panics and switches the runes off. The platform falls. The computer panics again and switches them all back on. The platform leaps up.

For about fifteen seconds, I watch a two-tonne steel beam bounce up and down in the air like a yo-yo on a string. Brask throws himself flat on the floor. Bethany, safely behind a concrete block, films it on her phone.

I hit the kill switch. The platform crashes down on its landing struts with a sound like a car accident.

"Well," says Bethany. "It lifted it."

"It *oscillated.*" I'm already sitting down with a notebook. "Classic underdamped response. The controller overcorrects, and the delay makes it worse. This isn't a magic problem, Bethany. It's a *control* problem."

"Is that better?"

"It's *much* better. Control problems have been solved."

---

I spend the next three days doing the most enjoyable engineering of my life.

First: find out what the system actually does. I call it system identification, and Brask calls it poking the platform with a stick, and we're both right. I give the runes a small, sudden command and measure exactly how the platform responds. I do it a hundred times, at different loads and different heights.

The ferrofluid takes about eighty milliseconds to settle into its new shape. The rune takes another thirty to start drinking. The push builds up smoothly after that. And, critically, the height of the relief matters. Tall ridges push harder than short ones. The magnets can raise the fluid anywhere from flat to full height, which means the rune isn't just on or off.

It's a dial.

That changes everything. A switch can only slam. A dial can steer.

Second: build a model. A set of equations that predicts where the platform will go when I turn the dial, delay and all. The computer can test ten thousand controllers against the model in a second without ever dropping a beam on anyone.

Third: design the controller. It reads the rangefinders five hundred times a second and asks three questions. How far am I from where I should be? How long have I been wrong? How fast am I moving? Then it turns each dial by exactly the right amount. Plus a feedforward term that pushes up with exactly the weight of the beam before the error even starts, so the controller only has to correct the little things.

Fourth: tune it. Gently. Too aggressive and it bounces. Too timid and it sags. Bethany, who has never taken a controls class in her life, turns out to be very good at this. She says it's like titrating a drug dose. You're trying to get to the effect without the side effects.

On the fourth morning, I tell the platform to lift the beam to ten meters and hold it.

It rises. Smoothly. It slows as it approaches ten meters, like a lift arriving at its floor, and it stops. And it stays there, a two-tonne beam floating in the air on four little black ridges of oil, quivering by less than two millimeters.

I walk underneath it. I'm not afraid. I wrote the controller.

Brask gets up off the floor, where he's been lying as a precaution since the yo-yo. *It's holding,* he says, awed. *With nobody holding it.*

"Everybody's holding it," I say. "Pythagoras, Newton, Maxwell, a man named Nyquist. It's the most crowded beam in the world."

---

After that, the machines multiply.

The lifters get smaller and there are more of them: a swarm of twenty platforms, each carrying a beam to its place, coordinated by a scheduling program that reads the building plans and works out the order of every lift. A beam goes up; the next platform is already waiting beneath it.

The welders are heat runes on little tracked crawlers that run along each seam. A thermocouple rides just behind the rune, measuring the metal. If the bead gets too hot, the controller lowers the ridges. Too cold, it raises them. Every weld comes out the same, perfect bead, thin and even, better than my ninth attempt with my mind.

The surveyors are Billy's drones, flying LiDAR over the site every hour, comparing what's there with what should be there and flagging every beam that's more than three millimeters out of place. There are very few.

The floors go up at one every three days. Then one every two. Day and night, rain or clear. The lifters don't get headaches. The welders don't get bored. And none of them wait for me.

On the ninth week, I walk out onto the roof of the thirtieth floor of the first farm, finished, gleaming, humming with light and life and death on every level beneath my feet. I stand there in the wind and look out over the bamboo, and realize I haven't lifted a single beam myself in a month.

I've been removed from the critical path.

For most people, that would be a demotion. For me, it's the best news of the year. Because it means the only thing limiting how fast I can build is how many motes I have to spend.

And I have a machine that makes motes.

"The farms build the machines," I say to Bethany, who has come up to join me. "The machines build more farms. More farms make more motes, which build more machines."

She hears it before I say it. "That's exponential."

"It's *compound interest,*" I say. "The most powerful force in the universe. Einstein said so, allegedly, and he was wrong about almost nothing except quantum mechanics."

---

Of course, the door is in House Sable's field. That was always going to be a conversation.

Sable's harvesters find it on the third day: a ring of strange black-oil machines around a humming hole in the air, with a road of crushed husk-wood already running from it toward Pinnacle, and trucks from another world rolling along it at night. By the fourth day, an envoy is waiting at the door with twenty guards, and by the fifth, I'm climbing the stairs of House Sable's tower. Nine hundred and forty of them. Fewer than Verdane's. I make a note of it.

The Lord of Sable is young for a House head, perhaps thirty-five, with sharp cheekbones, a sharper smile, and the particular restlessness of a man who inherited a House that is losing. Verdane's lower city has been lit for half a year. Sable's is still dark. His people have noticed, and his people have been crossing into Verdane's quarters to buy batteries, and every mote they spend there is a mote that leaves his city.

*You put a door in my field,* he says, before I've even sat down.

"I did."

*Without my leave.*

"Correct."

*I could have your door walled in and your road dug up.*

"You could," I agree. "And your lower city would stay dark, and your people would keep walking to Verdane to buy light, and the Lady of Verdane would keep getting richer while you watched." I cross my legs. "Or you could have the same deal she has, today. Lamps in every quarter of Sable, under your seal. One mote in ten of everything I sell here."

His eyes narrow. *You offered two in ten, once. Through my envoy.*

"I did. That was before I had a door in your field and a lit city next door. Prices change." I smile. "One in ten. The land around the door, two hectares, as my enclave. A harvest license on the same ground. Rights to the husk-wood from your fields. And your guards stay off my road."

He's quiet for a long time. I can see him doing arithmetic too. He's better at it than the Lady. Which will matter later, but not today.

*And if I say yes,* he says finally, *Verdane will say I bent the knee to her pet.*

"Then don't bend the knee." I stand. "Shake the hand. I don't have pets. I have partners, and none of them outrank each other."

He takes my hand. His grip is firm, and he watches my face while he shakes it, the way Nkemelu did. I like him less than I'd like to.

---

The second farm goes up in Sable's field, beside the door I've started calling the Sable Gate. The rune lifters build it in five weeks. Thirty floors of yeast, larvae, fungus and duckweed, glowing like a lantern in the green, fed by husk-wood from a thousand hectares of Sable's fields. A second turbine arrives from Earth, through the Gate, in pieces a full six hundred millimeters wider than they used to be allowed. Then a third.

House Ochre's Lady, who has watched both her neighbors light up, sends an envoy with a gift of honey and a question she doesn't need to finish. The third farm starts in Ochre's field the following month.

By the end of that season, the numbers look like this:

|  | Motes per day |
| --- | --- |
| Farm one (Verdane) | 61,000 |
| Farm two (Sable) | 48,000 |
| Farm three (Ochre, 12 floors) | 17,000 |
| **Total** | **126,000** |

Swap stations in all three Houses' lower quarters: sixty-one of them. Subscribing households: forty-four thousand. Every window in the lower city of Pinnacle, all the way up the dark stone canyons, glows white at night.

And one mote in ten of everything, every day, goes up three different staircases.

I notice something about that, in the evenings, doing my accounts. Before I arrived, the Houses' income came from their fields and their licensed butchers. Now more than half of each House's new money comes from me. Twelve thousand motes a day, going up the stairs, keeping three Houses rich in a way they can't earn without me.

They think it's tribute.

It's a dependency.

---

The last thing I do that season is sign a lease, through yet another boring company, for a salt mine four hundred kilometers east. It closed in 1998. The previous owners were delighted to find someone who wanted it. They asked what we'd store there.

"Data," I told them.

It's two hundred meters underground. Dry, stable, enormous, and very quiet. And directly above the cliffs of Ruler's Reach, one universe over, where no House has ever held a stake, and never will.

---

The bunker's command room still has a Cold War projector: a heavy gray box on a cart with a fan that sounds like a small aircraft. I had it serviced. Some things are worth doing properly.

It's late. The flywheels hum in the hangar. Beyond the Sable Gate, three farms glow in the bamboo, printing a hundred and twenty-six thousand motes a day. And for the first time since the night of the badge photo, I have time to think about everyone else.

I dim the lights. Bethany, who has learned to recognize the sound of a projector warming up, sighs and sits down with her tea.

---

LADY AND LEDGER

*presented by ANGLINE RULER*

---

"Lady and Ledger," I begin. "Today we will be covering: Accounts Receivable. Please save your questions for the end."

"Is this a revenge list?"

"It's a *ledger.* Revenge is emotional. This is bookkeeping." I click. "People have wronged me, Bethany. Debts have been incurred. And a debt that isn't collected isn't a debt. It's a gift. I don't give gifts to people who've wronged me."

Click.

The first slide is a photograph of a very large government building.

"The government. Who refused me a spy satellite for personal use. Who took eleven months to approve a reactor I designed in three days. Who sent a colonel to arrest me, and who is now, I assume, trying to work out how my glove works." I pause. "They wouldn't give me a satellite, so I'll build my own. In the Green, nobody controls the sky. Nobody tracks launches. There's no airspace, no treaty, no committee. I'll put up one satellite, then ten, over both worlds. And one day that government will look up and find it's being watched by the person it said no to."

"And Rourke?"

"Colonel Rourke wanted a door." I smile. "One day, I'll open one for him. In his office. At the time of my choosing. And he can watch me walk through it."

Click.

Three towers, photographed by drone against the evening sky: Verdane, Sable, Ochre.

"The Houses. Who believe I answer to them. Who threatened to hold me, and made me climb their stairs, and own the right to make money in a world where I make more of it than all of them together." I click to a chart: House income over the season, with my tithe climbing through it like a vine. "They're dependent on me already. More every month. One day, when they need me more than they need their own fields, the tithe will stop. My electricity will be cheaper than their motes. And somewhere in an encrypted file, there's a rune that can drink a treasury from outside the walls." I let that sit. "They won't fight me. You can't fight the thing that keeps your lights on."

Click.

The next slide is two columns. On the left, in red: DEBTS. On the right, in green: CREDIT.

"Not everyone's a debtor," I explain. "Detective Inspector Nkemelu," I tap the green column. "She did her job, and did it well. She's the only person who's ever checked Helsinki. People like that don't get punished. They get *hired,* eventually. Keith, the auditor: same column. Integrity is rare. You collect it. The loan officer: he gave me money when I needed it. He'll be paid back with interest, early."

"And Gerald?"

I click. The slide is just Gerald's name, in gray.

"Gerald handed Rourke the key to sub-basement four." I consider it. "Gerald gets nothing. Gerald's punishment is being Gerald."

Bethany laughs into her tea. I let her. It's a good line.

Click.

The next slide is the logo of a national news channel.

"The broadcaster," I say, very evenly, "that showed my security badge photo to eleven million people. Taken six years ago, under fluorescent light, on a morning when I had not slept."

"Angline—"

"I'm going to buy it. The whole channel. Through a very boring company. Then I'm going to find the person who chose that photo and fire them. Then I'm going to hire them back, at twice the salary, with a single job description. *Choose better photos.*"

"That's insane."

"It's *proportionate.*"

Click.

---

The last slide is one I made years ago, in the old lab, the day before I shot Alphonseo. Hand-drawn, scanned, a little crooked. A rough diagram titled THE PLAN, mostly arrows, and a single bullet point.

WIN.

But I've been adding to it. The arrows go further now. They branch. They run from a bunker to a farm, from a farm to a salt mine, from a salt mine to a bay, from a bay to forty-one cities, from those cities up into the sky. And at the end of every one of them, circled twice, the same word.

"None of this happens quickly," I tell Bethany. "That's important. Hasty revenge is how villains lose. They get angry, they move too early, they make a scene, and a dashing young hero comes and ruins it." I switch off the projector. The fan spins down. "I'm not going to fight anybody. A battle means both sides have a chance. I'm going to be a tide. You don't fight a tide. You don't even notice it's coming in, until the water's around your ankles, and then your knees, and then it's much too late to be anywhere else."

The command room is quiet. The flywheels hum through the walls.

"Great plan, Ms. Ruler," Bethany says finally. "Great plan."

"I know." I pause. "Which column do you think you're in?"

She looks at the blank screen. She looks at me.

"Neither," she says. "I'm not on the ledger. I'm on the slides."

I consider that. I find, somewhat to my surprise, that I agree with it completely.

"Staff," I say.

"*Senior* staff."

"Don't make it weird."

---

Before bed, I make one last phone call, from a new number, through a very boring company.

It's to the best portrait photographer in Europe. She's booked for the next fourteen months. I pay her to clear a day, a year from now, at double her rate, somewhere undisclosed, subject unnamed.

When they have to put my face on the news again, and they will, it's going to be a good one.

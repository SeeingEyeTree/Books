# Chapter 9: Resignation

The lab is on the fifth floor. Sub-basement four is nine flights below it. I take the stairs.

Elevators are for people who don't have to be anywhere, and also for people who don't need two minutes to think. I have somewhere to be, and I need exactly two minutes.

Flight one. The options.

Option one: I go down, I raise my hands, and I let Colonel Rourke take me into custody. I call a lawyer from the car. Not a good lawyer. The best one, the kind who bills in units of small nations. The portal becomes a matter of national security, which means secrecy, which means no public trial. Alphonseo has no body. The lava pool is a thermal reservoir. Nkemelu has an email from the wrong IP address and a car with five months of parking tickets, and that's all she has. In two years, maybe three, I walk out.

Flight two. And in those two or three years, the portal belongs to Rourke. Soldiers drive through it. The farm, Brask, the vault, the swap stations, and a boy reading by a lamp he pays for every week: all of it becomes a military site, run by a man with three flags in his office who has never once built anything. And the Lady of House Verdane meets the Earth for the first time through a rifle.

Flight three. Unacceptable.

Option two: I go down and I tell the truth. Some of it. Enough to make Rourke need me more than he wants to arrest me. It works, maybe. But it makes me his asset, and assets don't get to decide anything. I would answer to him, for real this time.

Flight four. Also unacceptable.

Option three.

I stop on the landing between the ground floor and sub-basement one, and I think about the load-bearing walls. Rourke's belief. Halvorsen's. Keith's. Gerald's. I built my whole Earth life on people believing I answered to them, and tonight those walls are coming down whether I knock them out or not.

The new structure has been standing for months. On the other side of a shimmering circle. It's eight floors tall with twenty-two more to come, it prints eleven thousand motes a day, and it doesn't need any of these people at all.

It's time to stop pretending.

---

Flight five. I check my coat.

Like any good European who discovers a new source of power, the first thing I built with it was a weapon. That's not a criticism. That's history. Every energy source humanity has ever found, we have first pointed at each other and only later at the kettle. I merely skipped the centuries in between.

My right glove is lined with a thin steel palm plate, and milled into that plate, to a tenth of a millimeter, is a push rune. Six bars fanned outward from a single hollow. Clipped to the wrist is a small sprung slide that holds a mote a finger's width too far from the hollow to drink. Push the slide with my thumb, and the mote clicks into reach. Let go, and it springs back out.

It's a switch. The gate guard would love it.

On Earth, every mote is taxed ninety percent. A mote gives me a hundred kilojoules, and force converts about a quarter of that into push. Twenty-five kilojoules. For comparison, a rifle bullet carries about three. The glove is far weaker here than it would be in the Green.

It's still enough to put a grown man through a wall.

In the left inside pocket, four heat discs, steel pucks the size of a coaster, each with a spiral rune on one face and a mote held off it by a pin. Pull the pin and the mote snaps in, and the disc dumps everything it has into heat in about two seconds. On Earth, not enough to melt a vehicle. Enough to make one very unpleasant to stand next to.

In the right inside pocket, a tray of twenty spare motes.

In the lower pockets, as always, C4, with timers and detonators, because I bring explosives everywhere and sub-basement four is not special.

Flight six. I straighten my coat.

Flight seven. I check my hair in the reflection of the fire extinguisher cabinet. It's perfect. It takes effort to look effortless, and tonight of all nights I'm not going to be arrested with a strand out of place.

Flight eight.

Flight nine.

I push open the door to sub-basement four.

---

Sub-basement four is brighter than I've ever seen it. Someone has brought in floodlights on stands, and they throw hard white light across the concrete and turn the portal's shimmer into something almost solid.

Colonel Rourke stands in front of it with his hands behind his back. Four soldiers are spread in a loose half-circle, rifles lowered. Gerald hovers by the door with his clipboard pressed to his chest like a shield, looking like a man who has just realized what his key opened.

The trailer is still there, half in and half out of the portal, its front end simply gone.

Bethany stands beside it with her hands on her head. When she sees me, her shoulders drop about three centimeters. It's the most relieved I've ever seen anyone look at me.

"Dr. Ruler," says Rourke.

"Colonel."

"You told me it was inert."

"I did."

"You told me it was construction rubble."

"Technically, I told you we'd *filed* it as construction rubble. Which we had." I walk forward, slowly, into the floodlights. Two of the rifles come up a few degrees. "You never came down to check, Colonel. In five months. I want you to know I noticed."

His jaw tightens. "Where does it go?"

"Somewhere else."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." I stop beside Bethany. "Put your hands down. You look like a hostage."

"I *am* a hostage," she says, but she puts them down.

Rourke takes a step toward me. "Dr. Ruler, you are going to explain to me, in detail, what that is, where it goes, what you've been moving through it, and why a detective was in this building this morning asking about your missing supervisor. Then you're going to come with me."

I consider him for a moment. He's used to being answered. You can see it in the way he stands. Men like him think that authority is a property of the person, like height, rather than a belief held by other people, like money.

"No," I say.

A pause.

"I beg your pardon?"

"No to all of it. I resign." I reach into my coat, and the rifles come all the way up, and I take out a folded sheet of paper and hand it to Gerald. "My resignation letter, effective immediately. And your variance, Gerald. For the thermal reservoir. Signed."

Gerald takes it with shaking hands. It's the happiest I've ever seen him.

"Bethany and I are leaving now," I tell the colonel. "I'm not going to elaborate. I'd advise you not to stop us."

Rourke looks at me for a long moment. Then, very quietly, to his soldiers: "Detain them both."

---

The nearest soldier is two meters away. He reaches for my arm.

I put my right palm flat against his chest armor, and I push the slide with my thumb.

The mote clicks into the hollow. The rune drinks. And the soldier leaves the floor. He travels four meters backward, not falling but *flying*, the way a coat flies off a hook, and hits the concrete wall with a sound like a dropped mattress. He slides down it and sits there, stunned, blinking, entirely alive.

Contact is cheap. I've never been so glad to be right about something.

The second soldier shouts and raises his rifle. I don't need to touch him; I just need to touch the gun. I hold my gloved hand out toward it and push again, farther this time, more expensive. The rifle tears out of his grip and cartwheels over his head into the dark behind the floodlights. He stares at his empty hands.

The third soldier fires.

I don't know if he meant to. I don't know if he was aiming at me or at the ceiling or at nothing. The shot is shockingly loud in the concrete room, and something hot passes close enough to my left ear that I feel it move my hair.

My hair, which I did this morning.

I pull the pin on a heat disc and roll it across the floor. It stops at his boots, and for two seconds it glows cherry red, and the concrete around it cracks and hisses. He and the fourth soldier stumble back from it, shielding their faces, and by the time the glare fades, I'm standing close enough to put my palm flat on both of their rifles, one after the other, and twist.

The barrels bend.

It's very quiet after that. Four soldiers, one sitting against a wall, one staring at his empty hands, two holding rifles shaped like question marks. Nobody dead. I planned it that way. Dead soldiers make martyrs. Bruised ones make paperwork.

Rourke hasn't moved. He's looking at my glove.

"What *is* that?"

"A glove." I flex it. "The next part of your career, if you want it. Somebody's going to spend the next ten years trying to work out how it works. It might as well be you."

---

I turn my back on him. It's a calculated insult and I enjoy it enormously.

"Bethany. The trailer."

She runs to the back of it and pulls the hitch pin. I put both hands flat on the trailer's rear door, thumb the slide, and push. The trailer rolls forward, slowly, into the shimmer, and disappears into the Green: a full load of LEDs delivered to the far side, where they belong.

Then the fiber terminal on the wall beside the portal. I type fast.

*BRASK. DOOR CLOSING. NOT MY CHOICE. KEEP THE TURBINE SPINNING, KEEP THE FARM RUNNING, KEEP THE LADY HAPPY. PAY THE CREWS FROM THE VAULT. I WILL COME BACK THROUGH A DIFFERENT DOOR. — A.R.*

I hit send and watch the little light blink green. Message delivered. Somewhere on the other side, it's the middle of the night, and an old man is asleep beside a humming turbine. He'll read it in the morning.

Then the C4.

I set four charges: two on the walls, one on the floor, one on the gray metal box bolted to the ceiling above the portal. The box is labeled, in Gerald's handwriting, VENTILATION IMPROVEMENT, UNIT 4. It isn't. It's the key. A field resonator I built, over a long weekend, and filed under ventilation because nobody ever looks up.

I set the timers. Three minutes.

"Everyone out," I say, loudly. "This room will cease to exist in three minutes. Up the stairs, all of you. Colonel, that includes you. Gerald, especially you."

"You're *blowing it up?*" Rourke says.

"I monologue responsibly," I say. "I also detonate responsibly. You have two minutes and fifty seconds."

They go. The soldiers help their dazed friend up the stairs, and Gerald goes first, clutching the variance. Rourke is the last out the door. He stops there, and turns, and looks at me across the empty, floodlit room.

"They'll hunt you," he says. "You know that. Everyone. Everywhere."

"Colonel," I say, "I've been underestimated my whole life. Being hunted will make a nice change."

He goes.

---

I stand in front of the portal one last time.

Five months. Seventeen days of it filed as rubble, and every day after that, a door. I've driven through it, walked through it, eaten sandwiches beside it, measured it with a tape. It's humming softly, as it always has, indifferent to all of it.

I reach up to the resonator and turn it off.

The portal shrinks.

Not quickly. The shimmer draws inward from the edges, like a pupil in bright light: three meters, two, one. The fiber-optic cable running through it goes taut, then snaps with a small, clean sound, cut through by the closing edge. The circle becomes a point of light, hanging in the air at head height.

Then it's gone, and sub-basement four is just a concrete room with floodlights in it.

One minute forty.

"Angline," says Bethany from the doorway to the ramp, in a voice I've never heard her use. "*Now.*"

I go.

---

Billy is waiting in the loading bay in sub-basement two, where he always waits between trips, broad and blunt-nosed and patient. We're inside with the hatches sealed in forty seconds. I take the driver's seat. Bethany takes the passenger seat and puts on her seatbelt, which in an armored personnel carrier is mostly a gesture of hope.

We're halfway up the exit ramp when the charges go.

It's not loud, from here. It's a thump more than a bang, felt through the floor and up the spine, as if the building has hiccuped. Dust sifts down from the ramp's ceiling. Somewhere above us, every fire alarm in the building begins to scream at once.

Sub-basement four no longer exists. Neither does the key. Neither does any evidence that a door ever stood there, except in the memories of four soldiers, a colonel, and Gerald, whose account of events will, I suspect, be focused mainly on the variance.

Billy noses out of the loading dock and into the street.

It's 10:20 on a Monday night. There's a bus stop across the road with two people at it, and they both turn slowly to watch a flat grey-green armored vehicle with a 35mm autocannon roll past them at a stately forty kilometers an hour. One of them takes out a phone.

"They're filming us," Bethany says.

"Good. The footage will be very confusing later."

"You can't drive an APC through a city! Everyone in the country is going to be looking for an APC!"

"Then we'll stop being in the city," I say, and turn left, toward the river.

She realizes what I'm doing about two seconds before we reach the embankment. "Angline. *No.*"

"Billy is amphibious, Bethany. I told you that the day we met."

"You told me that in *a different universe!*"

We go over the embankment, through a gap in the railing I measured three years ago for exactly this reason, and down the concrete boat ramp, and into the black water.

For a moment, the windows are full of bubbles. Then there's nothing but dark, and the soft green glow of the instruments, and the hum of the reactor as Billy seals, trims, and settles into the current at a depth of four meters.

Above us, the sirens pass over the bridge. None of them stop.

Bethany lets out a breath she seems to have been holding since sub-basement four. For a long time, she doesn't say anything. Then:

"We're fugitives."

"We're *independent,*" I correct her.

"Everyone we know is going to think we're criminals."

"I did shoot my boss. And you helped put him in lava. We *are* criminals. The difference is that until tonight, it was a secret." I adjust the heading upstream. "Honesty is freeing. I recommend it."

She looks at me for a long moment in the green glow.

"You came down," she says. "You could have run. You were five floors up. You could have left me there."

"You're my biology lead."

"That's not—"

"You run the research loop. You found the rune grammar. You found the yeast." I keep my eyes on the sonar. "Operative B0 was disposable, Bethany. You haven't been Operative B0 for a long time."

She doesn't answer. After a while, I notice she's stopped holding the seatbelt quite so tightly.

---

We travel upstream for four hours, slow and deep, beneath three bridges, a weir, and a very startled late-night rowing crew. Twenty-two kilometers outside the city, where the river bends through wooded hills, I bring Billy up beside a disused quarry landing and drive him up the bank, streaming water, into the trees.

The road through the woods leads to a steel gate with a faded sign that says FERNHOLT DATA STORAGE — PRIVATE. Behind the gate, set into the side of a hill, is a concrete portal with a pair of blast doors three meters tall.

"What is this?" Bethany asks.

"A Cold War command bunker. Built for a war that never came. Decommissioned in the nineties." I tap the code into the keypad. "I bought it three years ago, through a company that owns a company that owns a data storage business."

"*Why?*"

"I always play with a stacked deck," I say. "Billy was my ace. This is my king."

The blast doors grind open. Behind them, a long concrete tunnel slopes down into the hill, lit by caged bulbs, wide enough for Billy with room to spare. The air smells like cold stone and old diesel. There's a real ventilation system down here. I checked. For once, the word means what it says.

We drive in. The doors close behind us with a boom that I feel in my teeth.

The tunnel opens into a chamber the size of a small aircraft hangar, and in the middle of the chamber, under a tarp, is a stack of pallets.

Bethany pulls the tarp back.

Silver. Bars of it, rough and gray and stamped with the cartographer's mark, stacked on pallets in rows. The first bars of a trade loop that started quietly while she was busy counting larvae.

"How much?" she whispers.

"Six hundred and forty kilograms," I say. "Bought in Pinnacle at about forty motes a kilo. Carried back in the trailers under the LED cartons. Stored here, because I didn't trust any building with a Gerald in it." I run my hand along the top bar. "Tomorrow morning, every account in my name will be frozen. This won't be."

She looks at the silver. Then at the bunker. Then at me.

"You planned for this."

"I planned for *something,*" I say. "I just didn't know it would be tonight."

---

I don't sleep. I don't need to. I have a problem, and problems are more restful than sleep.

The bunker's old command room still has its wall-sized map board, a relic from when men in uniforms moved little flags around to plan the end of the world. I tear down the faded Cold War map and pin up two of my own, side by side: a survey map of the country around us, and the LiDAR map Billy's drones built of the Green around Pinnacle, twenty kilometers in every direction.

The problem, stated simply: the only door is gone. On the other side of it are a farm, a turbine, a vault of motes, an old man, and a House that thinks I answer to it. On this side are two fugitives, an APC, a pile of silver, and thirteen motes, which on Earth are worth roughly thirteen cups of tea.

I need a new door. Which raises the question of where it would open.

"You don't know?" Bethany says, handing me a coffee from the bunker's ancient kitchen. It tastes like 1983.

"I have a hypothesis."

I've had it for months. On our second week in the Green, I set up the theodolite at the portal and measured the sun at noon. Its elevation matched what it would have been in the car park outside the lab, on the same date, to within a tenth of a degree. Same latitude. The days were the same length. Brask's lodestone points the same way my compass does. And the Green's gravity, measured properly with the instruments rather than magic, is Earth's to four decimal places.

"It's the same planet," I say. "Or the same shape of planet, the same size, spinning the same way. Different everything on the surface, same bones underneath. Which means every point on Earth has a twin in the Green, at the same latitude and the same longitude."

"One to one."

"One to one. The old portal stood under the lab, and it opened in the bamboo at the same coordinates. If I open a door here—" I tap the bunker on the Earth map, "—it opens at the same coordinates over there."

I take a ruler and a protractor. The bunker is 22.4 kilometers from the lab, on a bearing of fifty-one degrees. I lay the same line on the Green map, from the old portal, at the same angle, the same distance.

It lands in the bamboo, fourteen kilometers north of Pinnacle. The LiDAR shows open rows, a harvesting road nearby, and a boundary marker on the map that Oren once pointed out to me.

House Sable's fields.

"Of course," I say.

"Is that bad?"

"It's *politics.* Politics is always bad, but it can be managed." I circle it. "Sable wanted a charter. Sable is about to get a door in its field and a very interesting offer."

---

The next question is how.

I take the pencil and draw the key: the resonator that's now a scorch mark on the ceiling of sub-basement four. I've never explained it to Bethany. I've never explained it to anyone.

"The orphanage portal was already there when they dug it up," I tell her. "Dormant. A thin place, a door that wanted to exist but couldn't quite open. The resonator pushed it the rest of the way. It took the building's whole substation for four seconds, at two in the morning on a long weekend, and Gerald never noticed because I'd filed a form in advance saying we were testing ventilation."

"And here? Is there a thin place under this hill?"

"No idea. Probably not." I flip back through my notebook to the curve I drew beside the portal months ago: the portal-feeding test, the motes-per-centimeter curve that ran off the edge of the page. "Opening a door where none wants to exist costs far more. Maybe a hundred times more. Maybe a thousand. I don't know, because nobody has ever done it."

"So how much power do you need?"

"Somewhere between forty and four hundred megawatts," I say, "for somewhere between one second and ten minutes."

She stares at me. "That's a *huge* range."

"It's an *honest* range. Engineering is mostly admitting how little you know and then building for the worst case." I draw a box next to the key. "Billy's reactor makes one megawatt. Not remotely enough. But it doesn't need to deliver it all at once. It can charge something that does."

I draw it. A row of flywheels, each one a heavy steel rotor spinning in a vacuum chamber on magnetic bearings, twenty thousand revolutions a minute. Billy spins them up at his steady megawatt. Then, when the key needs its pulse, the flywheels dump everything back through their generators in a single roar.

"Spinning magnets," Bethany says, in the tone of someone who has seen this coming for months.

"The *best* spinning magnets," I say. "Magnets that spin so they can store the spin for later. It's spinning magnets all the way down."

---

The last question is supply.

By tomorrow, my name will be on every watch list in Europe. Janis in Latvia will not be taking my calls. The Shenzhen factory will be told by someone that Angline Ruler is not a customer anymore. Every trailer I ordered, every carton of LEDs, every piece of copper is going to be tracked from now on.

So I won't be buying anything.

Fernholt Data Storage will.

That's the beauty of a data center. It needs everything I need, and nobody ever asks why. Backup power: turbines, generators, flywheels. Cooling: pumps, piping, heat exchangers. Racks of batteries. Kilometers of cable. Enormous deliveries at strange hours to a site in the woods with blast doors and no windows. Data centers are *supposed* to look suspicious.

I start a list on the map board, in the margin, where the Cold War generals kept their notes.

*FERNHOLT DATA STORAGE — PROCUREMENT*

*Flywheel units, grid-storage class: 12.*

*Steam turbines, 5 MW, secondhand: 3. For the Green. One farm each.*

*Copper, cable, switchgear: lots.*

*LEDs, "server status indicators": 400,000.*

*Batteries, "UPS backup": all of them.*

*A silver dealer who doesn't ask questions: 1.*

"You can pay for all that with six hundred kilos of silver?" Bethany asks.

"No. I can pay the *deposits.* Then the first door opens, and the vault is on the other side, and the farm is printing, and the silver loop starts again." I underline the last line. "The trick is surviving until the door opens."

She looks at the map. At the pencil line running northeast from a scorch mark to a bunker, and northeast again from an old portal to a field in House Sable's bamboo.

"And after that?"

I take the ruler again. I lay it from the bunker eastward across the Earth map, four hundred kilometers, to a spot in the hills where, according to the geological survey, there's a salt mine that closed in 1998. Two hundred meters underground, dry, stable, enormous, and very quiet.

Then I lay the same line across the Green.

It lands on the cliffs above Ruler's Reach.

"Two doors," I say. "One by the farm. One by the sea. Both underground, where nobody on Earth will ever look." I put the ruler down. "And one day, Bethany, a door anywhere I like."

---

At six in the morning, the bunker's old television finds a news channel through the static.

The explosion is the top story. The building, the smoke, the fire engines. Then a clip from the bus stop: Billy rolling past, grainy and serious. Then the anchor's voice says *police wish to speak to two women in connection with the incident and the disappearance of a senior scientist,* and our photos come up on the screen.

Bethany's is a holiday photo, smiling, clearly taken from her social media. She looks lovely.

Mine is my security badge photo. Taken six years ago. Under fluorescent lights. On a morning when I hadn't slept.

I stare at it for a long, silent moment.

"Angline," Bethany says carefully. "We're wanted by the police. For murder. And terrorism, probably."

"I know."

"You look *upset.*"

"They used my *badge photo,*" I say. "Of all the photos in the world. They used the *badge photo.*"

I turn off the television.

Then I go back to the map board and get to work, because the fastest way to get a better photo in the news is to become someone they have to photograph properly.

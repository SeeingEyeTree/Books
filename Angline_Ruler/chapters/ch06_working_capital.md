# Chapter 6: Working Capital

I am in the lab by six on Monday. Nobody asks why, because I'm always in the lab by six. Consistency is the best disguise.

The room still smells faintly of what happened in the lava pool on Saturday. I turn the extraction fans up and put on coffee. Then I set out the tools of the most important experiment of my career: an insulated flask, a liter of tap water, a digital thermometer, a scale, and one mote.

I want to explain something about myself, since it will matter later.

Every limit I have ever run into has been a budget. Not physics. Physics has never once told me no; it only tells me *how.* The people who tell me no are the ones holding the money. The government that wouldn't give me a spy satellite for "personal use." The committee that took eleven months to approve a reactor I designed in three days. The facilities department, which I have learned to lie to in the language of ventilation. Every no in my life has come down to someone else deciding what my time and my ideas are worth, and then deciding it in units they control.

A mote is a unit nobody controls yet.

It's energy you can hold in your hand. It's money you can burn. It pushes, it heats, it lifts, and it tells you things about the world that you'd otherwise need an instrument for. A mote is the only substance I've ever encountered that is simultaneously fuel, currency and tool. Give me one, and I can do a small thing. Give me a thousand, and I can do a large thing without asking anyone.

Give me an unlimited supply, and I never have to ask anyone for anything again.

That's the goal. Not a lot of motes. *Infinite* motes. Everything else is a milestone.

But before infinity, you need a unit. So: calorimetry.

I fill the flask with exactly one liter of water at 20.0 degrees Celsius. I press the mote to the outside of the flask, the way the water-seller pressed his to the jar, and I think *heat*: not a fireball, not a flame, just the plain, boring intention of *warmer.* The warmth runs out of my hand and into the glass. When the mote goes cloudy, I read the thermometer.

44.1 degrees.

A temperature rise of about twenty-four degrees in a kilogram of water. Specific heat of water, 4.18 kilojoules per kilogram per degree. Call it a hundred kilojoules. One whole mote, on Earth, is worth a hundred kilojoules of heat.

I look at the number for a long moment.

A hundred kilojoules is about what it takes to boil a cup of tea. It is less energy than is in a single cookie.

"Morning," says Bethany from the doorway. It's 8:31. She's holding two coffees and has a new, professional-looking bandage on her hand. "Why do you look like someone died?"

"Someone did," I say. "But that's not it." I hand her the dead mote. "On Earth, one mote is a cup of tea."

"That's… bad?"

"That's *pathetic.* In the forest, I held one of these and made a ball of fire I could throw. The water-seller condensed a whole jar out of the air with a sliver of one. Either motes are wildly weaker than I thought, or—"

"Or something about Earth makes them weaker."

I look at her. She shrugs and sets a coffee in front of me.

"I'm tired," she says, "not stupid."

"Grab a flask," I say. "And your coat. We're going to go boil water in another universe."

---

We take the freight elevator down to sub-basement four with a lab cart: flasks, a ten-liter insulated drum, thermometers, a lux meter, a tape measure, two drones and a folding table. It feels like going on a picnic to the edge of the universe. I've packed sandwiches. I'm not an animal.

The portal hangs where it always hangs, humming softly, a shimmering disk in the concrete dark. I push the cart through first. Then Bethany. Then me.

The bamboo closes over us like a cathedral roof. The air is warm and green and smells of cut stalks. Somewhere far off, a bird that isn't a bird makes a noise like a creaky hinge.

I set up the table between two stalks and repeat the experiment exactly. Same thermometer. Same kind of mote. Same intention. But this time I use the ten-liter drum, because I have a hypothesis, and the good thing about a hypothesis is it tells you what size bucket to bring.

Twenty degrees to 43.8.

Ten kilograms of water, a rise of nearly twenty-four degrees. About a million joules. One megajoule, from one mote.

On Earth: a hundred kilojoules. Here: a thousand.

"Ten times," Bethany says, reading over my shoulder.

"One sample isn't a result." I hand her a mote. "Again."

She does it. Her number comes out at 9.8 times. I do a third: 10.3. Three trials, one ratio, near enough. A mote on the Green is worth ten motes on Earth. Or, as I prefer to think of it, Earth charges a ninety percent tax on magic.

"So they work *better* here," Bethany says.

"They work *properly* here. Earth is the anomaly." I write it down. *Mote, Green side: ~1 MJ heat. Earth side: ~0.1 MJ. Factor of 10.* "Every effect, same ratio. Probably. Which means we do our magic here and our manufacturing there, and we never, ever pay the tax."

---

The next test is the one I've been looking forward to. Why is light so expensive?

I hold a fresh mote against a glass bulb, the way I've seen it done in Pinnacle, and think *light.* A blue glow blooms inside it, dim and cold. I point the lux meter at it and start the stopwatch, and Bethany and I eat our sandwiches in the blue glow while it fades.

Nineteen minutes and forty seconds.

I do the math on a napkin. I integrate the light output over the time. The total energy that came out as light is barely a kilojoule.

The same mote that heated ten liters of water by twenty-four degrees gave me one kilojoule of light. A thousandth.

"It's a terrible lamp," I say. "One tenth of one percent efficient. A candle would be embarrassed."

"Why?"

"I don't know." I write it down and underline it. *Light: 0.1%. WHY?* "Heat is easy for it. Pushing is easy. Water is easy. Light is almost impossible. Maybe it's the frequency. Maybe it has to do with how it gets out of the crystal. I have no idea yet." I smile at the napkin. "I love it."

"You love not knowing?"

"I love *finding out.* Not knowing is just the part before."

Bethany looks at the fading blue glow. "So your whole business only works because magic is bad at one thing."

"Every business works because something is bad at one thing." I fold the napkin into my coat. "Candles made lamps. Lamps made bulbs. Motes are about to make me."

---

The last test is the portal itself.

I measure it first. Tape across, edge to edge: 3.6 meters. A circle of about ten square meters. Billy fit through with twenty centimeters to spare on each side, which I described at the time as "ample" and which Bethany described as "why is the car screaming."

A shipping container is 2.44 meters wide and 2.59 tall. The diagonal of its face is 3.56 meters. It would *technically* fit, in the same way that I technically fit into the dress I wore to the defense ministry gala. A truck with a trailer, a boiler, a steam turbine: absolutely not. If I'm going to move industrial quantities of anything, this door has to get bigger.

So I try feeding it.

I press a mote against the shimmering rim, and push. Not heat, not light; just *more.* The rim ripples like a pond. I measure again with the tape.

3.62 meters.

Two centimeters. It holds for about forty seconds, then relaxes back to 3.6 with a faint sigh I feel in my teeth.

"It drinks," Bethany says quietly.

"Everything here drinks." I'm already grabbing more motes. "Ten, all at once. Count the time."

Ten motes. The rim bulges outward and stays there. 3.78 meters. Eighteen centimeters, not twenty. Four minutes and ten seconds before it slides back.

I do a third run with a mote on the Earth side of the rim, just to check. A tenth of the effect, like everything else. The tax applies to doors too.

I sit on the edge of the cart and do math for a while. Bethany eats my second sandwich. I let her.

The curve is ugly. Every extra centimeter costs more than the last, and every size decays back unless you keep feeding it. To hold this door open at eight meters, wide enough for a real truck, I estimate thirty thousand motes a day, give or take a factor of two. That's nearly three times what the whole lower city of Pinnacle spends on light, burned to keep one door open.

And a new door, somewhere else, made from nothing?

I extend the curve. The error bars are enormous. But the number at the end of it is bigger than all the motes the Green could produce in a year.

"How many do we have left?" Bethany asks.

I count them. "A hundred and eighteen."

"And you need?"

I look at the portal, humming in the forest, indifferent as ever. On the other side is a whole world full of warehouses, factories, and steel mills. On this side is a world full of power. And between them, one door that's ten square meters, and a price I can't pay.

"More," I say. "Much more. Eventually, all of them."

I leave two drones perched high in the bamboo above the portal, set to watch and record anything that moves, and we wheel the cart back through to Earth.

---

There are, on Earth, a number of people who believe I answer to them. By two in the afternoon, three of them have tried to prove it.

The first is Gerald from facilities.

Gerald has a clipboard and the haunted look of a man who reads utility bills for pleasure. He finds me in the lab and holds up a printout like a subpoena.

"Dr. Ruler. The thermal installation drew a very large spike of power at four in the morning on Saturday."

"Calibration," I say. "The thermal reservoir needed a load test."

"On a *Saturday*?"

"Heat doesn't know what day it is, Gerald."

He looks through the internal window at the lava pool, which is bubbling innocently, and then back at his clipboard, and I watch him decide that he doesn't have the vocabulary to win this. He never does. It's why I chose the phrase "thermal reservoir" on the original form. Nobody argues with a noun they can't picture.

"It needs a signature," he says. "Dr. Alphonseo's. For the variance."

"Dr. Alphonseo is in Helsinki."

"Then who signs?"

---

That question gets answered at eleven, by the second person who thinks I answer to them.

Dr. Halvorsen is the director of research, which means she is Alphonseo's boss and therefore, in her mind, mine. She calls me into her office. It has a view of the car park and a plant that has been dying for as long as I've worked here, very slowly, with dignity.

"Alphonseo's extended his trip," she says, frowning at her screen. "Fjords, apparently. He's never taken a holiday in his life."

"People change," I say. "Sometimes very suddenly."

"Well, I need someone to hold his program together while he's gone. You know the portal work better than anyone." She slides a form across the desk. "Acting program lead. Signing authority on the budget. It's temporary."

I look at the form. I read it twice, because I read everything twice, and because I want to enjoy it.

Signing authority on the portal program's budget. The one with the blank check.

I want to be clear about what I don't do next. I don't buy two hundred thousand LEDs with it. I have met military auditors. I once spent four hours defending a per diem to a man named Keith who could find a rounding error in a sunset. Government money is watched by people with nothing else to watch, and the one thing I will never do is give Keith a reason. The portal program's budget will be spent on the portal. Every euro of it. Exactly as described in the paperwork, which I will write.

It just so happens that the portal is the single most important asset my business has.

"Of course," I say, and sign. "Anything for the program."

---

The third is Colonel Rourke.

Rourke is the military liaison to the program, and he believes, sincerely and without evidence, that the portal belongs to him. He has never seen it. He's never been in sub-basement four. He calls in on a video link at two, from an office with three flags in it, and wants a status report on "the anomaly."

"Inert," I tell him, with the gravity of a person delivering bad news to a family. "It's a residual construction artifact. A field effect in the foundation. There's nothing through it but sub-basement four." I pause. "We've written it up as construction rubble."

Off-camera, Bethany chokes on her coffee.

Rourke's face falls. He wanted a door to something. Men like Rourke always want a door to something.

So I give him one.

"But," I say, and bring up a slide I prepared last night, "while studying it, we did make progress on something else." The slide says COMPACT FIELD REACTOR — CONCEPT. It is, in fact, a slightly cleaned-up version of the reactor I built in Geneva. I made him a copy. It's like giving a child a toy car so they stop trying to drive the real one. "Deployable power, one megawatt, fits in a vehicle. I'll need a small budget line to develop it."

The colonel leans toward his camera. I can see the flags reflected in his eyes.

"Put it in writing," he says. "And Dr. Ruler—remember who signs your checks."

"I never forget," I say, and end the call.

Bethany looks at me over the rim of her cup.

"You just gave the military a reactor so they'd stop asking about the portal."

"I gave a man who thinks I work for him a reason to keep thinking it." I close the laptop. "Belief is load-bearing, Bethany. Rourke thinks I answer to him. Halvorsen thinks I answer to her. Gerald thinks I answer to a form. All three beliefs are holding up the building I'm standing in."

"And when you don't need the building anymore?"

"You don't knock out a load-bearing wall," I say, "until the new structure is up."

---

That evening, I do my least favorite kind of engineering. Accounting.

I am wealthy. I want that on the record. I have a salary most people only see in job postings they don't apply for, patent royalties from three inventions I've since improved beyond recognition, a top-floor apartment, a portfolio, and a 1967 grand tourer in racing green that I have driven exactly as fast as it can go, twice.

But wealth is not the same as capital. Wealth is what you have. Capital is what you can *spend*, this week, without selling your furniture. Almost everything I own is a thing, not money. And my shopping list, when I add it up at my kitchen counter, beside a hundred and eighteen glowing motes in rows of ten, comes to roughly four times the money I can actually move.

I stare at the gap for a while. Then I close it the way people have always closed gaps. I sell things, and I borrow.

The portfolio goes first. Defense stocks, mostly, which feels appropriate. I sell them all on Tuesday morning before the market has finished its coffee.

The apartment gets a mortgage. It had none. It was the one thing I owned outright, clean and paid, like a proof with no loose ends. Now it's collateral. I sign the papers without reading them twice, which is how I know how much it costs me.

The car is the hard one.

A collector comes on Tuesday afternoon with a trailer and a cheque. He runs his hand along the fender like it's a horse. I stand in the garage and watch him drive it away, and I feel something I would, in anyone else, call grief.

"You *loved* that car," Bethany says. She came to help with the paperwork. She has been very quiet.

"It was a car," I say. "The Green is a civilization."

"You're still sad."

"I'm allowed to be sad," I say, "and correct."

---

The last piece is a loan.

The bank has a small, beige meeting room with a bowl of mints that nobody has ever eaten. The loan officer is a pleasant man named Priya's husband—I don't catch his own name, only that he mentions his wife twice—who reads my business plan with growing interest.

"Off-grid lighting for underserved markets," he reads aloud. "Battery-swap stations. Subscription model. Eight million potential customers with no existing grid." He looks up. "Where exactly?"

"Very rural," I say. "Very, very rural."

"And no competition?"

"They currently light their homes with something that's a thousand times less efficient than a candle."

He writes that down. It's the truest sentence in the whole meeting. Then he taps the revenue section.

"And your customers pay you in…?"

And there it is. The real problem, the one I've been walking around all day like a pothole.

I'll spend euros. On LEDs, on batteries, on turbines, on interest to this man's bank. But I'll be paid in motes. And on Earth, a mote is worth a hundred kilojoules of heat: a cup of tea, a fraction of a cent of electricity. I can't pay a mortgage in tea. I can't sell motes to collectors without a lot of people asking what a glowing crystal is. And I can't turn them into gold, because I already tried, and it cost me a whole crystal and some dignity.

"Local currency," I say smoothly. "We'll convert."

He nods, satisfied, and approves a smaller loan than I asked for and a larger one than he wanted to give. We shake hands. I take a mint on the way out, just to be the first.

---

"So how *do* you convert?" Bethany asks in the car. Her car. I no longer have one.

"A mote is only worth a hundred kilojoules here," I say. "So I don't bring motes to Earth. I bring back whatever motes can *buy*, that Earth values more than the Green does."

"Like what?"

I take out my notebook and flip back to Pinnacle, to the eleven pages. And there, in my own handwriting, next to the cartographer's stall: *map weights, small white bars, heavy for size. Tin?*

They weren't tin. Tin isn't that heavy. I remember holding one while I looked at the map, and it had the density of something much better.

"Silver," I say. "Maybe. In a world where the money is crystal, metal is just metal. He was using it to hold paper down."

I draw the loop across a clean page. *Light → motes. Motes → silver. Silver → euros. Euros → LEDs.* An arrow from the end back to the start.

"All I need is the price of silver in motes," I say. "Dimensional analysis does the rest. Motes per kilogram there, euros per kilogram here. The kilograms cancel."

Bethany looks at the page for a long moment.

"You're going to be a precious-metals smuggler."

"*Importer,*" I say. "Smugglers don't file paperwork."

---

Wednesday is for orders. I spend it at the long bench in the lab with two monitors, a phone headset, a spreadsheet, and a single governing constraint written on a sticky note above the screen:

*EVERYTHING MUST FIT THROUGH A 3.6 m CIRCLE.*

You would be amazed how much of the world does not fit through a 3.6 meter circle.

The LEDs are easy. Two hundred thousand warm-white lamps from a factory in Shenzhen, eight weeks' lead time, shipped in cartons small enough to carry. The batteries are easy too, if expensive: rechargeable cells by the pallet, and the parts for swap stations I'll assemble myself on the other side.

The turbine is not easy. I find a secondhand five-megawatt unit from a biomass plant in Latvia that closed last spring, and I spend an hour on the phone with a very patient man named Janis, convincing him that yes, I really do want it delivered in pieces. No piece wider than 3.4 meters. He asks why. I tell him my loading bay has a round door. He says he's heard stranger. I doubt it.

The boilers go the same way: modular, bolt-together. So does the copper cable, in drums I've measured twice. And then, at last, after a lifetime of wanting one, I order trailers.

Three of them. Custom. Low-bed, narrow-profile, built to follow Billy through a hole in the universe with a hand's width to spare on either side.

"You look happy," Bethany says from across the bench.

"I'm buying *trailers.*"

"You said that like other people say *I'm in love.*"

"Other people haven't met my trailers."

I pay the deposits. The number in my account goes down very quickly and very far, and I watch it go without blinking, like the lava settling on Saturday. It's done. Everything after this has to work.

---

Then I start on the surveying equipment, and I make my first mistake of the week.

I add a GPS base station to the cart. A good one, survey-grade, centimeter accuracy. I have the payment half-entered before I stop, and look at it, and feel very stupid for about a second and a half, which is my limit.

GPS needs satellites. There are no satellites over the Green. There is nothing over the Green. On our first day, Billy scanned every band and heard only noise. No satellites, no radio, no grid of any kind. Every coordinate system I've ever used is a thing someone launched into orbit for me.

Over there, if I want a map, I have to *make* one. From the ground up.

I delete the GPS. I order the old tools instead, the ones that surveyed empires before anyone put a clock in space: a total station, a theodolite, laser rangefinders, a steel survey tape a hundred meters long. A LiDAR payload for Billy's drones, so they can map what they see instead of just photographing it. A magnetometer, because Brask spins a lodestone and so this world probably has a magnetic field, but *probably* is not a unit. Soil augers and a penetrometer, because a steam turbine weighs a great deal and I refuse to build it on mud. Water test kits. A seismometer, because I have never once regretted owning a seismometer.

"What's all this for?" Bethany asks, scrolling through the order.

"Foundations. Water. Fuel. Roads." I lean back. "I'll run a baseline from the portal to Pinnacle, measure it to the millimeter, and triangulate outward from there. Every tower, every road, every stalk. The Houses have a painted map. I'll have a survey."

"What's the difference?"

"A painting tells you what someone thought was there," I say. "A survey tells you what *is.*" I think of the cartographer's map, of forty-one little cities floating in a painted green sea. "And whoever draws the real map decides where everything goes."

---

The alert comes at 11:40 that night.

I'm at home, in silk pajamas, halfway through my evening skincare, when my phone chimes with Billy's tone. One of the drones I left in the bamboo above the portal has seen movement.

I open the feed with one hand and hold a jade roller to my cheekbone with the other.

The night-vision image is grainy and green. Between the great stalks, far from the portal but closer than I'd like, figures are moving through the rows. Six of them. They carry spears tipped with small cold lights, and they are searching in a slow, careful line, the way people search when they've been told not to come back empty-handed.

The one in front wears pale gray. On his shoulder, even in the grain, I can make out the shape of a bamboo leaf.

In his hand, he's holding a flashlight. It's very dim now. Barely a glow. He keeps shaking it, as if that will help.

I watch him for a long moment. Then I put down the jade roller.

Rourke thinks I answer to him. Halvorsen thinks I answer to her. And now, it appears, a House on the other side of the universe has decided that I answer to *them.*

I smile at the screen.

It's nice to be wanted.

"Keep watching," I tell Billy. "Don't shoot anyone. They're customers."

Then I finish my skincare, because effortless beauty doesn't take a night off, and I go to bed and sleep beautifully.

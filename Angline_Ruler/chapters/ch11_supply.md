# Chapter 11: Supply

Three farms is a business. A hundred farms is an empire. The difference between them is a spreadsheet.

I build it on the command room's map board over one long night, a column for every resource a farm consumes, a row for every farm I want. One hundred rows. I start filling in the numbers, and by two in the morning, the spreadsheet has started telling me no.

I like that. A plan that never says no hasn't been checked.

---

**Power.** I size a single farm properly for the first time, floor by floor, and discover something embarrassing.

The plant floors need light. Duckweed and bamboo shoots under LEDs want about two hundred and fifty watts per square meter to grow at full speed. Ten floors of plants at two thousand five hundred square meters each is six megawatts on its own. Add heating for the yeast, air for the larvae, climate control for the fungus, pumps, lifts, and the harvest machinery, and one fully running farm wants about twelve megawatts.

Each farm has one five-megawatt turbine.

I stare at the number. Then I pull up the optimizer's logs for the last three months, and there it is, in the history of its decisions. It's been quietly moving floors from plants to yeast and fungus. Not because they print more per square meter. Because they don't need light, and it didn't have enough power to give the plants.

My research assistant has been working around my mistake for months without complaining. I've never had a colleague so considerate. I'll never tell it.

A hundred farms at twelve megawatts each: 1.2 gigawatts. That's a large city's worth of power, in the middle of a bamboo forest, where the most advanced power plant was, until recently, an old man cranking a lodestone.

**Steel.** Thirty floors at two thousand five hundred square meters each is seventy-five thousand square meters of floor. At about fifty kilograms of steel per square meter, one farm is three thousand seven hundred tonnes. A hundred farms: three hundred and seventy thousand tonnes.

Through the Sable Gate, a twenty-five-tonne truck every three minutes, day and night, could technically carry that in a month. But every tonne has to be bought on Earth, through Fernholt Data Storage, a company whose stated business is keeping hard drives cool. A data center that buys three hundred and seventy thousand tonnes of structural steel is not a data center. It's a signal flare.

**Copper.** Sixty tonnes a farm, for wiring, motors and LEDs. Six thousand tonnes. Same problem, smaller flare.

**Feed.** Yeast doesn't live on light. It lives on sugar. The larvae live on the spent fungus bedding. The fungus lives on husk-wood. Every floor eats something that another floor makes. A hundred farms need a hundred times the sugar, which means a hundred times the plants, which means a hundred times the light, which brings me straight back to power.

**Crystals.** This is the one I almost miss.

Every harvest needs a crystal next to it to catch the mote. We refill husks, over and over, and ship the full ones to the vault. But a hundred farms at forty thousand motes a day each would fill four million husks a day. There aren't four million husks in Pinnacle. There might not be four million in the Green. And even if there were, I'd need to *store* them: a vault the size of a stadium, full of glass pebbles, each one worth a meal.

I sit back and look at the board. Five columns, all red.

"How bad?" asks Bethany, who has woken up and come in to find me surrounded by numbers at four in the morning, which she's told me is my happiest state.

"Beautifully bad," I say. "Every single limit is something I can build my way out of. Power, steel, copper, feed, storage. None of them is physics. They're just *supply.*"

"And if you buy it all on Earth?"

"Then everything depends on Earth. On the door, on Fernholt, on Ottoline, on nobody noticing." I tap the column marked STEEL. "And one day, Bethany, somebody is going to try to take all this away from me. Houses, governments, colonels. When that day comes, everything I need should come from ground I control."

"You're talking about war."

"I'm talking about *logistics.* War is just logistics with shouting. Nothing burns material faster, and the side that makes its own steel is the side that's still standing at the end." I erase the column header and write something new above all five.

*MAKE IT HERE.*

---

To make steel, you need iron. To find iron on Earth, you need geologists, core samples, and about three years. To find iron in the Green, it turns out, you need a mote and a good question.

I tried to build a rune that asks. I wanted a machine that could fly over the Green on a drone and read the ground like a map. The optimizer spent a week on it and found nothing. Push runes, heat runes, light runes, all fine. But no shape of oil, however clever, will *ask a question.*

"It needs a mind," Bethany says, reading the failed results. "A question isn't an effect. It's an intention."

"So it needs a person."

"It needs *you.*"

So for three weeks, I become a geologist. Billy carries me in slow loops across the Green, west and north of Pinnacle, and every five kilometers we stop. I get out, stand in the bamboo with three motes in my hand, and ask the ground what's under it. *Iron? Copper? How deep? How much?* The answers come as they always do: weight, direction, a sense of depth. I write them all down, plot them on the LiDAR map, and draw contours between them like a weather chart.

By the end of the third week, the map shows a red smear sixty kilometers west of Pinnacle. It's a low, rocky ridge where the bamboo grows thin and stunted, because its roots can't get through the stone. The locals call it the Red Ridge, and nobody farms it, because nothing grows well on it.

Nothing grows on it because it's made of iron.

Hematite, rich and shallow, some of it lying exposed on the surface like rust on an old car. Enough, by my contours, to make every steel beam I'll ever need and a great many I won't.

A hundred and ten kilometers northwest, a smaller smear of green: copper. Twenty kilometers south of the ridge, a band of limestone for flux. And on the way back, over a salt pan far to the south, the ground gives me an answer I didn't ask for, a strange, light, bright feeling I have to think about for a whole day before I recognize it.

Lithium.

I mark it on the map with a star, and I don't tell anyone why yet.

---

House Ochre technically claims the Red Ridge, the way Houses technically claim everything within a day's ride. The Lady of Ochre is so surprised that anyone wants a hill where nothing grows that she leases it to me for ninety-nine years at one mote in twenty of whatever I dig out of it. She thinks she's robbed me.

I build the mill in eleven weeks.

A blast furnace needs two things: heat, and carbon to pull the oxygen out of the ore. On Earth, coke provides both. Here, I split the job in two.

The carbon comes from husk-wood. The Houses' drained stalks, the rot they gave me for free, go into kilns and come out as charcoal, black and light and perfect. The heat comes from runes. Heat is the cheapest thing a mote can do. A mote turns into heat at nearly a hundred percent, and heat runes, now that the optimizer has tuned them, waste almost nothing. So the furnace walls are lined with them, steel tiles milled with spirals, each with a hollow and a mote magazine, and the furnace runs hotter and cleaner than anything that burns.

Half the charcoal. None of the smoke. And every megajoule of heat paid for in motes I print myself.

The oxygen for turning iron into steel comes from water, split by electricity. The rolling mill is driven by rune motors, forty of them, arranged along the line in banks, each one tuned by a controller. The cranes are rune lifters. The whole mill runs day and night, and it employs nine hundred people from the lower city of Pinnacle, who come out to the ridge in carts every morning and go home every night with wages in their pockets. More motes than any of them has ever earned, to run machines none of their grandparents could have imagined.

On the morning of the first tap, I stand at the edge of the casting floor in a heat suit and watch the plug knocked out of the furnace. Iron pours out in a white-orange river, brighter than anything else in the Green, even the Lamp Hall. The air shimmers. Brask stands beside me and doesn't say a word.

The first beam comes off the rolling mill four days later. It's warm. I put my hand on it anyway.

"Four hundred tonnes a day," I tell Bethany that night. "Two thousand by the end of the year. It's the first steel ever made in this world by anyone who wasn't hitting it with a hammer."

"And the copper?"

"Next month. Same method, smaller furnace." I lean back. "And every single tonne of it is made from ground I lease, with fuel I print, by people I pay, and not one gram of it had to come through the door."

---

Steel solves itself with iron and fire. Power doesn't.

I need 1.2 gigawatts. Husk-wood can't do it. Every drained stalk in the Green, burned in boilers, might give me a third of that, and I'd be spending all my time hauling rot. Mote motors can't do it either: turning motes into electricity is the circular firing squad of energy policy, burning my own money to light the rooms that print it.

There's only one answer, and I've known it since the spreadsheet. I've known it, if I'm honest, since Geneva.

Billy's reactor is a fusion reactor. A small sun in a steel can, built in a hotel room in three days, because I was bored. It fuses deuterium and tritium, two heavy forms of hydrogen, into helium, and the heat boils water, and the steam spins a turbine. It's the most elegant way ever devised to spin a magnet, and the committee that eventually approved the full-size design took eleven months to do it and never built one.

There is no committee in the Green.

---

"So build a big one," says Bethany. "What's stopping you?"

"Tritium."

I draw it for her on the command room board. Deuterium is easy. One hydrogen atom in every six thousand or so in ordinary water is deuterium; split enough water with enough electricity and you can collect it. Tritium is the hard part. It's radioactive, with a half-life of about twelve years, so there's almost none of it in nature. On Earth, it comes out of a handful of specialized reactors, and every gram of it is counted, tracked, reported to international inspectors, and guarded like a secret. The whole civilian supply on Earth is a few tens of kilograms.

"How much do you need?"

"A fusion plant producing a gigawatt of heat burns about fifty-six kilograms of tritium a year." I write it down. "To make 1.2 gigawatts of *electricity*, I need about three and a half gigawatts of heat. That's two hundred kilograms a year."

"That's more than exists."

"On Earth. And even if I could buy a gram, the moment Fernholt Data Storage orders tritium, every inspector in Europe knows exactly who's buying." I tap the little canister symbol I've drawn in the corner. "All I have is what Billy carries. Forty grams, from the old military budget. Keith signed off on it, bless him."

"So you can't do it."

"So I can't *buy* it." I draw a second circle around the first. "I have to *breed* it."

---

Here is the trick, and it's a very old one; fusion engineers have been drawing it on whiteboards for fifty years.

Every time deuterium and tritium fuse, they release a fast neutron. Most reactors treat that neutron as a nuisance. But if you surround the fusion chamber with a blanket of lithium, the neutron hits a lithium atom, and the lithium splits into helium and a brand-new atom of tritium.

The reactor makes its own fuel. Put lead in the blanket too, and some neutrons knock loose extra neutrons from it, so each fusion can breed slightly more than one tritium. Engineers call that a breeding ratio. A good blanket gets about 1.15: burn a hundred atoms of tritium, breed a hundred and fifteen.

"So it grows," Bethany says slowly.

"It *compounds.*" I'm writing fast now. "I start small, on Billy's grams. I run the reactor only as hard as my tritium supply allows. Every day, the blanket gives me back a little more than I burned. The more tritium I have, the harder I can run it, and the harder I run it, the faster it breeds more."

I do the arithmetic on the board. Keep about two days of fuel in circulation. Net gain fifteen percent of what's burned. The inventory grows by roughly seven percent a day. It doubles every nine or ten days.

Thirty grams to two hundred kilograms is about thirteen doublings.

"Four months," I say. "From a thimble of Billy's fuel to enough tritium to run a grid. Compound interest, Bethany. I keep telling you."

"And the lithium?"

I tap the star on the map, over the salt pan far to the south. "I asked the ground a question I didn't mean to. It answered anyway."

"And the lead?"

"Where do you think Pinnacle's silver comes from?" I smile. "Silver comes out of the ground with lead. Ottoline's been melting down the by-product of the best lead mine in the Green."

---

The seed reactor goes up beside the Sable Gate in eight weeks: a compact, ugly steel sphere in a concrete building, wrapped in a lithium-lead blanket from the salt pan and the silver mines, with Green steel in its frame and a secondhand turbine from Earth beside it. It's barely larger than Billy's. It doesn't need to be large. It needs to be patient.

I name it *Eleven Months.* For the committee.

We start it with thirty grams of Billy's tritium. Billy keeps the other ten, because a reactor that's also an armored vehicle shouldn't run out of fuel in the middle of a sentence.

For the first week, it runs at ten megawatts, and the tritium readings barely move. Bethany checks them every morning with the expression of someone waiting for dough to rise. In the second week, it runs at twenty. By the end of the first month, eighty. The gauge on the tritium store climbs like a stock price.

On the fortieth day, I stand in the turbine hall beside *Eleven Months,* listening to steam from a small sun spin a magnet. It's the same song as the husk-wood turbine, but deeper. Steadier. Like it has somewhere to be for the next fifty years.

"You made a mini sun," says Bethany, "just to spin a magnet."

"I told you that on the day we arrived," I say. "You didn't believe me."

"I believed you. I just didn't think you'd make *another one.*"

"This is the small one," I say. "The big ones start in four months."

---

That leaves the problem I almost missed: where to put the money.

The vault at the first farm is now three concrete buildings and a fourth under construction, full of shelves of motes in labeled trays. Each mote weighs about five grams and holds about a megajoule. It's a fine coin. It's a terrible battery. A hundred thousand of them weigh half a tonne and fill a room, and I'm printing that every day.

But I've seen denser crystals. Oren's grapefruit-sized collector's crystal, hanging from its chain. And the great crystal at the heart of the Lamp Hall, taller than a man, humming like a transformer. Nobody carries a room full of pebbles around their neck.

So I ask Brask where the big ones come from.

*They grow,* he says, as if I'd asked where babies come from, and he's embarrassed for me. *You plant a mother crystal by a field, and every harvest, it drinks. Most of what it drinks, you break off as motes. Chip, chip, a thousand little coins. But a little of it stays. Every harvest, the mother gets a hair thicker.* He holds his fingers a tiny distance apart. *A hand's width a lifetime.*

"And the Lamp Hall crystal?"

*Four hundred years old. The Houses say it was planted when Verdane was a village.* He shrugs. *It isn't only bigger. Old crystal is* heavier. *Tighter. A fist of it holds more than a bucket of motes.*

Denser. Slower growth, tighter lattice, more energy per gram. The Houses have been making high-density storage for four centuries. The question is whether they know what they've made.

"Bethany," I say. "How would you like a new research target?"

---

The crystal chambers go into a new shed, well away from everything, behind an earth berm. I've learned. Twenty of them, each holding a seed crystal clamped in a steel cradle, fed by a steady stream of harvests from a yeast tank beside it. The optimizer controls everything it can: feeding rate, temperature, the pressure of push runes squeezing the cradle from all sides, the rhythm of the harvests.

It takes six weeks to find the first pattern and another six to push it.

Slow is better. Steady is better. Cold is better. And, to my great satisfaction, squeezed is *much* better. Push runes clamping the growing crystal from all sides, like the weight of a mountain, force the lattice tighter as it grows. The best chamber grows a crystal in five weeks that the Houses would take a century to grow, as dense as anything in the Lamp Hall.

We call them cores. A core the size of my fist weighs about a kilogram and holds ten thousand motes. Ten gigajoules. Fifty times denser than motes by weight. A hundred thousand motes, the whole vault's daily intake, fit in ten cores in a backpack.

The night the first full core comes out of its cradle, I hold it in both hands under the shed lights. It's warm, heavy, and a deep, clear blue, like looking down into the sea. It hums very faintly against my palms.

"It's beautiful," Bethany says.

"It's *storage,*" I say, and don't let go of it for about ten minutes.

---

The near miss happens three weeks later.

Chamber nine is growing a core faster than the optimizer's safety limits recommend, because the optimizer, as I have mentioned, is a better researcher than me and a great deal less careful. At eight thousand motes, a flaw in the lattice, too small for any instrument to see, reaches its breaking point.

The core cracks.

A mote gives up its energy politely, over seconds or minutes, drawn out by a rune or a hand. A cracked core doesn't give it up. It *loses* it. All of it, at once.

I'm six hundred meters away, in the turbine hall, when the berm lights up like sunrise. The sound arrives two seconds later: a deep, flat thump that I feel in my chest and in the soles of my feet. The windows of the turbine hall flex inward. When I get outside, there's a column of steam and dust rising over the berm, lit orange from underneath.

The crystal shed is gone. Where it stood, there's a crater ten meters across, its walls glazed and glowing. The yeast tanks beside it are gone. The earth berm, three meters of packed soil I built as a precaution, has done its job and very little more.

Nobody is hurt. Nobody was in the shed at night, because after the vault accident, nobody is ever in a test shed at night.

Bethany arrives beside me at a run, in her lab coat over her pajamas, as she did last time. She looks at the crater for a long time.

"Eight thousand motes," she says quietly.

"Eight gigajoules, released in less than a second." I've already done the arithmetic. I couldn't stop myself if I tried. "About two tonnes of TNT."

"From something the size of a fist."

"Yes."

We stand and watch the crater glow.

I'm thinking about two things.

The first is the obvious one, and it's not a comfortable thought even for me. A core is the most valuable object in this world, and it's also a bomb. A perfect battery and a perfect weapon, and the only difference between them is whether it's handled gently. I'll have to build safety into every core we make: slow growth limits, flaw scans, cradles that bleed off energy if a crack begins. And I'm going to encrypt the growth records next to the long-reach rune, in the file I never open.

The second is worse.

"Bethany," I say. "The crystal in the Lamp Hall. How big do you think it is?"

She thinks. "Two meters tall? A meter across?"

"Four hundred years of growth. Denser than our cores." I do the math, and for once, I really wish I hadn't. "Millions of motes. Maybe tens of millions."

She goes very still.

On our first day in the Green, I told her to assume the locals had nuclear weapons. I was disappointed when they didn't.

They do. They've had them for centuries. They hang them at the tops of their towers and read by their light. And I've been assuming, the way you assume things about people without electricity, that they've never worked out what they're sitting under.

---

I find Brask at the edge of the crater at dawn, with a mug of something hot, looking down into the glassy bowl. He doesn't seem surprised to see me.

*You've made a small Carrow,* he says.

"A what?"

He gives me the particular patience he saves for questions a child could answer. Then he tells me.

Five and a half centuries ago, before the Compact, an army besieged a city called Carrow. Its seat held a great crystal three hundred years old. The attackers broke the walls and fought their way up the tower, and somewhere in the fighting, a stray blast struck the crystal.

Nobody knows exactly what happened next, because nobody within twenty kilometers survived to say. Carrow is gone. So is the army that besieged it. So is the hill it stood on. What's there now is a lake, perfectly round, a kilometer and a half across. Carrow Mere.

*Every House child learns it before their letters,* Brask says. *It's why the Compact says a seat is never stormed. It's why the Lady keeps her crystal at the top of a thousand stairs, under a megalith, under guard, where no fight can ever reach it.* He sips. *You thought we didn't know?*

I think of the Lamp Hall. The great crystal humming in its cold white room, and the Lady beneath it, upright and still, never raising her voice.

She isn't calm because she doesn't know what hangs over her. She's calm because she does. Because everyone does. Because any House that ever came up those stairs with an army would die with her, and they all know it.

"You have nuclear weapons," I say slowly. "You've had them for five hundred years. And you've built an entire civilization around never using them."

*Is that what you call them?* Brask tries the word, and doesn't like it. *We call it the peace.*

---

I stand at the edge of my small Carrow for a long time, while the sun comes up over the fields, and I rethink the last year.

I've climbed their stairs. I've drunk their wine. I've taken their tithes and their rot and their hills, and I've talked, and talked, and never once done what I did in sub-basement four. At the time, I told myself that was strategy. Diplomacy is cheaper than war.

It was. It was also, I now understand, the only reason I'm still alive.

I can grow faster than the Houses. I print more motes in a day than some of them harvest in a season. But they've been harvesting this planet for generations. Every one of them sits on centuries of stored crystal, under megaliths built by people who knew exactly what they were doing, bound by a peace that has held for five hundred years because everyone who breaks it dies. I've been walking around their world like a clever child in a room full of loaded guns, and they've been kind enough, or careful enough, not to point any at me.

I'm very glad I negotiated.

On the first day, I was disappointed there would be no worthy opponent. I was wrong. They've been here the whole time, sitting quietly at the tops of their towers with the end of the world in their laps, far more sophisticated than I gave them credit for.

It's the best news I've had all year.

---

By the end of the season, the spreadsheet on the command room wall has changed color.

The steel column is green: the Red Ridge makes nine hundred tonnes a day and climbing, with a second furnace being lined with heat runes. The copper column is green: a smaller mill at the northwestern smear, pouring its first ingots. The storage column is green: cores, grown slowly and carefully now, flaw-scanned and cradled, with a hundred thousand motes to a backpack. The power column is amber: *Eleven Months* is breeding tritium on schedule, and the foundations for four full-size reactors are already poured beside it, waiting for the fuel to catch up.

The feed column is still red. There's no such thing as a finished spreadsheet.

And for the first time, the build column says twelve.

Twelve farms under construction at once, on twelve plots of licensed ground across the lands of three Houses. Twelve swarms of rune lifters, twenty platforms each. Twelve crews of welding crawlers running their seams under floodlights. Green steel on every frame, from the ridge, by the wagonload. At the rate the ridge produces, the steel for a new farm comes off the rolling mill every four days, and every four days, somewhere in the bamboo, another thirty-floor lantern starts to rise.

The limit isn't me. It isn't motes. It isn't even steel, anymore.

It's ground.

Every one of those twelve plots is a charter, a license, a tithe, and a set of stairs. Every new farm makes another House a little richer and a little more dependent on me, which is exactly what I want. But it's also exactly what the Houses want: every farm is on their land, under their stakes, where their law applies. And one day, I suspect, one of them will start to wonder what I'd do without it.

---

That one arrives, as I expected, in the person of the Lord of Sable.

He comes out to the Red Ridge on a gray morning, uninvited, with four guards and no envoy, and he walks the length of the casting floor beside me in silence, watching iron pour. He watches the rolling mill turn out beams. He watches the rune cranes lift them, and the carts roll them away toward the farms, each one stamped with a small circle that Brask designed and that means, as far as anyone in the Green is concerned, *made by Ruler.*

*This is a great deal of steel,* he says finally. *For farms.*

"Farms are very large."

*They are.* He watches another beam slide past. *In my grandfather's time, the Houses fought over the Red Ridge. It's where the old spears came from. It was abandoned when the fields grew more profitable than war.* He turns and looks at me. His smile is very thin. *It seems it's profitable again.*

"Steel is profitable for many things, my lord."

*That's what concerns me.*

He leaves as quietly as he came. I stand on the casting floor and watch his carriage roll away toward the city, and I note, in the ledger in my head, that the Lord of Sable is better at arithmetic than I'd like.

---

That night, in the bunker, Bethany finds me at the map board with a ruler, drawing a line I've drawn a dozen times. From Fernholt east, four hundred kilometers, to a salt mine where a second flywheel bank is being installed. And in the Green, the same line, to the cliffs above Ruler's Reach.

Where the harvest law ends at the waterline. Where there are no stakes, no stairs, and no Houses.

"You're going to the sea," she says.

"The sea has no landlords."

She looks at the map for a long moment. At the drone photographs pinned beside it: the bay, the cliffs, the kelp forests dark beneath the water. And the little fishing villages strung along the shore, with their boats pulled up on the shingle.

"It has people," she says.

"It does."

"Who don't answer to any House."

"No."

She waits. I realize, after a moment, that she's waiting for me to say what they'll answer to instead.

I find, to my surprise, that I haven't decided yet.

"That," I say, "is next season's problem."

I put down the ruler, and turn off the map board light, and go to bed. It takes effort to look effortlessly beautiful, and the portrait is only nine months away.

# Chapter 15: Unassailable

### Angline

I take it back the next morning. The breaking and entering.

"Belay that," I tell Nkemelu, over coffee in the clinic courtyard. "It was a fugitive's idea. I had it because I've been thinking like a fugitive for a year and a half, and it's made me small."

She looks at me over her cup. "You're not a fugitive?"

"I'm a fugitive on Earth." I nod at the cliff top, the door, the lit town, the sea. "Here, I'm the largest economy on the coast. I've been sneaking around a planet I don't live on, hiding from people who can't reach me, and planning to pick a lock in a building I don't need." I set my cup down. "I'm done hiding."

---

That afternoon, I stand in front of the whiteboard in the courtyard and write down every way in.

This is the whole of my position, when you strip it down. I live in a universe that the people who want to arrest me cannot reach. My farms, my reactors, my rail, my people, all of it is on the far side of a door. Nobody on Earth can touch any of it unless a door opens that I didn't open.

So I list the doors.

| Way in | Earth side | Green side | Who controls it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sub-basement four scar | Rourke's building | Beside Farm One, Verdane land | Rourke, in eight weeks |
| Sable Gate | Fernholt bunker | Sable's field, Sable garrison | Me, watched |
| Reach door | Kessel salt mine | Ruler's Reach | Me, now known to Nkemelu |
| Thin place 1 | ? | ? | Nobody yet |
| Thin place 2 | ? | ? | Nobody yet |
| Thin place 3 | ? | ? | Nobody yet |

"Six," says Bethany, reading it.

"Six ways in. And my entire bargaining position, everything I'll ever say to any government on Earth, rests on that number being zero." I tap the board. "The day I tell the world I exist, I need them to understand, very clearly, that there's nothing they can do about it. No door they can open. No door they can seize. Nothing on their side of the universe worth raiding. Only me, coming and going when I choose."

"You want to be unassailable," says Nkemelu.

"I want to be *unassailable for as long as possible,*" I correct her. "Nothing lasts forever. But the longer it lasts, the more I can build before it ends."

---

To get to zero, I need two things nobody has ever done.

The first is to close a door permanently. Not shut it, the way I shut sub-basement four with a switch, leaving a scar that a colonel with thirty megawatts can push back open. *Heal* it. Leave the place as ordinary as any other patch of air.

The second is to open a door from the Green side alone. Every door so far has needed a push from Earth: a flywheel bank, a resonator, someone on the far side with a timer. If I'm going to close every Earth-side door I own, I need to be able to open a new one, anywhere I like, without anyone on Earth doing anything at all.

I give both problems to the research loop. And I give the loop something it's never had before: Brask.

Brask runs the rune works now. It's a long hall on the plateau, next to *Bielefeld*, full of CNC mills cutting rune plates out of the best materials we've found, bronze for heat, a dense gray stone from the Red Ridge for push, all milled to a hundredth of a millimeter from the optimizer's designs. He walks the floor in a leather apron with a loupe on a chain around his neck, like Ottoline, shouting at machinists in three dialects, and he's the happiest I've ever seen him.

*You want a rune that opens a door by itself,* he says, when I explain. *And a rune that closes one forever.*

"Yes."

*By next week.*

"Three weeks."

He looks at me for a long moment. *Two,* he says. *I have machines now.*

---

It takes nineteen days. Brask is annoyed about the extra five.

The opening turns out to be a matter of scale. A door from nothing, pushed from one side only, needs a feeding ring far bigger than anything we've built: a circle of twenty-four stone megalith runes, each the size of a car, milled at Brask's works and set on the cliff top by lifter swarms, around a point we've chosen above the bay. And it needs motes. A great many.

One hundred and twenty thousand.

Not long ago, that would have been a month of printing. Now it's a little over a day of the coast's output. I watch the ring drink it in eleven seconds. The air at the center folds inward, and then outward, and there it is, a small ragged door, a meter across, hanging over the cliff top with a cold wind blowing through it from a wet hillside on Earth.

Nobody on Earth pushed. Nobody on Earth even knows.

The closing is harder, and stranger. The optimizer finds it by running the opening backward: a second ring of runes, their push bars reversed and their hollows turned outward, that doesn't widen the door but *pulls its edges through each other,* like drawing a knot tight. It costs twenty thousand motes. When it's done, there's no point of light, no shimmer, nothing at all. I stand where the door was, holding three motes, and ask the air what's there.

It says: *nothing.* Not a scar. Not a thin place. Just air, over a cliff, like any other.

"Healed," says Bethany.

"*Sealed,*" I say. "I'm not a doctor."

I look at the whiteboard. At the six ways in.

Then I pick up the marker, and circle the first one.

---

The first one is on Verdane's land. Of course it is.

The old portal site sits in the bamboo a few hundred meters from Farm One, which is no longer mine. Verdane's harvest-masters live there now. To seal the scar, I need to put twenty-four stone megaliths in a ring around a patch of empty ground in a House field, spend a small fortune in a single night, and ask the House's permission to do it.

Which means I have to tell the Lady why.

---

There's a lift in the Lamp Hall tower now. My lift. A steel cage in a shaft cut through the tower's core, humming up a thousand and eight steps' worth of height in ninety seconds, powered by a line from *Personal Use.* Oren rides it with me, and grips the rail the whole way, and says nothing, the way people say nothing in the presence of something they've decided to be impressed by later, in private.

The Lady receives me under her great crystal. She looks, I think, slightly less tired than she used to.

*You want to put stones in my field,* she says, after I've asked. *Twenty-four of them. Around an empty patch of ground by the farm you gave me.* She regards me. *Why?*

I've considered lying. I considered it all the way up in the lift. It would be simpler. I could call it a ward, and she'd know I was lying, and she'd let me do it anyway, and remember.

I find I don't want her to remember that.

"There's a door there," I say. "The first one. The one I came through, the day I bought my way through your gate with a flashlight. I closed it, a year and a half ago. But a closed door leaves a scar, and on the other side, in my world, there are people who want to open it again. They've built a machine. They'll try in six weeks."

The Lady's stillness changes quality. It doesn't break. It deepens.

*People from your world,* she says. *Coming through into my field.*

"Soldiers," I say. "With weapons that don't need motes. Into a field beside your farm, a morning's walk from this tower. They won't know what a House is. They won't know what the Compact is. They'll know only that they've been sent to take something back, and that everything they see is strange."

She's silent for a long time. The great crystal hums above us. I think of Carrow.

*And you,* she says finally, *want to close a door into your own world. Against your own people.*

"They're not my people. They're my former employers." I shrug. "And I'd like my world and yours to meet, if they're going to meet, on terms I choose. Not through a hole in your field, with rifles."

She looks at me for a long time with those pale, still eyes.

*Put your stones in my field,* she says. *On one condition.*

"Name it."

*I will watch.*

---

The megaliths come by rail, on flatcars, from Brask's works on the coast: twenty-four stones, each the size of a small car, each milled with a single reversed push rune, their hollows turned outward. The lifter swarms set them in a ring in the bamboo over three nights, under floodlights, while Verdane's harvest-masters stand at the edge of the light and watch in silence.

On the fourth night, the Lady comes.

Her bearers carry her chair down from the city, through the rows, and set it at the edge of the ring. Oren stands beside her with a lamp. Six of her guards form a loose circle around her in the dark, and behind them, very still, stands someone I don't see at first: a woman in plain gray, with nothing in her hands, whose outline, when I look directly at her, seems to shimmer faintly, as if there's heat coming off her on a cool night.

A Shade. The Lady's.

I keep my radar on her the whole time. I suspect she knows.

I stand in the center of the ring, over the empty patch of ground, holding three motes, and ask the air what's there.

It answers the way a bruise answers a finger: a thin, sore, tight feeling, like a place where something has been torn and is holding itself closed with effort. Like a door with its hand on the handle.

"It's here," I say.

Brask throws the switch.

The ring drinks. Thirty-five thousand motes, pulled from a wagon of cores at the ring's edge, far more than our test door on the cliff, because this scar is old and wide and was once a real door that stood for months. The stones glow along their carved bars. The air at the center goes strange. It doesn't shimmer. It *tightens*, the way skin tightens as a wound closes, and for a moment, just a moment, I see it: the edge of the old portal, a ghostly circle three and a half meters wide, folding in on itself, pulling its own edges through each other, drawing tight.

Then nothing. Air, and bamboo, and floodlights, and the cores at the edge of the ring gone cloudy and dark.

I raise three more motes and ask again.

*Nothing.* Not sore. Not tight. Just a field, at night.

---

The Lady sits in her chair at the edge of the ring for a long time, looking at the place where there's nothing.

*You've closed a door into your own world,* she says.

"One of them."

*How many are there?*

I think about it. About the whiteboard in the clinic courtyard. About what I'm going to do in the next five weeks.

"After I'm finished," I say, "only the ones I make."

She considers that. Behind her, the Shade in gray has stopped shimmering. She's looking at the stones, and then at me, with an expression I recognize from the Lord of Sable, from Brask on the night of the invisible predator: someone recalculating.

*Then I'll stop counting your doors, Ruler,* says the Lady. *And start counting you.*

Her bearers lift her chair. They carry her back through the rows toward the city, toward my lift, and her tower, and her crystal.

I stand in the empty ring and watch her go. Then I take out my phone, and message Bethany, and cross the first line off the list.

---

The next three belong to Nkemelu.

She brings them through the salt mine on paper, in her folder with the elastic band: three thin places, out of the eleven reports she started with. She's spent the last month visiting every one of them on her own time, with a compass, a thermometer, a camera and the mote I gave her. At each one, she stood where the reports said, held the mote, and asked, the way I taught her.

"The first is a flooded quarry in the Ardennes," she says, laying out photographs. "Belgium. Closed in the sixties after two workers walked into the water on a calm day and didn't come up. Divers found nothing. Locals won't swim there. Compasses swing about eight degrees at the water's edge."

"And when you asked?"

"It felt like standing next to a door someone was leaning on from the other side." She turns to the second photograph: a tunnel mouth in rock, snow on the ground. "A disused rail tunnel in the Alps. Built in 1911, abandoned in 1913. The official reason is water ingress. The unofficial one is that the last maintenance crew refused to go in. There's a spot two hundred meters inside where the temperature drops six degrees, and it's been six degrees colder for a hundred years."

The third photograph is a building: tall brick, barred windows, weeds in the yard.

"A psychiatric hospital, closed in 1987. Three hundred kilometers north of here, Earth side. Patients in one ward in the basement used to report a door in the wall that wasn't there. Staff called it the Quiet Room, because nobody who slept in it ever wanted to talk about their dreams." She taps the photograph. "When I asked, there, it was the strongest of the three."

I look at them for a long time. Three natural doors, on a continent of five hundred million people, and she found all three in a month.

"Inspector," I say, "you're very good at this."

"I know," says Nkemelu. "It's the most fun I've had in twenty years."

---

Finding the other side is my job.

One to one: the same latitude, the same longitude. I run each set of coordinates across onto Billy's survey of the Green. The quarry lands in wild bamboo, three hundred kilometers southwest of Pinnacle, beyond any House's ward-posts. The tunnel lands in the mountains far to the south, in country nobody in the Green farms because the slopes are too steep and the things living in them are too hungry. The hospital lands in a House field outside a city called Tessary, whose Houses I have never met and have no charter with.

Natural thin places, it turns out, are cheaper to seal than scars. They were never torn. They're only thin, and a ring of twelve small stones and eight thousand motes pulls them tight.

We do the quarry first, in daylight, with Billy and a crew and radar on every side. It takes an afternoon.

The tunnel takes a week, because the mountains are exactly as bad as I was told. On the third night, something the size of a bear, cold and invisible, comes down the slope toward the camp, and the radar sees it, and Billy's autocannon draws a line of fire in the scree three meters in front of it, and it thinks better of us. I sleep with my glove on for the rest of the trip.

Tessary takes one night, and no permission. Twelve small stones in a House field, set in the dark by a single lifter swarm running without lights, eight thousand motes, and gone again before dawn. Nobody sees us. The Houses of Tessary will never know there was anything there to seal.

On each of the three nights, on Earth, Nkemelu stands at the other end: at the quarry's edge, in the tunnel, in the basement of the Quiet Room, holding her compass and her mote.

On each of the three mornings, she sends the same message.

*Compass steady. Temperature normal. Nothing there.*

---

I tell her about Brussels on the evening after Tessary, in the clinic courtyard, with Bethany there, because I don't do important things in private.

I tell her all of it. The drones Brask's works are building: four thousand of them, each carrying a ring of light-bending runes and a small core. The veil. Sixty seconds, a city vanishing, and nobody hurt. Then the broadcast, and the message: I exist, I am out of reach, I could have done something much worse, and here is what I would like instead.

"Why Belgium?" says Bethany.

"Brussels is where a whole continent keeps its government. It's the one city where making an impression on one country makes it on twenty-seven." I shrug. "And I have nothing against Belgium. That's important. It isn't personal. It's a demonstration."

Nkemelu hasn't said anything. She's looking at the table.

"No warning," she says finally. "Nobody told. A million people look up, and their city is gone."

"For sixty seconds. Nobody is harmed."

"A million people *terrified.* Planes over it. Cars on the ring road watching the city vanish in front of them. People who'll have nightmares about it for the rest of their lives." She looks up at me. "And then you go on television and say *next time, a crater.*"

"I say next time it *could* be a crater. I'm not going to do it."

"They won't know that."

"That," I say, "is the point."

She's quiet for a long time.

"I agreed to find things out," she says. "I found them. I sealed your doors for you, because I agreed with you. Soldiers through a hole in someone's field would have been a disaster. But this." She shakes her head. "This is threatening a city to make a government do what you want. There's a word for that, and I've spent twenty years arresting people for smaller versions of it."

"I'm not asking you to help."

"You're asking me to know about it and do nothing." She stands up. She takes the mote out of her coat pocket, the one I gave her on the cliff, and holds it for a moment, and then sets it on the table between us.

"I'm a police officer," she says. "I'll still be one tomorrow. I'm going to go home and write down everything I know. Every word. The truth, the way you like it."

"Nobody will believe you."

"Probably not." She picks up her folder, with its elastic band. "I'll write it anyway."

I could stop her. I'd only need to close the salt mine door tonight instead of next week. She'd be here, with us, on the coast, forever, with a view of the sea and no way home.

I don't. It wouldn't be optimal. People who are kept don't work. And she'd hate it, and I find, somewhat to my irritation, that I don't want her to hate it.

"The report," I say, as she reaches the courtyard gate. "You'll say that I told you the truth? About Alphonseo?"

She stops. "Yes."

"Good," I say. "Put that in. It's the only good thing anyone will ever write about me in a police file."

She looks back at me once, with an expression I can't classify, and then she goes. Down the cliff path, across the plateau, through the humming door, into the salt, and home.

Bethany doesn't say anything for a while. Then she reaches across the table and picks up the mote.

"What column is she in now?" she asks.

I think about it. About the ledger, and its two columns, debts and credit.

"I don't have a column for her," I say. "I'm going to have to make one."

### Bethany

We close Fernholt first.

It takes nine days to empty it. Everything that matters comes through the Sable Gate on Billy's trailers: the twelve flywheels, the resonator, the last of the silver, the command room's map board with Angline's pencil lines still on it. Sable's garrison watches every load go past with the expression of men who've been told to inspect everything and have given up trying to understand any of it.

Then the bunker is just a bunker again. Empty concrete, caged bulbs, the smell of cold stone. I walk through it once on the last night, because I lived here, briefly, when we were running. The kitchen where the coffee tasted like 1983. The cracked mirror where Angline put on a beige cardigan and nearly cried.

On the Green side, Brask's crew sets twenty-four stones around the Gate, and the Sable garrison watches them do that, too.

When the ring drinks and the Gate folds in on itself and vanishes, the Shade who commands the garrison, a thin young man who has barely spoken to any of us in six months, walks slowly into the empty space where it stood. He stands there for a long time. Then he turns and looks at Angline with the same face I saw on the Lady's Shade in Verdane's field: someone recalculating.

He'll report it to the Lord of Sable by nightfall. I'm certain the Lord will do arithmetic.

---

The government's test is six days later.

We watch it through the last drone Nkemelu left on the roof across the street, the night before she stopped answering our messages. At 10:00 exactly, the building's new substation hums. The thermal camera shows the ventilation stack glow, then glow brighter, then brighter still, for four long minutes, as thirty megawatts go into a scar that isn't there anymore.

Nothing happens.

At 10:40, they try again. At 11:15, a third time. At noon, a van arrives, and people in suits get out of it, and go inside, and don't come out for a long time.

Angline watches the whole thing with her arms folded and an expression of deep professional satisfaction.

"They'll assume the scar decayed," she says. "Or that they built the resonator wrong. They'll spend a year rebuilding it." She smiles slightly. "They'll probably improve it. I'd have liked to see that."

"And Nkemelu's report?"

"If she's filed it, someone's reading it right now. About a door that doesn't exist, in a salt mine they'll find empty, to a place they can't get to." She shrugs. "It's the most accurate document in any government archive on Earth. And it's completely useless."

She says it lightly. She doesn't look at the empty chair at the courtyard table.

---

The salt mine is last.

This one takes longer, because this is the door we live on. Everything the coast has gotten from Earth for a year came through it: the cabins, the filters, the antibiotics that saved thirty-three people, the diesel, the first reactor parts. Now, before it closes, everything Angline thinks we might ever need comes through at once. Trainloads, day and night, for two weeks. Machine tools. Medical supplies. Seed stock. Spare parts for everything. Kessel buys it all through Ottoline's banks in one last spending spree, and the flatcars roll through the humming circle on the cliff top faster than the crews can unload them.

And then there's nothing left to bring.

On the last night, I go through one final time.

I don't tell anyone. I don't make a phone call. I've learned. I just walk down the rails into the salt gallery, where the floodlights are already off, and stand in the dark on the Earth side of the door for a few minutes, breathing air that smells of cold stone and, very faintly, of rain on wet hills two hundred meters above me.

This is the last time I'll stand on Earth unless Angline decides I should. After tonight, there won't be a door I can walk through by myself. The world I was born in, with my mother in it, and her clock, and her fridge with my certificate on it, will be on the other side of a wall with no gaps in it.

I thought I'd feel worse.

I think about the town above the cliff. About the second cohort, seventy-one students, sitting in six containers learning to read a controller screen. About the thirty-three.

Then I walk back through, and up onto the cliff, where the stones are waiting in their ring, and I nod to Brask, and he throws the switch.

The door folds in on itself, tighter and tighter, and is gone.

There's nothing on the cliff top but air, and wind off the sea, and twenty-four stones with their bars going dark.

Angline is standing beside me. She doesn't say anything for a while.

"Zero," she says finally.

"Zero," I agree.

"How do you feel?"

It's the first time, I realize, that she's ever asked me that.

"Like I live somewhere," I say.

She nods, as if that's a reasonable engineering result, and writes it down.

### Angline

Brussels, one to one, is a marsh.

The Green side of the city is four hundred kilometers north and west of the coast: a wide, flat, reed-choked wetland that no House has ever bothered to farm, because the bamboo won't root in it and the grazers sink. Billy drives the last sixty kilometers through water up to his hull, towing a flatcar of shipping containers on a float. In the containers are four thousand drones.

Brask built them. Each one is the size of a dinner plate, with four rotors, a small core in its belly, and on its upper face, milled into a bronze disc, a ring of light-bending runes the optimizer spent six weeks designing. One alone does almost nothing. It bends the light passing near it by a fraction of a degree, like a heat shimmer. Four thousand of them, arranged in a shell around a city and controlled to the millisecond, bend light all the way around it. Anyone outside looks toward the city and sees what's behind it.

It's the Shades' trick, the one that makes a woman in gray shimmer at the edge of a floodlight. Scaled up. Pointed at a capital.

The cost is obscene. Every effect on Earth pays the ninety percent tax, and the shell is kilometers wide, so every drone is projecting at a distance that costs it most of what it spends. I've done the arithmetic twice. Sixty seconds of veil will burn nine hundred thousand motes, more than a week of everything we print.

It's the most expensive minute of my life. It's also, by a very long way, the best value.

---

At 11:20, local time, Brask's crew sets twelve megaliths in a ring on a hummock in the marsh, and at 11:31, they drink a hundred and twenty thousand motes, and a door opens from our side alone into a beech forest on the southeastern edge of Brussels. It's a meter across, and the air coming through it smells like late spring on Earth: wet leaves, distant traffic, a dog barking somewhere.

The drones go through in a stream, rising out of the marsh and vanishing through the circle into the beeches, a hundred a minute for forty minutes. On the other side, they climb into the sky over the forest and spread out, too small and too high for anyone to notice, taking their places one by one in a shell twelve kilometers across, centered on the Rue de la Loi.

Bethany stands beside me at the control console in Billy's cabin, watching the positions fill in on the screen. She hasn't said anything for twenty minutes.

"You're sure nobody gets hurt," she says finally.

"The drones are all above four thousand meters. The air traffic's been clear of the shell for ten minutes; I timed the gap." I tap the screen. "Nothing touches the ground. Nothing touches a person. For sixty seconds, light takes the long way round."

"And the people?"

"Will be frightened." I don't look away from the screen. "That's what it's for."

She's quiet. Then, very softly: "Nkemelu was right."

"Nkemelu was right," I agree. "She usually is. It's why I liked her."

At 11:59:30, every drone is in position.

At 12:00:00, I press the key.

---

This is what the world sees.

On the ring road around Brussels, at midday on a Thursday, drivers stuck in traffic look toward the city and see the towers of the European Quarter, the dome of the Palace of Justice, the whole skyline, simply stop being there. In their place, the land runs on, flat and green, to low hills on the far horizon that nobody has been able to see from here for a hundred and fifty years. A car in the outside lane goes into the barrier at twenty kilometers an hour. No one is hurt. The driver, interviewed that evening, says she thought she'd had a stroke.

On a flight descending into the airport from the north, a passenger filming the view out of the window watches the city vanish under the wing, a gray hole in the world, fields and forest where the streets should be.

And inside the shell, in the streets, the sky goes strange.

Not dark. Pearl-colored, all at once, as if the city were under a vast soap bubble. The sun becomes a bright, warped ring in the middle of it. Shadows go soft and pale. On the Grand-Place, a tourist photographs it and later sells the image to every newspaper in the world. On the steps of a parliament building, a crowd of people stand still with their faces turned up, and nobody says anything at all.

For sixty seconds.

At 12:01:00, I release the key. The shell dissolves. The drones fall back into formation and stream home, down through the beech forest, into the circle, into the marsh. The light takes the short way again. Brussels is exactly where it was.

At 12:44, the last drone comes through. At 12:51, the ring on the hummock drinks twenty thousand motes, and the door folds into itself, and is gone, and the ground is ground again.

No way in. Zero.

---

At 13:00, every channel of a certain national broadcaster interrupts its programming.

It was sold four months ago, for more than it was worth, to a holding company owned by a holding company owned by a company so boring that no financial journalist has yet bothered to read its accounts. The old management stayed on. They were told there would be one editorial instruction, one day, without warning, and it would not be optional.

Today is the day.

The screen goes black. Then it fills with a portrait.

It was taken three weeks ago, the week before the salt mine closed, at double the photographer's rate, in a room on the coast with the sea behind me and the light coming in low from the west. She signed an agreement longer than most treaties, and she will never tell anyone which sea it was. It's the best photograph of me that has ever been taken. I made sure of it. I stood for four hours.

Then the portrait fades, and I'm there, live, in my coat, at a desk in the clinic courtyard with a plain wall behind me, looking into the camera.

"Good afternoon," I say. "My name is Angline Ruler. Some of you will know my face from a much less flattering photograph. I'd like to take this opportunity to replace it."

I let that sit for a moment.

"An hour ago, Brussels disappeared for sixty seconds. That was me. Nobody was harmed. That was deliberate. It could have been otherwise, and it wasn't, and I would like everyone watching to spend a moment on the difference."

Another pause. Every word of this is timed. I wrote it the way I'd write a controller: nothing wasted, and nothing overshooting.

"I live somewhere none of you can reach. I don't say that to frighten you. I say it because it's true, and because the most useful thing I can do today is save your governments a great deal of time looking for a way in. There isn't one. There won't be one unless I open it."

"I don't want much. I would like to be left alone to build things. If I want to build a power plant, it's mine. If I want to put a satellite in the sky, it's mine. Anything that belongs to me, that I made, stays mine, and nobody sends a colonel. And I'd like the charges against Dr. Bethany dropped. She's my colleague, and she's saved a great many lives, and she has a mother."

"In return, I have things to offer that you'll find hard to refuse. Food. More than you can imagine, at prices you won't believe. And energy, clean and cheap, from reactors of a kind none of your committees has ever managed to approve. I'll say more about both, soon."

I lean forward, very slightly, toward the camera.

"I'm aware that some of you are already telling yourselves that what happened in Brussels was a trick of the light. It was. That's rather the point. So, for anyone who needs something more solid: look at the moon on Friday night, at ten o'clock, Brussels time. The Sea of Tranquility, western edge. Bring a telescope."

I sit back.

"Thank you for your time," I say. "I'm here to help."

The screen goes to the portrait again. Holds for five seconds. And returns to a cooking program, which is still running, and in which a man is still, for reasons no one will ever explain, holding a whisk.

---

That night, on the coast, Bethany and I sit in the courtyard with a laptop between us and watch the world watch us.

Every channel. Every language. The ring road, the pearl sky, the car in the barrier, the tourist's photograph of the Grand-Place under its soap-bubble sun. And, over and over, on every one of them, a portrait: a woman in a long coat, with the sea behind her, in low western light, looking out of the screen with an expression of calm, amused certainty.

Not a badge photo. Not once.

Bethany watches it for a long time.

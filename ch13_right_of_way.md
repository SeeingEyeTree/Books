# Chapter 13: Right of Way

### Angline

On the twentieth day, the track-laying train stops fifty meters short of a stone pillar.

It's a ward-post, like the ones we passed on the way east: pale stone, carved deep with a rune, capped with a crystal the size of a fist. On one side of it is the wild bamboo, tangled and dark. On the other is a House field: neat rows of stalks, dark-leaved crops growing in their shade, and a herd of grazers lifting their heads to stare at the enormous steel machine that has just come out of the wilderness and stopped.

Sixty kilometers of rail lie behind it, straight and shining, all the way back to the cliffs. Ahead lie three hundred and forty more, and every one of them belongs to someone.

I climb down from the cab and walk to the ward-post and put my hand on it. The stone is warm. The rune hums faintly under my palm, the way the Lamp Hall crystal hums, doing its one job, which is to keep things like me out.

"Whose field?" I ask Brask.

*Sable's,* he says. *Then Ochre's, then Sable's again, then Verdane's, then all three of them in strips, all the way to Pinnacle.*

"Of course it is."

I look back down the line at what I've built in the wild: the rail, the poles going up beside it for the wires that will come, the camp at the railhead with its radar mast and its floodlights and its crews. Then I look forward, at a field I can't cross without asking.

I drove this route in four days, and nobody stopped me. A convoy passes through a field and is gone by evening. A railway stays. It's forty-eight thousand tonnes of steel that will lie across someone else's land for the next hundred years, and that, it turns out, needs permission. I have never enjoyed asking for it.

---

I go back to the coast that night, on the diesel, to pack.

Shingle has changed in the two months since I drove the silver spike. The kelp lines fill half the bay now, rows of buoys bobbing on the gray water like a vast abacus. A pier runs out from the beach, built of Red Ridge steel that came four hundred kilometers by Billy and trailer before the rail could carry it. At the top of the cliff, beside the door, the swap station has become a small cluster of buildings: a workshop, a store, a clinic tent that Bethany put up in the first week and has been living in ever since.

And there are more people. Not just Shingle's three hundred and twelve. Families from two villages up the coast arrived three weeks ago, walking, with their boats towed behind them along the shore, because they'd heard. Another group came last week. Bethany has been keeping a list.

I find her in the clinic tent at midnight, writing in a notebook of her own by the light of a lamp.

"I'm going to Pinnacle," I tell her. "The rail needs the Houses. All three of them, and they'll want to talk to me together. That means weeks of stairs, wine and posturing."

"How long?"

"A month. Maybe two." I sit down across from her. "You're in charge."

She looks up. "Of what?"

"Everything east of the ward-posts."

She puts down her pen.

"Angline. I'm a biologist. I ran a research loop. I've never been in charge of anything bigger than a lab bench."

"You've been in charge of this tent for two months. Nobody in Shingle has died since you put it up." I lean back. "The coast needs someone who talks to people instead of at them. I'm told that's not me."

"Who told you that?"

"You did. Frequently."

She doesn't laugh. She's looking at her notebook, and I realize it's the list. Names, villages, numbers, ages. Several pages of it.

"More are coming," she says quietly. "A lot more. I've been hearing it from the ones who've arrived. Every village from here to the southern headlands knows about the water and the lamps. Some of them are already walking."

"Then you'll need houses." I stand. "Order whatever you want from Fernholt. Containers come through the door on flatcars. Brask's crews stay here, except the ones I need at the railhead. Marit knows the coast. You know everything else."

"And if I get it wrong?"

I stop at the tent flap and consider the question seriously, the way she deserves.

"Then you'll have made a decision," I say. "You'll find out what was wrong with it, and the next one will be better. A mistake is just a data point with a bad reputation."

I leave in the morning. From the cab of the diesel, as it pulls away from the cliff, I can see her standing outside the tent with the notebook under her arm, watching the train go, looking as if someone has just handed her something heavy and she hasn't yet worked out where to put it down.

She'll work it out. She always does.

### Bethany

The first thing I learn about being in charge is that nobody tells you when it starts. There's no ceremony. The train leaves, and then someone walks into the tent with a question, and then someone else, and by noon there's a line.

The second thing I learn is that Angline makes it look easy because she never, ever doubts herself. I doubt myself constantly. It turns out that's not fatal. It just makes everything take longer.

---

In the first week, eighty people arrive from the north. In the second, two hundred from the south. By the end of the month, the list in my notebook runs to fourteen hundred names, and Shingle, which was forty-one huts on a beach, has become a camp that spills up the cliff path and across the plateau at the top, around the door, in every direction there's flat ground.

I don't know how to build a town. So I do what I'd do with any problem I don't understand. I find someone who does, and I ask.

"Where do people go?" I ask Marit, on the first morning, with a map of the plateau spread on a crate between us.

*Depends who they are,* she says.

She knows everyone. Not personally, not all of them, but she knows every village on this coast, and which ones marry into which, and which ones haven't spoken to each other since a dispute over a net four generations ago. She puts the people from Kettle Point next to the people from Low Sand, because they're cousins. She puts the Grayrock families at the far end of the plateau from the Harrow families, because of the net.

"Is the net really still a problem?"

*The net was a very good net,* says Marit, with complete seriousness.

The houses come through the door on flatcars. Shipping containers, bought by Fernholt from a company in Rotterdam that converts them into site cabins: insulated, windowed, a door at each end, a stove flue already cut. Angline would have designed something better from scratch. I don't have time to design anything. I order four hundred of them. Brask's crew has left me a swarm of rune lifters, and every day the lifters set down forty more cabins in the rows Marit and I drew.

The people from the coast walk through them with their hands on the walls, touching the steel as if it might be a trick. Some of them have never been inside a building with a window.

I draw the latrines. That's the part nobody wants to think about, and it's the part that matters most. Downhill of everything. Downwind. Far from the water. I draw the water too: Angline's spring intake has enough flow for Shingle, but not for fourteen hundred people, so I have Brask's foreman tap a second spring a kilometer inland and run a pipe to a second filter and a second doser. I check the chlorine levels myself, every morning, at the tap, with a test kit. It's the most boring job on the coast.

It's the one I'm proudest of.

---

The outbreak starts on the thirty-first day.

A group of sixty walks in from the south in the evening: a village called Tern Hollow, two hundred kilometers down the coast, eleven days on foot. They're thin and exhausted and several of them are carrying children. And three of them, by the time I reach them, have the sickness Marit calls *the summer.*

It's cholera. I've seen enough of it in textbooks to know, and I've seen the goat stream in Shingle. On the walk north, Tern Hollow drank from every stream they passed. Somewhere along the way, one of them was bad.

By morning, it's eleven sick. By the next evening, thirty-four.

I've never treated a patient in my life. I worked in pharma. I tested compounds on cell cultures. I know how cholera kills: it doesn't poison you, it drains you. You lose water faster than you can drink it, and your blood thickens, and your heart stops. I also know that most of the deaths are preventable with things so cheap and simple it's almost insulting. Clean water. Salt. Sugar.

So that's what we do.

I put up a second tent at the far end of the plateau, downwind and downhill of everything, and I move the sick into it. I put a chlorine footbath at the door and a bucket of chlorinated water beside every cot. Marit's daughters and three women from Kettle Point mix oral rehydration solution by the barrel, a precise amount of salt and sugar in clean water, and carry cups of it from cot to cot, all day and all night, making every patient drink, and drink, and drink. For the ones too weak to drink, I have antibiotics and IV fluids from Fernholt's last order, which I'm suddenly very glad I asked for.

I don't sleep for three days. I don't think about it. I think about the next cot.

On the fourth day, the new cases stop.

On the fifth, the first patients walk out of the tent on their own feet.

Thirty-four people got sick. Thirty-three of them live.

The one who doesn't is an old man from Tern Hollow called Fenn, who was already weak when he arrived, and who died on the second night, holding his granddaughter's hand, while I was two cots away trying to get a line into a child's arm. I didn't even know he was going until he'd gone.

I sit with his granddaughter for an hour afterward. I don't know what to say to her. There isn't anything. So I just sit.

---

That evening, I sit on the edge of the cliff with a cup of tea and Marit, looking down at the lit windows. There are a lot of them now.

*They're calling you something,* Marit says.

"Please don't tell me it's a title."

*Doctor.*

I close my eyes. "I'm not that kind of doctor. I have a PhD in molecular biology. I've never been to medical school."

Marit shrugs, which is what she does instead of arguing. *Thirty-three people are alive who'd be dead in Tern Hollow. They don't care what school you went to.*

"Fenn."

*Fenn was eighty-one and walked two hundred kilometers.* She sips her tea. *In the old days, we'd have lost half of them. You lost one. Accept the name. You're a doctor now.*

I sit with that for a long time.

Here's what I think about, on the edge of that cliff, watching the windows.

Angline shot my boss in front of me. She set my hand on fire. She's the reason my face was on the national news beside the words *murder* and *terrorism,* and the reason I can never go home. By any reasonable measure, she's the worst thing that has ever happened to me.

She's also the reason I get to build something.

For years before she walked into that lab with a pistol, first in pharma and then for Alphonseo, I tested compounds on cells and wrote reports nobody read. I was good at it. It never mattered. If I had an idea, it went into a proposal, and the proposal went to a committee, and the committee said no, or said yes two years later, and either way I never saw what it did for anyone. Here, I had an idea about where the latrines should go on a Tuesday, and by Friday, they were there. I changed three things in the design of a farm, and it's rising. I mixed salt and sugar into clean water, and thirty-three people are alive. I've never been able to see the other end of my own work before. Now I'm sitting on a cliff above it.

Angline would say she did it because it was the optimal path. She'd make a table.

I didn't make a table. I just did it.

I don't know yet what that says about either of us.

### Angline

The Houses of Pinnacle don't meet in anyone's tower.

I learn this from Oren, on the stairs of the Lamp Hall, when I ask which tower I'll be climbing. He looks at me as if I've suggested the Lady bathe in the public fountain.

*The Compact,* he says. *No House climbs another's stairs to bargain. They meet at the Stake Hall.*

The Stake Hall is at the very bottom of Pinnacle, at the center of the city, where four of the oldest towers lean together over a small square. It's the only important building I've seen in this world at ground level. Low and square and very old, built of black stone, with a single round room inside and three doors, one for each House, each carved with its crest.

There's no fourth door. I come in through Verdane's, a step behind the Lady, and I notice, and I don't like it.

---

The round room has a round table and a hole in the roof, directly above it, open to the sky. Thirty stories up, a small disk of daylight. Oren tells me, quietly, that it's so no House can sit higher than another. Everyone sits under the same patch of sky.

The Lady of Verdane sits at her place, upright and still. The Lady of Ochre sits at hers, a round, cheerful, sharp-eyed woman in her fifties with honey-colored rings on every finger, who greets me like an old friend and watches me like a merchant. And the Lord of Sable sits at his, and waits for everyone to settle, and speaks first.

*I've made a list,* he says.

He has. He unrolls it on the table: a long sheet of bamboo paper, numbers in neat columns. I recognize the shape of it immediately. I've made a hundred like it.

*Ruler's tithe to the three Houses,* he reads. *Twelve and a half thousand motes a day. More than half of each House's new income, for a year now.* He glances at the other two. *Ruler's lamps in forty-four thousand homes. The lower cities of all three Houses. If they go dark, I don't think any of us want to be in our towers when it happens.*

The Lady of Ochre stops smiling.

*Ruler's steel. Nine hundred tonnes a day, from a ridge the Houses once went to war over. Ruler's farms. Fifteen, on our land, under our licenses, printing more than all our fields together.* He looks up at me. *And now Ruler's road. Four hundred kilometers of iron, from a coast no House holds, where she keeps a village that calls her by her name like a title, and a door to wherever she comes from. Through the fields of all three Houses. To here.*

He rolls up the list.

*My ladies,* he says, *we have spent a year taking this woman's tithe and calling it tribute. I've done the sums. It isn't tribute. It's a leash. And every month, it gets shorter.*

---

The silence is long. The disk of sky overhead is very blue.

I'll give him this: it's a very good speech. It's what I'd have said in his place. It is, almost word for word, what I said to Bethany in the command room at Fernholt, standing in front of a chart of House income with my tithe climbing through it like a vine.

He's read my ledger without ever seeing it. I find I respect him more than I'd like to, again.

"What do you want?" I ask.

It's still the only question.

He has a list for that too. The coast under a House charter, which is to say Sable's, since Sable sent the last collector to it. A cap on my farms. House guards at every station on my road. And my steel, sold only to the Houses, at a price the Houses set.

The Lady of Ochre nods along to every item. The Lady of Verdane says nothing at all.

I let him finish. Then I stand, because I think better standing, and because I'm the only person in the room without a chair that's been carved for me.

"My lord is right," I say. "It is a leash. I made it. I'd like to point out, though, that it has two ends."

They look at me.

"If my lamps go dark, your lower cities riot. That's true. If my tithe stops, you'll all be poorer. That's also true. But if you close your fields to my road, it isn't me who loses most. I'll build around you. It'll take longer. It'll cost me more. My farms will move to the coast, where no House holds anything, and the Glass keeps the tax collectors out. And your lower cities will watch the light go east."

The Lord of Sable's jaw tightens. He knows it's true. He did the sums.

"Or," I say, "you can have what the road carries."

I tell them. Free carriage for the Houses' own goods: grain, cloth, husk-wood, anything they want moved, anywhere the rail goes, a day's journey in an hour. And power, to every House seat.

"Every tower in Pinnacle burns motes just to be a tower," I say. "Porters lifting people and water up a thousand steps, a mote a floor. Lamp-motes in every corridor. Heat in winter. You pay for all of it, every day, out of the same crystals you print your money with." I look at the Lady of Verdane. "Your lift to the Lamp Hall alone, my lady, costs your House more each year than an entire village on my coast. I'll do all of it with electricity. Lifts, light, pumps, heat. For nothing."

The Lady of Verdane turns her head, very slowly, and looks at me.

She's run the arithmetic of her own tower every morning for sixty years. Oren keeps the ledger. I've seen it. Moving a House up and down its own tower is one of the largest lines in it.

---

It takes three days.

Three days of numbers, under the patch of sky, with Oren and two other clerks writing it all down. The Lady of Ochre wants her grain carried free and her quarter lit first. The Lady of Verdane wants her tower powered first. And the Lord of Sable wants something that costs me, because he knows, and I know, that a deal that costs me nothing is a deal he's lost.

They never threaten me. Not once, in three days. The Houses have been civil to one another for five hundred years, and they're very good at it. They simply mention things.

The Lady of Ochre mentions, over honey cakes on the second morning, that the Red Ridge lease is for ninety-nine years, which is a very long time, and that leases in the Green have occasionally been broken when a lessee's circumstances changed. She smiles as she says it.

The Lord of Sable mentions, while we argue about stations, that four hundred kilometers is a long way to watch. Fields burn in dry summers. Wild things slip through gaps in ward-posts. Rail, when you think about it, is only iron lying in the grass. He says this in the tone of a man worried on my behalf.

The Lady of Verdane mentions nothing at all. She doesn't need to. My first farm stands on her land, inside her ward-posts, a morning's walk from her tower and the Shades who live in it.

I count it up that night, alone in the rooms Oren has found me. Fifteen farms, all on House land. A steel mill on a leased hill. A copper mine, a lithium pan, three reactors, four hundred kilometers of track. Against that, I have Billy, a glove, some heat discs, a radar set, and a great many motes. I can see a Shade coming. I can't be everywhere it might come to.

They could take it apart. Not all of it, not easily, not without cost, but they could. They're polite because they're civil, and because I'm rich, and because they want what I sell. Not because they can't.

I'm not ready to find out what happens if they stop being polite. Not yet.

So I'll concede. And I'll remember. I add three new names to a ledger that already has a colonel and a government in it, and I go to sleep.

In the end, this is what we sign.

The road crosses the Houses' fields along the harvest roads, with poles for power beside it. Houses' goods ride free. Every seat gets power: lifts, light, pumps and heat. I build no more farms on House land beyond the fifteen already standing. The coast stays outside the Compact: no House will charter it, and neither, formally, will I.

And the first three farms, Verdane's, Sable's, and Ochre's, the ones I built when I had only a flashlight and a plan, pass to the Houses whose ground they stand on. Complete. Running. With their research chambers, their controllers, and a season of my people training theirs in how every floor works.

That's the price. It's a real one. Those three farms were my first hundred and twenty-six thousand motes a day. More than that, they're the design. Every House harvest-master who walks those floors will understand them in a year, and the Houses have five centuries of field lore I'll never match. In five years, there'll be towers in every House field in the Green. My monopoly on the vertical farm ends the moment I sign.

I sign anyway. I sign with a steady hand and my best smile, because the deal is good, and because the coast is outside all of it, and because I've learned that the way you pay a real price is gracefully, so that nobody gets the satisfaction of watching it hurt.

---

Afterward, in the square outside the Stake Hall, the Lady of Verdane stops beside me while her bearers bring her chair.

*You gave us your farms,* she says.

"I did."

*You didn't argue very hard.*

"It was a fair price."

She looks at me with those pale, still eyes.

*It was a clever price,* she says. *You gave us the thing we'll spend years learning to use, and kept the doors we don't understand.*

I don't answer. I've learned that when someone tells you something you didn't think they knew, the best response is to learn from it, not deny it.

She settles into her chair. Her bearers lift her.

*The Lord of Sable is counting your doors, Ruler,* she says, as they carry her away toward her tower and its very expensive porters. *So am I.*

### Bethany

I know it's a bad idea. I want that on the record, since Angline likes things on the record.

I know that my mother's phone is almost certainly being watched. I know that a detective with a very polite manner and a gray suit has almost certainly already been to see her. I know that every time a signal leaves a phone, it touches a tower, and the tower keeps a log, and logs are the kind of thing people like Nkemelu read for fun.

I know all of it, and on the forty-ninth night after the train left, I go through the door anyway.

---

The salt mine at night is the quietest place I have ever been. The Reach door opens into a gallery two hundred meters underground, walls of gray-white salt glittering in the floodlights, a railway running down the middle of it toward the shaft. I walk the rails to the lift. I ride it up in the dark, alone, for four minutes.

At the top, there's a yard, a chain-link fence, and a gate, and beyond the gate, a road through low wet hills under a sky full of real clouds. Earth clouds. It's raining softly. I stand in it for a minute with my face turned up, because I haven't felt Earth rain in a year and a half.

Then I take the phone out of my pocket.

It's a cheap one. I ordered it myself, through Fernholt, in a box of lab supplies, without telling anyone. It has never been switched on. It has one number in it, which I didn't need to save, because I've known it since I was six.

I tell myself: three minutes. Less. Say you're alive, say you're safe, hang up. Three minutes is nothing.

I switch it on. I dial.

It rings four times.

*"Hello?"*

She sounds older. That's the first thing. Eighteen months, and she sounds ten years older.

"Mum," I say. "It's me."

There's a silence on the line so long I think the call has dropped. Then a sound I've never heard my mother make, somewhere between a laugh and something breaking.

*"Bethany."*

"I'm okay. I'm safe. I can't tell you where I am, and I can't talk long."

*"They said—the television said—"*

"I know what they said."

*"A detective came. A woman. Very polite. She left a card."* A breath. *"She asked if I'd heard from you. I said no. I said you'd never do those things. Beth, you didn't do those things."*

I close my eyes. The rain runs down my neck into my collar.

I think about Alphonseo on the lab floor. The lava. Grabbing one foot.

"Some of them," I say. "Not the way they said."

The silence again. I can hear her breathing. I can hear the kitchen clock behind her, the one that's always been four minutes fast, that she's never fixed.

*"Is she making you do this? That woman? The one with you in the pictures?"*

It's a fair question. Eighteen months ago, the answer would have been yes. She set me on fire. She drew a sad face at my feet with a cannon. She used me to find out whether a crystal would get me nuked.

"No," I say. "Not anymore."

*"Then why—"*

"Because I'm building something." It comes out before I've decided to say it. "Mum, I'm a doctor now. A real one, sort of. There are people alive because of me. A lot of them. I'm building something. I'm helping build a town. It has clean water. Do you know how many people in the world don't have clean water?"

*"You were always a doctor,"* she says, and her voice cracks right down the middle. *"You have the certificate. It's on my fridge."*

I laugh. I can't help it. I'm standing in the rain outside a salt mine in a hill in the middle of nowhere, wanted in three countries, crying and laughing at the same time about a certificate on a fridge.

I look at the phone's screen. Two minutes, forty seconds.

"I have to go."

*"Will you call again?"*

"I don't know." I owe her the truth. "Probably not for a long time. It's not safe. For me or for you."

*"Beth—"*

"I love you. Fix the kitchen clock."

I hang up at two minutes fifty-one.

---

I take the battery out. I snap the SIM card in half, and then in half again. I walk back into the yard, to the crusher by the shaft head where they used to grind salt, and I drop the pieces in, and I press the button, and I listen to the machine turn my mother's voice into dust.

Then I ride the lift down, two hundred meters, four minutes, in the dark.

Three minutes, I tell myself on the way down. Less than three. It was nothing.

I walk back along the rails through the glittering salt, and through the door, and out onto the cliff top above Shingle, where it's warm and dry and the windows of fourteen hundred people glow below me in the dark.

I don't tell anyone where I've been.

It was nothing.

### Angline

I come home to the coast on an electric train.

While I was climbing no stairs at all and sitting under a hole in a roof, the tritium gauge beside *Eleven Months* crossed the line I'd drawn on it in marker pen four months ago. The first full-size reactor came up to power a week later: three hundred megawatts, clean and steady, spinning a turbine the size of a house. I named it *Personal Use*, after the spy satellite.

The wires went up along the wild sixty kilometers while I was gone, strung pole to pole by rune lifters, and the diesel has been retired to shunting duty at the railhead, where it sulks. The electric locomotive that carries me east is silent except for the hum of its motors and the singing of the wire overhead, and I sit in the cab the whole way with my hand flat on the control desk, feeling the magnets spin.

It's the most peaceful journey I've ever taken.

---

I don't recognize the cliff top.

I left a door, a camp, a swap station and a clinic tent. I come back to a town. Rows of steel cabins, still painted in the faded colors of whatever shipping lines owned them before, laid out across the plateau in neat streets with gravel paths between them. A second water tower. Latrine blocks, downhill and downwind, which I note with professional approval. A school, in two containers bolted together, with children's drawings taped to the windows. Smoke rising cleanly from flues. Washing on lines. And people, a great many people, who stop what they're doing to watch the train come in, and then go back to it, because trains are ordinary here now.

On the far side of the plateau, where I had marked a site for the first coastal farm and left instructions to wait for me, the foundation has already been poured, and the first two floors of frame are standing.

I find Bethany in the clinic, which is no longer a tent. It's four containers in a square around a small courtyard, with a sign over the entrance in the coast dialect. Billy translates it for me as I walk in.

DOCTOR.

She's writing in her notebook. She looks up when I come in, and for a moment her face does something complicated, and then it settles into its usual expression, the tired, dry, faintly exasperated one I've grown used to.

"You're back," she says.

"I'm back." I sit down across from her. "Report."

She hands me the notebook.

---

I read it all. It takes an hour. She sits and waits while I do, the way I used to make people wait while I read their papers in the lab.

Fourteen hundred and sixty people. Four hundred and twelve cabins. Two springs, two filters, two dosers, chlorine checked at the tap every morning, in her handwriting, every single day. A cholera outbreak: thirty-four cases, thirty-three recoveries. Fenn, eighty-one, of Tern Hollow. A school. A census of every village along the coast to the southern headlands, with populations, boats, and how far along the list of *walking to Shingle* each one is. And the farm: kelp-fed yeast floors on the bottom, larvae above, and three changes to my design, each with a note explaining why, each of which I'd have made myself if I'd been a biologist.

I close the notebook.

"I made a model of this," I say. "Before I left. What I expected the coast would look like when I got back."

"And?"

"You exceeded it. By a lot." I set the notebook down very precisely on the table. "The farm. The water. The outbreak. Thirty-three out of thirty-four, with no hospital and salt and sugar. That's better than some Earth hospitals would have done."

She doesn't say anything.

"You're promoted," I say.

"To what?"

"This. All of it. The coast. Permanently. I'll be at the railhead, and in Pinnacle, and on Earth, and wherever else. This is yours." I think about it. "You'll need a title."

"I have one." She glances at the sign over the door. "Doctor is fine."

"Director of Everything East."

"*Doctor* is fine, Angline."

"Doctor Director."

She laughs. Actually laughs, tired and helpless, with her head in her hands. It's a good sound. I realize I haven't heard it very often.

Then I tell her about the Stake Hall, and the things the Houses mentioned, and the price. Three farms, handed over, running, with their secrets.

"You gave them the farms," she says slowly. "They'll copy them."

"They will. In five years, every House field will have a tower in it." I nod out the clinic window, toward the humming door on the cliff top, the kelp lines in the bay, the dark wild bamboo beyond the ward-posts. "That's why everything important is here from now on. The farms, the harbor, the base. Nothing east of the ward-posts stands on anyone's land. Nobody can mention anything to me about it."

She's quiet for a moment.

"And the salt mine?"

"If anyone finds it, I close the door and open another somewhere else." I shrug. "Tearing a new hole is the hard part. It costs a season and thirty thousand motes. That's expensive, not fatal. Nobody on Earth knows it's anything but a hole full of salt."

---

Billy's tone sounds in my ear at 9:40 that night.

We're sitting outside the clinic in the dark with tea, watching the lit windows of the town. I almost ignore it. Then I see which camera it is, and I sit up.

The salt mine's gate. Earth side, on the surface, two hundred meters above the gallery where the door opens.

The feed comes up on my phone: gray, grainy, rain streaking across the lens. The chain-link fence. The gate, with its sign: KESSEL MINERAL ARCHIVE, a company so boring that I'm proud of it every time I read it. The wet road beyond it.

And on the road, parked with its lights off, a small gray car.

A woman is standing beside it under an umbrella. Forties. Gray suit. Short hair. She's taking photographs of the sign. Then the fence. Then the shaft head behind it, where the lift comes up. Then, for a long moment, the crusher by the shaft head, where they used to grind salt.

She lowers the camera and stands there in the rain, looking at the gate, the way she once stood in my lab, looking at the cover of a pool.

"Nkemelu," I say softly.

I don't know how. Fernholt bought nothing for the salt mine in its own name. Kessel has no link to me that I haven't buried under three companies. I've been careful. I'm always careful.

I turn to Bethany to say so.

She's gone white. Not the white of someone surprised. The white of someone who has just seen something she's been waiting for, and hoping, every day for a week, would never come.

She puts down her tea.

"Angline," she says. "I need to tell you something."

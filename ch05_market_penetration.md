# Chapter 5: Market Penetration

I have no projector. I have no slides. I have a notebook, a translator in my ear, and a stone shaft full of peasants who have all stopped walking to watch a House collector talk to a woman in a strange coat.

It will do. The great orators of history rarely had projectors either, and look how well they managed.

"I am Angline Ruler," I say, and Billy renders it in the local tongue a half-beat behind me, in my own voice, which is the correct voice for it. "I come from very far away. Farther than your maps go. And where I come from, light does not drink."

The collector waits. He is a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the patience of someone who is paid by the hour. The two guards behind him shift their spears.

"I have come to your city," I continue, "to bring it out of the dark. To lift up the low and — "

*Is it for sale?* the collector says.

I stop.

I have prepared, in my head, a nine-part monologue. It covers destiny, the moral arc of history, and my personal excellence, in that order. It has a callback in part seven.

"Yes," I say. "Obviously."

He nods, as though that was the only part of the speech he needed, and clicks the flashlight off and on again. *My name is Oren. I collect for House Verdane.* He taps the bamboo leaf on his shoulder. *The gate guard tried to sell this to a pawnbroker for three motes. The pawnbroker is a cousin of mine. Where is the mote?*

"There isn't one."

*Then what does it drink?*

"Electricity."

I expect incomprehension. Billy had no word for electricity in six hours of market chatter, so I brace myself to explain charge, current, and the lightning they must surely have here, from the beginning.

Oren frowns. Then he says a word Billy doesn't know, and when I ask him to describe it, he rubs his sleeve fast against his other arm and mimes a little snap between his fingers.

*Spark-stuff,* he says. *Scholars at the Lyceum play with it. Copper coils, jars that bite you. There's an old man who spins a wheel of lodestone in a cage of wire and makes a needle twitch.* He shrugs. *A toy. For students.*

I stare at him.

"You spin a magnet in a coil of wire," I say slowly, "and it makes current. You *know* that."

*Some people know it.*

"And you don't *use* it?"

He gives me the look you give a child who has asked why the sky doesn't fall. *To spin the wheel you need a man, or a mote. If you have a mote, why would you spin a wheel to make spark-stuff to make light? Just make light.* He holds up the flashlight. *Every step costs. The wheel wastes. The wire wastes. Mote to light is one step.*

I have to lean on the wall.

Bethany touches my arm. "Are you okay?"

"They found it," I whisper. "Bethany. They found electromagnetic induction, the single most important discovery in the history of the species, and they looked at it and said, *eh, too many steps.* They had the magnet in their hands and they *didn't spin it.*"

"You're shaking."

"I'm *grieving.*"

And underneath the grief, rising fast, something much better. He's right. For them, he's entirely right. When your fuel is a crystal you can touch to a jar and get water, a turbine is an absurd detour. Every conversion step leaks energy, and they already have a one-step machine. Electricity never paid for itself here, so nobody ever built the boilers, the dynamos, the grid, the thousand boring things that make it cheap.

Which means the moment someone makes it cheap, it's not a toy anymore.

It's a competing currency.

"The light is for sale," I tell Oren. "This one, today. More later. Many more."

*Thirty motes.*

Thirty motes is ten days of food. He's offering me ten days of a laborer's life for a hardware-store flashlight. A small voice in my head reports this to my pride, and my pride is not satisfied.

"It has never needed a mote. It never will. Every night your House burns motes on the Leaf bridges, and this does the same job for nothing." I don't mention the battery. Monologue responsibly. "Ninety."

*Forty.*

"Seventy-five, and you tell me who at the Lyceum spins the wheel."

He considers me for a long moment. Then he takes a pouch from his belt and counts sixty glowing chips into my hand. Warm. Faintly tingling. The tingle climbs my wrist like it's pleased to meet me.

*Sixty,* he says. *And the old man's name is Brask. He's mad. You'll like him.* He tucks the flashlight into his coat, very carefully. *My House will want to talk to you, Angline Ruler from far away.*

"Your House," I say, "will be first in line."

He goes. The crowd in the shaft breathes out and starts moving again. I stand there with sixty motes in my palm, twenty days of food, a small fortune for anyone on this street, and I close my hand around them and feel, for the first time since the portal, rich in the correct currency.

"You sold a flashlight," Bethany says, "for three weeks' wages."

"Twenty days," I say. "Be precise. And I sold a *sample.*"

---

A sample is worthless without a market, and a market is worthless unless you understand it. Before I build anything, I need to know what these people want badly enough to pay for it.

Market research, it turns out, is a job for Bethany.

This is not a demotion. It is specialization. My accent in the local tongue makes people wince, and I have been told, by several former colleagues and one HR department, that I have a manner that makes people feel audited. Bethany, on the other hand, has a burned hand and the face of someone who has had a very long week. Everyone in the lower tiers of Pinnacle has had a very long week. They trust her immediately.

So she asks, and Billy translates, and I stand three steps behind with the notebook.

She asks a cobbler, a laundress, two porters, a woman frying dough, a grandmother on a stoop, and a boy of maybe twelve who wants to know if the coat is for sale. It isn't. She asks each of them the same things. What do you spend your motes on? What do you go without? If you had a few more motes a week, what would you buy?

The answers are remarkably consistent. By full dark, I have a spreadsheet, in pencil.

| Household of four, lower tier | Motes per week |
| --- | --- |
| Food | 84 |
| Water (condensed, bought by the jar) | 5 |
| Lifting (porters, to anything above the fourth floor) | 3 |
| Cooking heat | 4 |
| Light | 1, maybe 2 |
| House tithe | "Whatever Oren says" |

The light line is the interesting one. Not because it's big, but because it's so small. Light, it turns out, is the most expensive thing you can ask a mote to do. The same chip that fills a water jar, or lifts a porter a whole floor, gives barely twenty minutes of dim blue light. Nobody down here can spare more than one a week. Twenty minutes. Per week. The laundress uses hers on the night before market to sort the week's washing. The grandmother saves hers for when someone is sick. The boy says, with a shrug that breaks something small and useless in my chest, that he has never read at night in his life.

If you had a few more motes, what would you buy?

*Light.* The cobbler. *Light.* The laundress. *A lift to the top floor, once, to see.* The grandmother. *Light.* Both porters.

It is not a market. It's a famine.

"They want to be able to see," Bethany says quietly, when we step away. "That's it. That's the whole wish."

"That," I say, writing, "is the most beautiful demand curve I have ever seen."

She gives me a look. I let her have it. She's not wrong that it's sad. She's just wrong that sad and profitable are opposites.

---

The research is cheap, but I believe in testing a market with real product. Fortunately, my coat is a warehouse.

The lighter goes first. The woman frying dough uses a mote-spark every morning to light her stove, and when I flick the lighter in front of her, she makes the same face the gate guard made. It holds a few thousand lights. I don't tell her that. She pays twenty-five motes, which is more than she makes in a day, and immediately starts lighting things just to watch them catch.

The glow sticks go next. I have a pack of six in an inside pocket, left over from a field exercise. A servant in a clean pale tunic, clearly sent down from the upper bridges on an errand, stops to watch me crack one, and when it blooms green in my hand, he forgets his errand entirely. They last a night and then they die, I explain. Beautifully. For parties.

He buys all six at ten motes each. The rich will pay for anything that ends.

Then Bethany does something I did not authorize.

She reaches into her lab coat, takes out a small plastic calculator, the kind that comes free with a bank account, and shows the cobbler how it adds. The numbers come up on the gray screen. He stares at it. She tilts it toward the last light from above, and when she covers the little strip along the top with her thumb, the screen fades.

"Bethany," I say. "Is that a *solar* calculator?"

"It's from my bank."

"You brought *solar* to a new world."

"It was in my pocket, Angline."

The cobbler pays forty motes for it. Forty. More than the lighter. I have never been so betrayed by a market.

"The customer is wrong," I tell Bethany, as she pockets her motes. "He will learn. Solar panels on a world with a hundred-meter canopy. He's going to be reading in the dark by Tuesday."

"I'm keeping these."

"You're keeping *thirty.* Ten is the franchise fee."

She hands me ten motes with the expression of someone filing it away for later. I take them anyway. Business is business, and heresy has a price.

---

The last stop is a cartographer.

His stall is a narrow slot between two towers, walls papered with maps, most of them of Pinnacle itself, drawn in cross-section like an anthill. But in the back, under a lamp-mote he is clearly burning to show off his stock, hangs a bigger one. The whole region, painted on bamboo paper.

The Green, it's called. The farm we drove through isn't a farm. It's a sea. It covers the whole map, stroke after stroke of painted green, and dotted through it like islands are cities. I count them twice. Forty-one. Roads run between them in straight lines, the gaps we saw from the drones. At the far eastern edge, the green stops, and there's a wide band of blue labeled *the Brine.*

An ocean.

"Fifteen motes," says the cartographer.

I pay twenty and tell him to keep the lamp burning while I look. He is delighted. He should be. I've just bought the most important document in this world.

The map has no scale bar, but it doesn't need one. Pinnacle is drawn as a circle about the width of my thumbnail, and I know from the drones that Pinnacle is three kilometers across. One thumbnail, three kilometers. Everything else follows. The nearest city is forty kilometers north. The Brine is four hundred east. The Green is roughly the size of a large European country, and it's all farm.

Bethany leans in. "You're doing math."

"I'm doing *market sizing.*"

I count towers from memory. I estimate floors, rooms per floor, people per room. Pinnacle holds perhaps two hundred thousand people. Call it fifty thousand households. Fifty thousand households each spending one mote a week on light, and each wanting three, four, *ten*.

Then multiply by forty-one cities.

I write the number down. I look at it. I draw a box around it. I draw a second box around the first box.

"Bethany," I say. "We need to go home."

"*Home* home?"

"I need a shower," I say, "and a supply chain."

---

Billy is exactly where we left him, sulking in the shadows of the tree line with four drones hovering over him like anxious birds. Nothing has tried to climb him. I'm almost disappointed on his behalf.

It's full night now. Behind us, Pinnacle is a column of darkness with a pale crown of light at the top, exactly like a matchstick that someone has lit upside down.

I climb in, pull down the main display, and plug in my laptop.

"No," Bethany says.

"You don't know what I'm doing."

"You made *slides.* When did you make slides?"

"While you were negotiating my calculator franchise. Sit."

She sits. She's tired enough that sitting wins every argument. I dim the cabin lights for atmosphere. The first slide comes up on Billy's screen, which is smaller than the one in the lab, but brighter, and armored.

---

LADY AND APC

*presented by ANGLINE RULER*

---

"Lady and APC," I begin. "Today we will be covering: How I Will Own the Night. Please hold your questions to the end."

Click.

**PHASE ONE: LIGHT, BY THE WEEK.**

"They spend a mote a week on twenty minutes of blue light. I will charge them a mote a week for light every night. A lamp, a battery, and a swap station on every street. They bring the dead battery in, they get a full one out, they pay one mote. I don't sell them lamps. I sell them *evenings.*"

"A subscription," Bethany says. "You're bringing subscriptions to a new world."

"I'm bringing *recurring revenue.* Capitalism's greatest invention after the lever."

Click.

**PHASE TWO: SPIN THE MAGNETS.**

The slide is a photograph of a steam turbine. It is my favorite photograph. I have it framed at home.

"Billy's reactor can't power a city, and I need him mobile. So we need fuel. And this world is *drowning* in fuel." I pull up a drone image of the cut rows from our first day, the gray stalks lying where they fell. "The Houses cut these down, drain them into crystals, and leave the corpses to rot. They think it's waste." I click, and my math appears. "One fallen stalk. Four meters across, a hundred long, a hollow wall about thirty centimeters thick. Call it two hundred and forty tonnes of dry plant. Dry wood burns at about sixteen megajoules a kilogram. That's roughly a thousand megawatt-hours of heat. Turn a third of that into electricity in a decent turbine—"

"—and you get what?"

"Three hundred megawatt-hours. One dead plant runs Billy's reactor at full power for two weeks. And they leave *thousands* of them lying on the ground."

Bethany looks at the photo of the gray stalks for a long moment. "They're throwing away power plants."

"They're throwing away *fuel.* The power plant is the part I bring." I tap the turbine lovingly. "Boilers. Turbines. Magnets. Spinning. At last."

"You could just put solar panels on the—"

"Click," I say loudly, and click.

**PHASE THREE: EVERYTHING ELSE THEY PAY FOR.**

"Once there's a grid, the rest of their spreadsheet falls. Electric pumps: water on every floor, no condensing jar. Electric lifts: nobody pays a porter to see the top floor. Refrigeration: the meat stops rotting, and so does their need for Hessen rot. Every line on that family's budget that says *mote*, I replace with a line that says *Angline.*"

Click.

**PHASE FOUR: MONETARY POLICY.**

This slide is plain black text on white. Some ideas don't need decoration.

"The Houses own the bamboo. The bamboo makes the crystals. The crystals are the money. That's their whole power: they don't collect the wealth, they *print* it. Every mote in Pinnacle is a House mote." I let it sit. "But a mote is only worth what it can buy. Mostly, it buys light, water, heat and motion. When I sell all four for less, every mote in this world is worth less, and every unit of power I make is worth more. The Houses print money. I will own what the money is *for.*"

The cabin is very quiet. One of the drones outside adjusts its hover.

"And eventually," I say, gently, "they'll pay their tithe to me."

Click.

**PHASE FIVE: WE WILL BE THE ARISTOCRACY.**

It's the same font as the lab. Continuity matters.

"Forty-one cities," I say. "Eight million people, give or take. All in the dark." I click to the last slide: the cartographer's map, photographed, with a red circle around the Brine. "And when the Houses realize what's happening, and they will, we'll have somewhere to go that they can't follow. Billy is amphibious. The ocean is four hundred kilometers east."

"And if they follow us anyway?"

"Then," I say, with real hope, "I will finally have a worthy opponent. And I have a great deal of C4."

Bethany is quiet for a while. The screen lights her face, pale and blue, the same color as a lamp-mote.

"The boy," she says. "The one who's never read at night. He'd get a lamp?"

"For one mote a week. Same as he pays now."

"So you're going to do something good."

"I'm going to do something *enormously profitable*," I say, "that has good side effects. Don't make it weird."

She looks at me for a long moment, with an expression I can't quite classify. Then she nods slowly.

"What do you need to start?"

"LEDs by the container. Batteries by the ton. Copper, a turbine, boilers, and enough steel to make the Houses nervous." I close the laptop. "And a trailer. I will *finally* get a trailer."

"Great plan, Ms. Ruler," Bethany says. "Great plan."

This time, I think she might mean it.

---

We drive through the night, following the drone map back down the rows. The portal is exactly where we left it, a shimmering circle standing in a gap between two stalks, indifferent to us as ever. I appreciate that about it. It doesn't need anything from me.

Billy rolls through at a sensible speed, and the world turns inside out, and then we're in sub-basement four, under fluorescent lights, surrounded by the smell of concrete and a faint, unmistakable note of solvent from the lab three floors up.

Earth. It's Saturday, 4:12 in the morning, according to Billy's clock. The building is empty. Nobody has come looking for us, because as far as anybody knows, nobody left.

There is, of course, one outstanding item.

"Alphonseo," Bethany says, as we ride the freight elevator up. She says it the way you mention a dentist appointment you've been avoiding.

"Handled."

"How is he *handled*? He's on the lab floor. We left him on the lab floor. We *stepped over* him."

"On the morning I shot him," I say, "I sent an email from his account to the whole department. He's at a conference in Helsinki, and he's extended his trip for some fjord time."

"Finland doesn't really have fjords."

"Nobody in this department knows that. Nobody ever questions Helsinki."

The elevator dings. The lab is exactly as we left it: long, irregular, smelling of solvent and ambition, the projector still aimed at the screen. And there on the floor is Alphonseo, looking considerably worse than he did last time and not at all like a man enjoying fjords.

I walk to the internal window and press the button beside it. In the next room, the cover over the pool slides back with a low industrial hum, and orange light floods through the glass. The lava is bubbling contentedly. It has been costing us four thousand a month to keep it that way.

Bethany looks at the lava. Then at Alphonseo. Then at me.

"*That's* what the lava pool was for."

"The lava pool is for many things. It's a threat, a centerpiece, a conversation starter, and a waste disposal solution." I pull on a pair of heavy gloves from the rack. "Good design has more than one purpose. Grab his feet."

"I have a *burned hand.*"

"Then grab one foot."

She grabs one foot.

I won't describe the rest. It is not a pleasant process, and it isn't quick, and afterward we both stand at the glass for a while in silence, watching the surface of the lava settle back into a smooth, glowing calm. The world is now one ex-boss lighter, and our secrets are tidy.

"Died of something adjacent to natural causes," Bethany says finally. "And then disposed of by something *entirely* natural. Volcanic."

I look at her with real pride. "You're learning."

"I'm *tired.*"

"Same thing, at the start."

---

In the parking garage, before we split up, I give her a first aid kit from Billy's stores and my best dermatologist's number, and I tell her to be back Monday at eight.

She looks at the car keys in her hand for a long moment. I know what she's thinking. She is on Earth. She has her own car. She could drive to the police, or the airport, or her mother's house, and there is nothing in Pinnacle holding her except a subscription business and a boy who's never read at night.

"You could run," I say. It's only fair to acknowledge it.

"I know."

"I'd find you."

"I know that too." She opens her car door. "I'm going to shower, sleep for fourteen hours, and eat something that isn't a ration. Then I'll be here Monday." She hesitates. "Eight-thirty."

I let her have the half hour. Good managers pick their battles.

---

My apartment is on the top floor of a building I chose for its view and its load-bearing capacity. I take the stairs. Elevators are for people who don't have to be anywhere.

I drop my coat on the chair by the door. It lands with a heavy thud of C4, the soft clink of exactly one hundred and thirty-five motes, and the crinkle of the map. I lay the motes out on the kitchen counter in rows of ten, under the pendant light. They glow very faintly, pale and cold, the most beautiful money I have ever earned.

Then, at last, the shower.

There is a belief, common among people who have never achieved it, that effortless beauty is effortless. It isn't. It takes effort to look effortlessly beautiful. It takes a forty-minute shower, three different cleansers, a serum I had synthesized to my own specifications, a conditioner that costs more than Bethany's car payment, and a moisturizer applied in upward strokes, never downward, because gravity is already doing enough.

I emerge an hour later, flushed and gleaming, and review myself in the mirror, and find that I am, as always, a triumph.

I get into bed. Silk sheets. Blackout curtains. I lie in the dark and think about forty-one cities with the lights off.

Then I sit up, turn on the lamp, grab the notebook from my bedside, and begin the purchase order.

*LEDs: 200,000 units.*

*Batteries, rechargeable: 500,000.*

*Steam turbine, secondhand, industrial: 1.*

*Trailer: 1. Large.*

I think about it, and cross out the last line.

*Trailer: 3.*

Then I turn out the light, and I sleep like someone who has never once been wrong.

## Interlude

The stairs to the top of House Verdane's tower number one thousand and eight. I have climbed them twice a week for eleven years, and I have never once been allowed to take the lift.

Tonight I climb them with the thing in my coat, and I count every step, because counting keeps my hands from shaking.

The Lady receives me in the Lamp Hall. It is the brightest room in Pinnacle. The crystal at its center is taller than I am, and it hums, and the light it gives off is so pure and cold that the Lady's face looks carved from ice. She doesn't look up from her ledger when I kneel.

"The tithe is light this week," she says.

"Yes, my lady. There's a reason." I take the thing out of my coat and hold it up. "A woman came in at the south gate. She calls herself Angline Ruler, from farther than our maps go. She sold me this."

I press the button. The white beam shoots across the floor of the Lamp Hall and lands on the far wall, a small hard circle, whiter than anything the great crystal makes.

Now she looks up.

She comes down from her chair, which she has not done for a collector in all my eleven years, and takes the thing from my hand. She turns it over. She unscrews the end, as I did, and finds, as I did, only a heavy little cylinder inside.

"Where is the mote?"

"There isn't one, my lady. She says it drinks *spark-stuff.*"

"Spark-stuff." The Lady says it the way you might say *toy.* She holds the light up and looks into it, and I watch her squint. "Oren. Everything that gives light gives it because something was cut down for it. Everything." She says it softly, the way you would recite a thing taught to children. "Nothing shines for free."

The beam flickers.

It's very slight, the first time. Then again. The circle on the far wall is dimmer than it was when I first switched it on in the shaft, three hours ago. I hadn't noticed until now, here, beside the great crystal, where there's nothing to hide it.

The Lady smiles. It isn't a pleasant smile.

"So it does drink," she says. "She just didn't tell you from what."

She hands it back to me, and I understand that I'm to keep it, and to carry it, and to bring back its owner.

"Find her," says the Lady of House Verdane. "And find out what it's drinking."

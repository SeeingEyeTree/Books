# Chapter 8: Runes

Four months after the turbine first turned, the vertical farm stands eight floors tall on its quarter hectare of licensed ground, with twenty-two more to go. The yeast floors went in first. The optimizer was very clear about that.

It prints a little over eleven thousand motes a day.

One in ten goes up the thousand and eight stairs to the Lamp Hall, as agreed. The Lady has stopped asking what I grow in my tall shed. She has started sending me wine. The rest goes into the vault: a windowless concrete room at the far corner of the site, lined with shelves of glowing motes in labeled trays, like a library where every book is a small fortune.

I don't spend it in the city. Not much of it. Eleven thousand motes a day, poured into a market where a family lives on eighty-four a week, would make every mote in Pinnacle worth less by the month, and the Houses would notice their money going soft long before they noticed why. A money printer is only useful if nobody can hear it running.

So I spend it on the one thing I've never once had enough of.

Curiosity.

For my entire career, every experiment I wanted to run needed a budget, and every budget needed a committee, and every committee needed eleven months. Now I have a vault full of energy that nobody approves but me. I stand in its doorway on the first morning and feel like a child left alone in a toy shop by a very foolish parent.

"What are you going to do with it?" Bethany asks.

"Everything," I say. "But first, I want to know what it's good for. And what it's good for, mostly, is *building.*"

---

The first test is force, at scale.

A two-tonne steel beam lies on the ground by the farm's ninth-floor frame. Normally, lifting it takes a crane we don't have yet. I put my palm flat on the steel with a mote in the other hand, and I push, the way the porters in Pinnacle push their crates up the outside of the towers. Contact is cheap. Distance is expensive.

The beam rises. Smoothly. Steadily. Ten meters, level with the frame, where Brask's crew guides it onto its bolts.

I check the mote. Eight-tenths of it is gone.

Lifting two tonnes by ten meters takes about two hundred kilojoules. The mote held a megajoule of heat. So force costs about four times what heat does for the same energy: a quarter efficient. Worse than heat, much better than light, and still better than a diesel crane on a cold morning.

The second test is heat, applied precisely.

I lay two steel plates edge to edge and try to weld them without an arc, pouring heat along the seam. The first attempt is a mess: a lump of slag and a hole I could put my thumb through. The second is a bead like a toddler's handwriting. By the ninth, the seam is thin, even, and strong enough that Brask's hammer bounces off it.

"You're welding with your *mind,*" Bethany says.

"I'm welding with my *attention,*" I say. "The mind is just where I keep it."

The third test is the one I care most about. Information.

I stand on the bare ground where the farm's second tower will go, hold three motes, and ask the world a question: *how far down to solid rock?*

The answer arrives the way gravity did in the bamboo, months ago. Not a number, exactly. A feeling of depth. Of weight. I translate it into meters as best I can and write down *about fourteen.*

Then I spend a full afternoon drilling with the soil auger and running the seismometer to check.

Thirteen point six meters.

Three motes of magic, and I have a geotechnical survey that would take a week and a drill rig on Earth. I sit down on the auger case and laugh out loud.

---

But by the end of the week, I've found the limit, and it isn't the motes.

It's me.

Every beam I lift, every seam I weld, every question I ask the ground, needs a person holding a mote and *concentrating.* Magic spends the crystal's energy, but it spends my focus too. After forty beams, my head aches like I've done a week of exams. After sixty, the seams start to wander. I can't lift a hundred beams a day. I can't put a thousand welds in a tower. And I can't be in two places at once, which I have always regarded as a personal failing of physics.

The vault is full of energy. What it needs is something that can spend that energy *without me.*

"You need a machine," Bethany says, when I explain this, lying flat on the floor of the vault with a cold towel on my eyes.

"I need a machine that eats motes," I say. "And nobody here has ever built one."

Brask, sorting motes on the shelves behind us, stops.

*That isn't quite true,* he says.

---

Brask brings it the next morning, wrapped in a cloth, like a relic.

It's a disk of fired clay about the size of a saucer, heavy and old, with a pattern carved deep into its face: a spiral that breaks into three curved bars, each ending in a small cup-shaped hollow. The grooves are crisp and precise, cut by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and had a very long time to do it.

*A kettle rune,* Brask says, setting it on the bench. *My grandmother's.*

He puts a pot of water on top of it. Then he takes a mote from his pocket and sets it on the bench about a hand's width away.

Nothing happens for a few seconds. Then the water starts to steam.

Nobody is touching it. Nobody is concentrating. The rune just sits there, drinking from the mote beside it, heating the pot all by itself.

I stare at it the way I stared at his lodestone wheel.

"How long have you had these?"

*Runes? Forever. Longer than the Houses.* He shrugs. *Nobody uses them much.*

"*Why?*"

He counts the reasons on his fingers, patiently, like a teacher who has been asked this by every student he's ever had.

*One: a rune does one thing. This one heats. It will only ever heat. If you want it to push, you carve another. Two: it takes a master carver six weeks to cut one, and one slip ruins it. There are maybe twenty carvers left in Pinnacle, and they're all old. Three: a rune is a little worse than a person. Any cook can heat a pot with a mote and waste less of it than this does.* He lifts the pot off, and the steaming slows but doesn't stop. *And four. It never stops. It drinks from any mote in reach, an arm's length or so, until the mote is empty. There's no way to turn it off except to take the mote away.*

He says it like it's the worst thing about them. He's describing, without knowing it, a circuit with no switch.

"The shape is the program," I say.

*The what?*

"The shape. The grooves tell the energy what to do, the way wires tell current where to go. The mote is the power supply. The rune is a machine, Brask. The simplest machine in the world, and your people have had it forever, and you've been using it to make tea."

Brask looks at the clay disk for a long moment.

*It's very good tea,* he says, a little defensively.

---

I spend three days finding out what a rune actually is.

I trace the kettle pattern in chalk on a slate. Nothing. I draw it in ink. Nothing. I press it into soft wax with a copy of the grooves. The water warms, barely. I have Billy's drones scan the clay disk to a tenth of a millimeter and mill the pattern into a steel plate. It works, and almost as well as the clay.

"So it's not the material," Bethany says, watching me line up the plates. "It's the depth?"

"It's the *relief.* The three-dimensional shape of the surface." I run my finger along a groove. "The walls, the hollows, the curves. A flat drawing has no walls, so it does nothing. Soft wax slumps a little, so it barely works. Clay and steel hold their shape exactly, so they work best."

Which is the problem. A shape that holds exactly takes six weeks to carve, and if you're wrong, you start again. There are twenty carvers left in the city, and every new rune is a six-week experiment you can only run once.

I think about the growth chambers. A hundred experiments a day, never tired, never attached to an idea.

Then I think: *what if the shape could change?*

---

"Ferrofluid," I tell Bethany that night, drawing on the back of a delivery manifest. "Oil full of tiny iron particles. Put it over a magnet and it pulls up into spikes and ridges, and holds them as long as the field holds. Change the field, change the shape. In milliseconds."

I draw a shallow glass tray. Under it, a grid of tiny electromagnets, sixty-four by sixty-four, each one switched by a computer.

"Every magnet pulls the fluid up by a different amount. The computer draws the pattern. The fluid becomes the rune. And when you want a different rune—"

"—you just change the pattern," she says. "A programmable rune."

"A *screen,*" I say, "that displays magic."

Building it takes nine days. The magnet grid comes through the portal in pieces, like everything else, and Brask spends two of those days just watching the ferrofluid rise and fall under the magnets, completely silent, the way I watched his generator. It's the first time I've seen him without anything to say.

On the tenth day, I load the scan of his grandmother's kettle rune into the computer. The magnets hum. The ferrofluid shivers, then rises out of the glass in a shining black relief: spiral, three bars, three hollows. Exact to the tenth of a millimeter.

I put a pot of water on it. I set a mote a hand's width away.

We wait.

The water starts to steam.

Brask makes a small sound. Bethany grabs my arm. And I, very calmly, because I am always calm, reach over to the keyboard and turn the magnets off.

The ferrofluid slumps back into a flat black puddle.

The water stops warming.

I turn the magnets back on. The rune rises. The water warms. Off. It stops.

"Brask," I say. "Your grandmother's rune has a switch."

He sits down heavily on a crate. For a while, he just watches me turn it on and off, on and off, like the gate guard with the flashlight.

*Six weeks to carve,* he says finally. *And you drew it in a moment. And you can take it back.*

"I can take it back," I say. "And tomorrow, the computer is going to start drawing ones nobody's ever carved."

---

Within two weeks, the Rune Shed has twenty ferrofluid rigs in a row along one wall, each one wired to the same optimizer that runs the growth chambers. Each rig has a mote on a sliding holder beside it and a set of instruments around it: a thermometer in a water cell, a load cell to measure push, a lux meter, a small wheel on a bearing. The optimizer draws a shape, slides the mote in, measures whatever happens, slides the mote out, and draws the next one.

Three hundred runes an hour. More than every carver in the history of Pinnacle has cut.

Most of them do nothing. The ferrofluid rises into a random pattern, the mote slides in, and the instruments sit at zero. That's expected. Most random sequences of letters aren't words either.

But the ones that do something, the optimizer keeps. And it mutates them, a little at a time, and keeps the ones that do it better.

On the fourth day, it finds a kettle rune thirty percent more efficient than Brask's grandmother's. Better than any cook with a mote in her hand. For the first time in this world's history, a rune beats a person.

Brask takes this well. He sends a sketch of the new pattern to the last twenty carvers in Pinnacle with a note that says, as far as Billy can translate it, *You were all doing it wrong. So was I. Come and see.*

---

Bethany is the one who finds the grammar.

She spends a week with printouts of every rune that works, spread across the floor of the shed, sorting them into piles. It's how she used to read protein structures, she tells me. You look for the shapes that keep coming back.

"Here," she says finally, pointing with a pencil. "Every heat rune has a spiral. Every push rune has one of these long bars, and the push goes in the direction of the bar. And every single rune that works has at least one of these hollows." She taps a small cup-shaped dip. "The hollows are where it drinks. No hollow, no effect."

"Motifs," I say.

"Domains. Like in proteins. Each part does one job, and you can combine them." She looks up at me. "So you don't have to search blind. You can *design.*"

That night we design one.

Six push motifs, arranged around the edge of the rig in a ring, each bar angled a little off the tangent. A single shared hollow in the middle. We load it into the ferrofluid, set a small iron wheel on a bearing in the center, and slide the mote in.

The wheel turns.

Slowly at first. Then faster. Nobody's touching it. Nobody's concentrating. It just spins, drinking steadily from the mote beside it, humming on its bearing like it's been waiting to do this for a thousand years.

A motor. A machine that runs on motes.

I measure it, because I'll never stop measuring things. Twenty percent efficient, turning motes into rotation. Worse than an electric motor on Earth. Infinitely better than anything that has ever existed here. And ten times worse on Earth, because Earth taxes everything, so this is a machine for the Green.

But it's a machine. Put it on a winch, and it lifts beams all day without me. Put it on a pump, and it moves water up thirty floors. Put it on wheels, and—

"You're thinking about cars," Bethany says.

"I'm thinking about *everything,*" I say. "This is the steam engine, Bethany. This is the moment right before everything changes."

---

It changes three days later, at 2:14 in the morning, and not in the direction I intended.

The optimizer has been running unattended, searching for intake designs, trying to make the hollows drink more efficiently. At some point in the night, it finds one that doesn't drink more efficiently. It drinks *farther.*

Much farther.

I'm asleep in Billy's cabin when the alarms go. By the time I reach the Rune Shed, rig fourteen is glowing orange. The ferrofluid is boiling in its tray. The water cell has cracked. And the rune, a strange, sprawling pattern with one enormous hollow at its heart, is drinking.

Not from the mote beside it. That's long gone.

From the vault.

The vault is twenty-two meters away, behind two concrete walls. And on the vault's shelves, row by row, the motes are going dark. I can see it through the vault's little inspection window: tray after tray fading from glowing blue to cloudy glass, like lights going off in a building at night.

I hit the master switch. The magnets die. The ferrofluid slumps. The glow stops.

For a long moment, the only sound in the shed is the hiss of boiling oil cooling in its tray.

Bethany arrives, breathless, in pajamas and a lab coat. She looks at the scorched rig. Then at me. "How many?"

I go and count. It takes a while.

"Four thousand one hundred and sixty motes," I say when I come back. "Nearly nine hours of the farm's output. Turned into heat in about six minutes."

She sits down on a crate. "It could have burned the whole site down."

"It could have," I agree. I'm looking at the pattern on the optimizer's screen: the sprawling shape with its one huge hollow. "Bethany. It drank through two concrete walls from twenty-two meters away."

"I know. That's why it's a disaster."

"No. That's why it's a *discovery.*" I turn the screen toward her. "Every House in Pinnacle keeps its motes in a tower. Behind walls. Behind guards. They think distance is a lock." I tap the huge hollow. "This is a key."

She stares at me.

"Put this rune outside the Lamp Hall," I say softly, "and every mote in House Verdane would drain into it. And nobody would ever know why."

"You are *not*—"

"Of course not. They're my partners." I save the pattern, encrypt it, and delete it from every rig. "But history teaches us one reliable method of wealth accumulation, and I intend to know exactly how to do it before anyone else does."

The next morning, I move the vault two hundred meters away. Until I know what blocks the drinking, distance is the only shield I have. And I add a new line to the optimizer's rules, above everything else, in capital letters.

*NO INTAKE REACH ABOVE ONE METER WITHOUT HUMAN APPROVAL.*

The machine is a better researcher than I am. It's also a great deal less careful. I've known people like that. I shot one.

---

The problem with the land is that someone always owns it.

By the end of the month, I've spent more time in towers than in the Rune Shed. House Sable wants a charter for its lower quarters, and will give me husk-wood from its fields, but only if I build my second farm on Sable ground under a Sable license, at two in ten. House Ochre wants the same, at one in ten, and also wants to know why Sable was asked first. The Lady of Verdane has noticed that I'm climbing other people's stairs, and her wine has become noticeably less generous.

Every hectare needs stakes. Every stake needs a House. Every House wants a tithe, a seal, and a promise that I answer to it. And that's just Pinnacle. There are forty-one cities on the cartographer's map, and every one of them, Brask tells me, has two or three Houses of its own.

I do the math on the back of Oren's latest letter. Forty-one cities. Perhaps a hundred Houses. A charter with each, at a few weeks of stairs and wine apiece.

"Six years," I tell Bethany. "Of negotiating. Before I've built anything."

"You'd enjoy that, though," she says. "You love a monologue."

"I love *my* monologues. I do not love listening to a hundred other people's."

---

That night, I unroll the cartographer's map in the Rune Shed, and I look past the forty-one cities to the band of blue at the eastern edge.

The Brine.

"Brask," I say. "Who owns the sea?"

He looks up from a rig, puzzled. *No one. The Houses hold the Green. The Green is where the stalks grow.* He shrugs. *Nothing grows in the Brine that anyone wants to cut.*

"And harvest law?"

*Applies on House land.* He pauses, and I watch him realize what I'm asking. *Nobody has ever needed a license to fish.*

The Lady's own words come back to me. *Not on our land, not in our city.* I'd agreed to it without knowing what harvest meant. Now I know exactly what it means, and exactly where her law stops.

At the waterline.

---

I send three long-range drones east the next morning, with a relay drone every eighty kilometers to carry the signal back. Four hundred kilometers of green passes beneath them over the next two days: fields, roads, two small cities that aren't on the map, and then, abruptly, the edge of the world.

The bamboo stops at a line of pale cliffs. Beyond them, the Brine: gray-green, wide, restless, running to a horizon with no far shore in sight.

Billy's analysis comes back within the hour. Salinity close to Earth's oceans. Water temperature fourteen degrees at the surface. And life. A great deal of life. Kelp forests visible from the air as dark shapes swaying under the surface. Shoals of fish thick enough to show up on thermal imaging. A few scattered fishing villages along the coast, a dozen boats apiece, no towers, no crests, no stakes. Nobody's land.

And forty kilometers south of the nearest village, a deep, sheltered bay, its mouth narrow and guarded by cliffs on both sides, with a floor that drops away fast to eighty meters.

I look at it for a long time.

Kelp grows half a meter a day on Earth. The ocean is the largest living system on any world. And not one Houseman in the Green thinks it's worth anything, because it grows nothing they know how to cut.

"Harvest without licenses," I say. "Farms without land rights. Floating ones on the surface, where the kelp grows. Sealed ones on the seabed, where nobody can see."

"An *underwater* base," Bethany says.

"I planned it on the first day. Water is corrosive, the ocean is volatile, and no one builds surveillance under the sea. Billy's amphibious." I tap the bay. "It was always going to be the ocean. I just didn't know what I'd be growing."

She looks at the map. At the four hundred kilometers of green between the portal and the bay.

"How do you get everything there?"

"Trailers. A road, eventually. And machines that run on motes, pulling them." I glance at the little iron wheel, still spinning on its rune on the bench. "The Houses think land is power. They're about to find out that power is whatever you can't stop someone from growing."

I name the bay that night, on the map, in careful ink.

*Ruler's Reach.*

Every empire needs a harbor. Mine might as well have my name on it.

---

I've been on Earth so rarely these last months that it feels like visiting a country I used to live in. The air smells of exhaust. The lights are too bright and too cheap. Nobody is growing anything.

I come back on a Monday for a meeting with Janis's shipping agent, and at 9:05, Dr. Halvorsen asks me to come to her office.

There's a woman sitting in the second chair. Forties, gray suit, short hair, a notebook on her knee. She stands when I come in and holds out a hand, and her grip is the grip of someone who is noticing how firm my grip is.

"Detective Inspector Nkemelu," she says. "I'm looking into the whereabouts of Dr. Alphonseo."

Halvorsen's dying plant is still dying on the windowsill. I focus on it for half a second while my mind runs through every possibility, ranks them, and settles.

"Helsinki," I say. "The fjords."

"Finland doesn't really have fjords," says Nkemelu.

I'm starting to dislike people who know that.

She opens her notebook. "His sister filed a report three weeks ago. She hasn't heard from him since the spring. So we asked the Finnish police." She turns a page. "There's no record of him entering Finland. No flight, no hotel, no conference registration. And the emails he sent about extending his trip, the ones the whole department received, came from an IP address inside this building."

The room is very quiet.

I sent that email from the lab. On the morning I shot him. Of course I did. I had a body on the floor, a portal to open, and a projector warming up. I didn't think anyone would check, because nobody ever questions Helsinki.

A detective does.

"He may have sent them before he left," I say. "Scheduled. He was very organized."

"Maybe." She writes something down. "His badge shows him entering the building on the Friday he disappeared. It doesn't show him leaving. And his car is still in the staff garage, level two. Five months of parking tickets." She looks up. "Were you working with him that day, Dr. Ruler?"

"All morning."

"And then?"

"And then he said he had a flight." I spread my hands. "I'm an engineer, Inspector. I watch machines, not people. People are very hard to monitor."

"I've found that too." She closes the notebook. "I'd like to see the lab."

I show her the lab. What else can I do? She walks its length slowly, past the benches and the projector and the knocked-through walls, and she stops at the internal window, looking at the covered pool.

"What's in there?"

"Thermal reservoir."

"What's it for?"

"Heat," I say.

She looks at me for a long moment. Then she looks at the pool cover. Then she writes something in her notebook, and I would give ten thousand motes to know what it is.

"I'll be back," she says at the door, "with a warrant for the building's security records. Every door, every camera, every badge. Please don't go anywhere, Dr. Ruler."

"I never go anywhere," I say. "I'm always here by six."

---

The email from Keith arrives at 3:40.

SUBJECT: *Audit notification — Portal Monitoring Suite, Sub-basement 4.*

It's short. Keith's emails are always short. They're like his questions: small, precise, and aimed at exactly the thing you hoped he wouldn't see. He'd like to discuss the forklift. And the electricity consumption on sub-basement four, which has risen by a factor of forty since the suite was installed. And the loading-bay logs, which record fourteen large trailer movements into sub-basement four and none out.

He'll be visiting Thursday. With Colonel Rourke.

I read it twice. Then I sit very still at the long bench and think, the way I haven't needed to think since the day I found the key.

The load-bearing walls are cracking. The detective believed Alphonseo went to Helsinki until she didn't. Keith believed my paperwork until the numbers stopped adding up. The only belief still standing is Rourke's. The anomaly is inert, and I answer to him.

Three days. I need to move the vault's reserve, hide the trailer records, and think of a reason a portal monitoring suite needs a forklift. Three days is enough. I've done more with less.

I'm writing the plan when my phone rings.

---

It's 9:50 at night. The caller ID says ROURKE.

I answer on the second ring, because answering on the first looks nervous.

"Colonel."

"Dr. Ruler." His voice is flat and strange. There's an echo behind it, the echo of a large concrete room. "Keith flagged your numbers this afternoon. I decided not to wait until Thursday. Your facilities man was very helpful. He had a key."

Gerald. Of course. Gerald and his clipboard.

"Where are you, Colonel?"

"Sub-basement four."

My phone buzzes. He's sending video.

I open it. The picture shakes, taken by someone walking. Concrete. Fluorescent lights. Two soldiers in the frame, rifles lowered but not slung. And there, at the center, the portal. Shimmering. Humming. Unmistakably not inert.

In front of it, halfway through, stands one of my trailers, loaded with pallets of LEDs, its back half in sub-basement four and its front half simply gone.

And beside it, hands up, lit by a soldier's flashlight, stands Bethany.

"She says she works for you," Rourke says. "She won't tell me where that goes."

Good. *Good,* Bethany.

"Dr. Ruler," says the colonel, very quietly. "You told me it was inert."

I look at the video for a moment longer. At Bethany's face, very pale in the white light. At the trailer, half here and half there. At the shimmer of the portal, indifferent as ever. Then I stand up, take my coat from the back of the chair, and feel the familiar weight in its pockets: the pistol, the C4, the notebook, and one small tray of motes I brought back to test the Earth tax again.

"Colonel," I say. "I'm on my way. Don't touch anything."

"Why not?"

"Because," I say, "I'm about to monologue."
